Gaston Leroux frames his tale as an investigation to prove that the Opera Ghost truly existed, stitching together archival traces, testimonies, and the crucial papers of a mysterious Persian who knew the specter by name. In Paris’s new, glittering Palais Garnier, whispers thicken around Box Five, which the ghost claims as his own while sending imperious letters that demand a private stipend and artistic obedience. The new managers, Moncharmin and Richard, mock the superstition until a scene-shifter is found hanged, the “magic envelope” tricks away twenty thousand francs, and a prima donna croaks like a toad at the height of her aria. In the same shimmering house, the young Swedish soprano Christine Daaé surges from chorus role to sudden stardom, attributing her miraculous training to an “Angel of Music” sent by her late father. Raoul, Vicomte de Chagny—her childhood friend—reappears and falls in love, only to overhear a man’s voice in Christine’s dressing room staking a terrible claim on her soul. The investigation’s narrator insists that behind the death’s-head mask is a living man named Erik, master ventriloquist, architect, and conjurer who has made the Opera his labyrinthine kingdom.

Christine’s triumph draws the envy of Carlotta and scrutiny from the crowd, while the ghost’s demands escalate through red-ink notes insisting on Box Five and on Christine’s casting. In the managers’ office, the stipend vanishes from a sealed envelope via a hidden trap, and the two incredulous directors are made ridiculous in front of their subscribers. When Carlotta defies the ghost, her voice breaks into a grotesque croak and the chandelier crashes down upon the house, killing a new box-keeper and transfiguring rumor into mortal fear. Christine disappears from the stage through a mirror that swings like a door and descends to a house on a subterranean lake, where she learns that her “Angel” is a monstrous genius who calls himself Erik. At Perros, Raoul has heard a violin sing over a grave at midnight; at the Opera ball, he glimpses Red Death stalking the corridors, and in Christine’s dressing room he sees the mirror swallow her image whole. The Persian, recognizing the signs, guides Raoul into the catacombs, through secret panels and breathing walls, warning him to hold his hand high lest Erik’s dreaded Punjab lasso tighten around his throat.

In the cellars, they meet rat-catchers, lost corridors, and a torture chamber disguised as a kaleidoscope of mirrors, where jungle heat and desert mirages are conjured by ingenious mechanics. The chamber’s illusions break the wits of intruders, while a nearby powder-magazine lies ready for a catastrophic blast; above, Christine pleads in a Louis-Philippe parlor faced with two ebony caskets—scorpion and grasshopper—whose turning will decide life or death. Erik’s ultimatum is stark: a wedding mass if she consents to be his living bride, a requiem mass for the Opera and all within if she refuses. As Christine hesitates, Raoul and the Persian spin helplessly through furnace heat and rising black water when she turns the scorpion and floods the cellars to soak the powder. Erik chains Raoul in a Communist dungeon and returns to demand his price, but Christine, moved by his wretched loneliness, promises to marry him if he will spare the others. A kiss on his deathly brow breaks the spell of vengeance, and the Phantom, who has never been loved, weeps like a child.

Count Philippe, Raoul’s brother, has already fallen on the lake shore, another casualty of the Opera’s haunted geography and jealous pursuit. Yet Erik honors Christine’s entreaty, frees Raoul, and lets the lovers flee by train to a distant life under northern skies. He returns only once more to the Persian, confessing through tears that he has known a moment of pure happiness and that he will soon die “of love,” sending the daroga Christine’s ring and promised relics. The Persian, who once saved Erik’s life in Persia, relates the Phantom’s genesis: a child abhorred for his face, a prodigy in fairs, a master of traps and invisible palaces at Mazenderan, then an engineer of secret chambers for sultans before vanishing beneath the Paris stage. Leroux’s narrator verifies columns that sound hollow, a managers’ floor with a pickpocketing trap, the mirror-door, and the secret passage to the powder-barrels, and finally witnesses the unearthing of a skeleton wearing a plain gold ring. The Opera Ghost, buried where the “Angel of Music” once cradled Christine by the lake, is revealed not as a specter but as a man whose genius, ugliness, and hunger for human warmth composed a tragedy.

Around this tragic triangle turns a comedy of bureaucrats and a carnival of superstition, yet always the themes of love, pity, and the peril of mistaking masks for souls press through the gaslit spectacle. The ghost manipulates accounts, bodies, and voices, but Christine hears the broken heart behind the tricks and finds the courage to answer with mercy instead of loathing. Raoul’s chivalry falters in the darkness, while the Persian’s wary compassion supplies a lifeline of reason in a house designed to bewilder. Music itself—heavenly, infernal, and irresistible—becomes both prison and key, elevating Christine while binding Erik to a dream of being “some one like everybody else.” The Opera House, modern marvel and mechanical labyrinth, stands as both stage and accomplice, its trapdoors, lakes, and mirrored chambers reflecting the city’s opulence and its shadows. In the end, Leroux’s investigator closes his dossier not with triumph but with a prayer over Erik’s bones, asking grace for a man who wanted only to be loved and built a kingdom of illusions when the world denied him a face.

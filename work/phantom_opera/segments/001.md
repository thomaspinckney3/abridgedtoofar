IN WHICH THE AUTHOR OF THIS SINGULAR WORK INFORMS THE READER HOW HE
ACQUIRED THE CERTAINTY THAT THE OPERA GHOST REALLY EXISTED

The Opera ghost really existed. He was not, as was long believed, a
creature of the imagination of the artists, the superstition of the
managers, or a product of the absurd and impressionable brains of the
young ladies of the ballet, their mothers, the box-keepers, the
cloak-room attendants or the concierge. Yes, he existed in flesh and
blood, although he assumed the complete appearance of a real phantom;
that is to say, of a spectral shade.

When I ransacked the archives of the National Academy of Music, I was
at once struck by the surprising coincidences between the phenomena
ascribed to the “ghost” and the most extraordinary tragedy that ever
excited the Paris upper classes; and I conceived that the tragedy might
reasonably be explained by those phenomena. The events do not date more
than thirty years back; and, in the foyer of the ballet, one could
still find old men of the highest respectability who remembered, as
though yesterday, the mysterious conditions that attended the
kidnapping of Christine Daae, the disappearance of the Vicomte de
Chagny and the death of his elder brother, Count Philippe, whose body
was found on the bank of the lake that exists in the lower cellars of
the Opera on the Rue-Scribe side. But none had yet thought to connect
the more or less legendary figure of the Opera ghost with that terrible
story.

The truth was slow to enter my mind, my inquiry constantly complicated
by events which, at first sight, might be looked upon as superhuman; I
was more than once within an ace of abandoning a task that seemed a
hopeless pursuit of a vain image. At last came the proof that my
presentiments had not deceived me.

On that day, I had spent long hours over THE MEMOIRS OF A MANAGER, the
light and frivolous work of the too-skeptical Moncharmin, who, during
his term at the Opera, understood nothing of the ghost and made all the
fun he could of it, at the very moment when he became the first victim
of the curious financial operation that went on inside the “magic
envelope.” I had just left the library in despair, when I met the
delightful acting-manager, who introduced me to a lively and
well-groomed little old man: the examining magistrate in the famous
Chagny case, M. Faure, back from fifteen years in Canada, his first act
on returning being to ask for a free seat at the Opera.

We spent a good part of the evening together. He told me the whole
Chagny case as he had understood it, and, for lack of contrary
evidence, had been bound to conclude in favor of the viscount’s
madness and the elder brother’s accidental death. He laughed when I
spoke of the ghost. He had been told of curious manifestations in some
mysterious corner of the Opera and knew the story of the envelope; but
he had never seen in it anything worthy of his attention as magistrate
and had contented himself with hearing a witness who declared that he
had often met the ghost: the man whom all Paris called “the Persian,”
well-known to every subscriber. The magistrate took him for a visionary.

I was immensely interested by this story of the Persian. I discovered
him in his little flat in the Rue de Rivoli, where he had lived ever
since and where he died five months after my visit. He told me, with
child-like candor, all that he knew and handed me proofs of the ghost’s
existence—including the strange correspondence of Christine Daae—to do
with as I pleased. I compared some of Christine’s writing found outside
the famous bundle and my doubts were removed. I went into the Persian’s
past and found an upright man, incapable of inventing a tale to defeat
justice. This, moreover, was the opinion of the more serious persons
mixed up at one time or another in the Chagny case, friends of the
family, to whom I showed my documents and set forth my inferences.

In this connection, I print a few lines received from General D----:

SIR:

I can not urge you too strongly to publish the results of your inquiry.
A few weeks before the disappearance of that great singer, Christine
Daae, and the tragedy which threw the whole of the Faubourg
Saint-Germain into mourning, there was much talk, in the foyer of the
ballet, of the “ghost;” and it ceased only in consequence of the later
affair that so greatly excited us. If it be possible—as, after hearing
you, I believe—to explain the tragedy through the ghost, then I beg
you, sir, to talk to us about the ghost again. Mysterious though he may
appear, he will always be more easily explained than the dismal story
in which malevolent people have tried to picture two brothers killing
each other who had worshiped each other all their lives.

Lastly, with my bundle of papers, I once more went over the ghost’s
vast domain, the huge building which he had made his kingdom. All that
my eyes saw, all that my mind perceived, corroborated the Persian’s
documents; and a wonderful discovery crowned my labors. Later, when
digging in the substructure of the Opera, before burying the
phonographic records of the artist’s voice, the workmen laid bare a
corpse. I was able at once to prove that this corpse was that of the
Opera ghost. I made the acting-manager put the proof to the test with
his own hand; and it is now a matter of supreme indifference to me if
the papers pretend that the body was that of a victim of the Commune.
The wretches massacred, under the Commune, in the cellars of the Opera,
were not buried on this side; their skeletons can be found near that
immense crypt which was stocked during the siege with all sorts of
provisions. I came upon this track while seeking the remains of the
ghost, which I should never have discovered but for the unheard-of
chance described above.

We will return to the corpse and what ought to be done with it. For the
present, I must thank M. Mifroid, commissary of police called in after
Christine Daae’s disappearance; M. Remy, the late secretary; M.
Mercier, the late acting-manager; M. Gabriel, the late chorus-master;
and, more particularly, Mme. la Baronne de Castelot-Barbezac, once the
“little Meg” of the story, eldest daughter of worthy Mme. Giry, now
deceased, who had charge of the ghost’s private box. Thanks to them, I
shall be able to reproduce those hours of sheer love and terror in
their smallest details. And I owe gratitude to the present management
of the Opera, to M. Messager in particular, together with M. Gabion,
the acting-manager, and that most amiable of men, the architect
intrusted with the preservation of the building, who did not hesitate
to lend me the works of Charles Garnier; lastly, to my friend and
former collaborator, M. J. Le Croze, who allowed me to dip into his
splendid theatrical library and borrow the rarest editions.

GASTON LEROUX.

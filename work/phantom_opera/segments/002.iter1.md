It was the evening on which MM. Debienne and Poligny, the managers of the Opera, were giving a last gala performance to mark their retirement. Suddenly the dressing-room of La Sorelli, one of the principal dancers, was invaded by half-a-dozen young ladies of the ballet, who had come up from the stage after “dancing” Polyeucte. They rushed in amid great confusion, some giving vent to forced and unnatural laughter, others to cries of terror. Sorelli, who wished to be alone for a moment to “run through” the speech which she was to make to the resigning managers, looked around angrily at the mad and tumultuous crowd. It was little Jammes—the girl with the tip-tilted nose, the forget-me-not eyes, the rose-red cheeks and the lily-white neck and shoulders—who gave the explanation in a trembling voice:

“It’s the ghost!” And she locked the door.

Sorelli’s dressing-room was fitted up with official, commonplace elegance. A pier-glass, a sofa, a dressing-table and a cupboard or two provided the necessary furniture. On the walls hung a few engravings, relics of the mother, who had known the glories of the old Opera in the Rue le Peletier; portraits of Vestris, Gardel, Dupont, Bigottini. But the room seemed a palace to the brats of the corps de ballet, who were lodged in common dressing-rooms where they spent their time singing, quarreling, smacking the dressers and hair-dressers and buying one another glasses of cassis, beer, or even rhum, until the call-boy’s bell rang.

Sorelli was very superstitious. She shuddered when she heard little Jammes speak of the ghost, called her a “silly little fool” and then, as she was the first to believe in ghosts in general, and the Opera ghost in particular, at once asked for details:

“Have you seen him?”

“As plainly as I see you now!” said little Jammes, whose legs were giving way beneath her, and she dropped with a moan into a chair.

The girls all began to talk at once. The ghost had appeared to them as a gentleman in dress-clothes, suddenly standing before them in the passage, as if he had come straight through the wall. For several months, nothing had been discussed at the Opera but this specter in dress-clothes who stalked about the building, from top to bottom, like a shadow, who spoke to nobody and vanished as soon as he was seen, making no noise in walking. People began by laughing and making fun of this specter dressed like a man of fashion or an undertaker; but the ghost legend soon swelled to enormous proportions among the corps de ballet. All the girls pretended to have met this supernatural being more or less often. And those who laughed the loudest were not the most at ease. When he did not show himself, he betrayed his presence by accidents, comic or serious, for which the superstition held him responsible. The dress-suit, they said, covered a skeleton; and, of course, it had a death’s head.

The idea of the skeleton came from Joseph Buquet, the chief scene-shifter, who had really seen the ghost on the little staircase by the footlights which leads to the cellars. He had seen him for a second—then the ghost fled—and he said to any one who cared to listen:

“He is extraordinarily thin and his dress-coat hangs on a skeleton frame. His eyes are so deep that you can hardly see the fixed pupils. You just see two big black holes, as in a dead man’s skull. His skin, which is stretched across his bones like a drumhead, is not white, but a nasty yellow. His nose is so little worth talking about that you can’t see it side-face; and THE ABSENCE of that nose is a horrible thing TO LOOK AT. All the hair he has is three or four long dark locks on his forehead and behind his ears.”

This chief scene-shifter was a serious, sober, steady man, very slow at imagining things. His words were received with interest and amazement. Sensible men said Buquet had been the victim of a joke; then came incidents so curious that the shrewdest began to feel uneasy. A fireman, Pampin, went to make a round of inspection in the cellars and reappeared on the stage pale, trembling, practically fainting in the arms of the proud mother of little Jammes. Why? Because he had seen coming toward him, AT THE LEVEL OF HIS HEAD, BUT WITHOUT A BODY ATTACHED TO IT, A HEAD OF FIRE! And a fireman is not afraid of fire.

Consternation seized the ballet. Leaders, front-row and back-row girls alike had now excuses for quickening their pace past dark corners. Sorelli herself, the next day, placed a horseshoe on the table before the stage-door-keeper’s box, which everyone entering otherwise than as a spectator must touch; this horse-shoe was not invented by me—any more than any other part of this story, alas!—and may still be seen when you enter through the Cour de l’Administration.

To return to the evening in question. An agonizing silence reigned in the room. They seemed to hear a rustling outside the door—no footsteps—light silk sliding over the panel—and then it stopped. Sorelli, trying to show more pluck than the others, went up and, in a quavering voice, asked:

“Who’s there?”

No answer. “Is there any one behind the door?” she cried loudly.

Meg Giry clutched her gauze skirt: “Whatever you do, don’t open the door!”

But Sorelli, armed with a dagger that never left her, turned the key and drew back the door. The passage was empty; a gas-flame, in its glass prison, cast a red and suspicious light without dispelling the surrounding darkness. She slammed the door again with a deep sigh.

“No,” she said, “there is no one there.”

“Still, we saw him!” Jammes whispered, edging back to Sorelli. “He must be prowling somewhere. I shan’t go back to dress. We had better all go down to the foyer together, for the ‘speech,’ and we will come up again together.”

The child reverently touched the little coral finger-ring she wore as a charm, while Sorelli stealthily made a St. Andrew’s cross with the tip of her pink right thumb-nail on the wooden ring she wore. “Come, children, pull yourselves together! I dare say no one has ever seen the ghost.”

“Yes, yes, we saw him!” cried the girls. “Just as when he appeared to Joseph Buquet!”

“And Gabriel saw him too!” said Jammes. “Only yesterday—in broad day-light—”

“Gabriel, the chorus-master?”

“Why, yes. He was in the stage-manager’s office. Suddenly the door opened and the Persian entered—you know the Persian has the evil eye—And you know how superstitious Gabriel is: when he meets the Persian, he just puts his hand in his pocket and touches his keys. Well, the moment the Persian appeared, Gabriel jumped to the cupboard to touch iron, tore a whole skirt of his overcoat on a nail, banged his forehead against a hat-peg, skinned his arm on the screen, tried to lean on the piano—the lid fell and crushed his fingers; he rushed out like a madman, slipped on the staircase and came down the whole first flight on his back. We picked him up, covered with bruises and blood; and then he thanked Providence and told us: he had seen the ghost behind the Persian, THE GHOST WITH THE DEATH’S HEAD just like Joseph Buquet’s description!”

Little Giry muttered that Joseph Buquet would do better to hold his tongue. “That’s mother’s opinion,” she added, lowering her voice, looking about her. “Mother says the ghost doesn’t like being talked about.”

Pressed and promised secrecy, Meg, burning to say all, stared at the door and began:

“Well, it’s because of the private box.”

“What private box?”

“The ghost’s box!”

“Has the ghost a box?”

“Not so loud! It’s Box Five, you know, the box on the grand tier, next to the stage-box, on the left.”

“Nonsense!”

“I tell you it is. Mother has charge of it. Orders have been given at the box-office that it must never be sold. No one has had it for over a month, except the ghost.”

“And does the ghost really come there?”

“Yes.”

“Then somebody does come?”

“Why, no! The ghost comes, but there is nobody there. That’s just it! The ghost is not seen. And he has no dress-coat and no head! All that talk about his death’s head and his head of fire is nonsense! You only hear him when he is in the box. Mother has never seen him, but she has heard him. Mother knows, because she gives him his program.”

There was a sound of hurried, heavy footsteps in the passage and a breathless voice cried:

“Cecile! Cecile! Are you there?”

Ma Jammes burst in and dropped groaning into a chair, her eyes rolling madly in her brick-dust colored face. “How awful! How awful!”

“What? What?”

“Joseph Buquet!”

“What about him?”

“Joseph Buquet is dead!”

Exclamations, outcries, frightened questions. “Yes, he was found hanging in the third-floor cellar!”

“It’s the ghost!” little Giry blurted—and at once clapped her hands to her mouth. Around her, the panic-stricken companions whispered:

“Yes—it must be the ghost!”

Sorelli turned very pale. “I shall never be able to recite my speech,” she said. Ma Jammes emptied a glass of liqueur and gave her opinion that the ghost must have something to do with it.

The truth is that no one ever knew how Joseph Buquet met his death. The verdict at the inquest was “natural suicide.” In his Memoirs of Manager, M. Moncharmin, who succeeded MM. Debienne and Poligny, describes the incident:

“A grievous accident spoiled the little party which MM. Debienne and Poligny gave to celebrate their retirement. I was in the manager’s office, when Mercier, the acting-manager, suddenly came darting in. He seemed half mad and told me that the body of a scene-shifter had been found hanging in the third cellar under the stage, between a farm-house and a scene from the Roi de Lahore. I shouted:

‘Come and cut him down!’

“By the time I had rushed down the staircase and the Jacob’s ladder, the man was no longer hanging from his rope!”

M. Moncharmin found a very simple explanation: “It was just after the ballet; and leaders and dancing-girls lost no time in taking their precautions against the evil eye.” There you are! Picture the corps de ballet scuttling down the Jacob’s ladder and dividing the suicide’s rope among themselves in less time than it takes to write! When I think of the exact spot where the body was discovered—the third cellar underneath the stage!—imagine that SOMEBODY must have been interested in seeing that the rope disappeared after it had effected its purpose; and time will show if I am wrong.

The horrid news soon spread all over the Opera, where Buquet was popular. The dressing-rooms emptied and the ballet-girls, crowding round Sorelli like timid sheep around their shepherdess, trotted through the ill-lit passages and staircases, making for the foyer as fast as their little pink legs could carry them.

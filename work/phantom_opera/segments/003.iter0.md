The New Margarita

On the first landing, Sorelli ran against the Comte de Chagny, who was coming up-stairs, unusually excited.

"I was just going to you," he said, taking off his hat. "Oh, Sorelli, what an evening! And Christine Daae: what a triumph!"

"Impossible!" said Meg Giry. "Six months ago, she used to sing like a CROCK! But do let us get by, my dear count," continues the brat, with a saucy curtsey. "We are going to inquire after a poor man who was found hanging by the neck."

The acting-manager, fussing past, stopped short:

"What!" he exclaimed roughly. "Have you girls heard already? Well, please forget about it for to-night—and above all don't let M. Debienne and M. Poligny hear; it would upset them too much on their last day."

They went on to the foyer of the ballet, which was already full. No gala had ever equalled this one. All the great composers of the day had taken the baton in turn—Gounod, Saint Saens, Massenet, Delibes—and, that evening, Christine Daae revealed her true self for the first time to the astonished and enthusiastic audience. She sang a few passages from Romeo and Juliet; but this was nothing to the superhuman notes she gave forth in the prison scene and the final trio in FAUST, sung in the place of La Carlotta, who was ill. No one had ever heard or seen anything like it. Daae revealed a new Margarita that night, a Margarita of a splendor, a radiance hitherto unsuspected. The whole house went mad, rising to its feet, shouting, cheering, clapping, while Christine sobbed and fainted in the arms of her fellow-singers and had to be carried to her dressing-room.

A few subscribers protested: why had so great a treasure been kept from them? Till then, Christine had played a good Siebel to Carlotta's rather too splendidly material Margarita; and it had needed Carlotta's incomprehensible absence for the little Daae, at a moment's warning, to show all that she could do! Did Debienne and Poligny know of her hidden genius? And, if they knew, why had they kept it hidden? And why had she kept it hidden? Oddly enough, she was not known to have a professor of singing; she had often said she meant to practise alone for the future. The whole thing was a mystery.

The Comte de Chagny, standing in his box, applauded loudly. Philippe Georges Marie Comte de Chagny, forty-one, head of one of the oldest families in France, was a great aristocrat, exquisitely polite to women, a little haughty to men. On his father's death, he had taken charge of the vast estates; his two sisters and his brother, Raoul, left themselves in his hands. Raoul, born twenty years later of the Comtesse de Chagny, née de Moerogis de La Martyniere, who died at his birth, had been twelve when the old count died. Brought up by his sisters and an old aunt at Brest, he had taken a taste for the sea, entered the Borda, finished with honors, made his trip round the world, and, thanks to influence, had just been appointed to the Requin, bound for the Arctic Circle in search of the D'Artoi's expedition. He was enjoying a long furlough. Shy—one might have said innocent—petted by women, he had retained a candid charm; a little over twenty-one, he looked eighteen, with a small fair mustache, beautiful blue eyes and a complexion like a girl's.

Philippe spoiled him. Proud of the lad and of the naval tradition of their house, he took advantage of the leave to show him Paris, even the foyer of the ballet. The count was said to be "on terms" with Sorelli; but what crime for a bachelor of his rank to pass an hour after dinner with a dancer who had the finest eyes that ever were seen? Lastly, Philippe would perhaps not have taken his brother behind the scenes if Raoul had not been the first to ask him, with a gentle obstinacy which the count remembered later.

That evening, after applauding Daae, Philippe turned and saw Raoul quite pale.

"Don't you see," said Raoul, "that the woman's fainting?"

"You look like fainting yourself," said the count. "What's the matter?"

Raoul recovered. "Let's go and see," he said; "she never sang like that before."

Soon they were at the door from the house to the stage, among subscribers slowly making their way. Raoul tore his gloves without knowing what he was doing; Philippe, kind-hearted, did not laugh, and now understood why his brother had been absent-minded and turned every talk to the Opera. They pushed through the mêlée of gentlemen, scene-shifters, supers and chorus-girls; Raoul led, his face set with passion; Philippe followed, smiling. At the back, a troop of ballet-girls blocked the passage; jests flew from little painted lips; Raoul did not reply; at last he passed and dived into the semi-darkness of a corridor ringing with the name of "Daae! Daae!"

The count was surprised that Raoul knew the way. He had never taken him to Christine's; Raoul must have gone alone while Philippe talked with Sorelli, who sometimes asked him to wait till it was her time to go on and handed him the little gaiters in which she ran down to preserve the spotlessness of her satin shoes and flesh-colored tights. Sorelli had an excuse; she had lost her mother.

Postponing his usual visit, the count followed his brother toward Daae's dressing-room. It had never been so crammed; the whole house was excited by her success and by her fainting fit. The doctor had just arrived when Raoul entered at his heels. Christine received the first aid of the one while opening her eyes in the arms of the other. The count and many more remained crowding in the doorway.

"Don't you think, Doctor, that those gentlemen had better clear the room?" asked Raoul coolly. "There's no breathing here."

"You're quite right," said the doctor, who sent every one away, except Raoul and the maid. Even the joint managers, Debienne and Poligny, thrust into the passage among the dandies, submitted. The Comte de Chagny, standing outside, laughed:

"Oh, the rogue, the rogue!" And he added, under his breath: "Those youngsters with their school-girl airs! So he's a Chagny after all!"

He turned to go to Sorelli, but met her on the way, with her little troop of trembling girls.

Meanwhile, Christine uttered a deep sigh, which was answered by a groan. She turned her head, saw Raoul and started; she smiled at the doctor, looked at her maid, then at Raoul again.

"Monsieur," she said, in a voice not much above a whisper, "who are you?"

"Mademoiselle," replied the young man, kneeling on one knee and pressing a fervent kiss on the diva's hand, "I AM THE LITTLE BOY WHO WENT INTO THE SEA TO RESCUE YOUR SCARF."

Christine looked at the doctor and the maid; and all three began to laugh. Raoul turned very red and stood up.

"Mademoiselle," he said, "since you are pleased not to recognize me, I should like to say something to you in private, something very important."

"When I am better, do you mind?" And her voice shook. "You have been very good."

"Yes, you must go," said the doctor, with his pleasantest smile. "Leave me to attend to mademoiselle."

"I am not ill now," said Christine suddenly, with strange and unexpected energy. She rose and passed her hand over her eyelids. "Thank you, Doctor. I should like to be alone. Please go away, all of you. Leave me. I feel very restless this evening."

Perceiving her agitation, the doctor did not thwart her. Outside, he said to Raoul:

"She is not herself to-night. She is usually so gentle."

Then he went. All that part of the theater was now deserted; the farewell ceremony was no doubt taking place in the foyer of the ballet. Raoul thought Daae might go to it and waited in the silent solitude, hiding in the shadow of a doorway. A terrible pain was at his heart; he wished to speak without delay.

Suddenly the dressing-room door opened; the maid came out alone, with bundles. She laughed and said her mistress was quite well, but wished to be left alone. She passed on. One idea filled Raoul's burning brain: of course, Daae wished to be left alone FOR HIM! Had he not told her that he wanted to speak to her privately?

Hardly breathing, he went to the door, ear to the panel, hand lifted to knock—then dropped it. He had heard A MAN'S VOICE within, curiously masterful:

"Christine, you must love me!"

And Christine's voice, infinitely sad and trembling, as though accompanied by tears, replied:

"How can you talk like that? WHEN I SING ONLY FOR YOU!"

Raoul leaned against the panel to ease his pain. His heart, which had seemed gone for ever, returned and throbbed so loudly that the whole passage echoed; surely, if it continued, they would hear, open the door, and the young man would be turned away in disgrace. What a position for a Chagny! To be caught listening behind a door! He took his heart in his two hands to make it stop.

The man's grave voice again: "Are you very tired?"

"Oh, to-night I gave you my soul and I am dead!" Christine replied.

"Your soul is a beautiful thing, child," said the grave voice, "and I thank you. No emperor ever received so fair a gift. THE ANGELS WEPT TONIGHT."

Raoul heard nothing after that. He did not go away, but, fearing to be caught, returned to his dark corner, determined to wait for the man to leave. At one and the same time, he had learned what love meant, and hatred. He knew that he loved; he wished to know whom he hated. To his astonishment, the door opened and Christine Daae appeared, wrapped in furs, face hidden by a lace veil, alone. She closed the door behind her, but did not lock it. She passed him. He did not even follow her with his eyes, for his eyes were fixed on the door, which did not open again.

When the passage was empty, he crossed, opened, went in, and shut the door. Absolute darkness: the gas had been turned out.

"There is some one here!" said Raoul, with his back against the closed door, in a quivering voice. "What are you hiding for?"

Darkness and silence; only his own breathing. "You shan't leave this until I let you!" he exclaimed. "If you don't answer, you are a coward! But I'll expose you!" He struck a match; the blaze lit the room. There was no one. Turning the key, he lit the gas-jets, went into the dressing-closet, opened cupboards, hunted, felt the walls with his moist hands. Nothing!

"Look here!" he said, aloud. "Am I going mad?"

He stood ten minutes, listening to the gas flaring in the silence. Lover though he was, he did not even think of stealing a ribbon with her perfume. He went out, not knowing what he did nor where he went. An icy draft struck his face: he found himself at the bottom of a staircase, down which a procession of workmen were carrying a sort of stretcher covered with a white sheet.

"Which is the way out, please?" he asked one of the men.

"Straight in front of you, the door is open. But let us pass."

Pointing to the stretcher, he asked mechanically: "What's that?"

"'That' is Joseph Buquet, who was found in the third cellar, hanging between a farm-house and a scene from the ROI DE LAHORE."

He took off his hat, fell back to make room for the procession and went out.

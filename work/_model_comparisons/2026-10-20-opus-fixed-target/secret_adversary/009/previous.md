TAKEN aback though he was by the man's words, Tommy did not hesitate. If audacity had successfully carried him so far, it was to be hoped it would carry him yet farther. He quietly passed into the house and mounted the ramshackle staircase. Everything in the house was filthy beyond words, the grimy paper hanging in loose festoons, a grey mass of cobweb in every angle. By the bend of the staircase he had heard the man below disappear into a back room. Clearly no suspicion attached to him as yet.

At the top of the stairs Tommy halted to consider his next move. From the nearest door on the left of a narrow passage came a low murmur of voices. It was this room which he had been directed to enter. But what held his glance fascinated was a small recess immediately on his right, half concealed by a torn velvet curtain. It was directly opposite the left-handed door and, owing to its angle, it also commanded a good view of the upper part of the staircase. As a hiding-place it was ideal. He thought things over in his usual slow and steady way, deciding that the mention of “Mr. Brown” was not a request for an individual, but in all probability a password used by the gang. His lucky use of it had gained him admission. But in that room perhaps a further password would be required, or, at any rate, some proof of identity. To enter it was a colossal risk.

A repetition of the signal knock sounded on the door below, and Tommy, his mind made up, slipped quickly into the recess, and drew the curtain across so that it shielded him completely. There were several rents and slits in the ancient material which afforded him a good view.

The man who came up the staircase with a furtive, soft-footed tread was obviously of the very dregs of society. The low beetling brows, the criminal jaw, the bestiality of the whole countenance marked a type that Scotland Yard would have recognized at a glance.

He stopped at the door opposite, and gave the signal knock. A voice inside called out something, and the man passed in, affording Tommy a momentary glimpse of four or five people seated round a long table. At its head, with papers in front of him, sat a tall man with close-cropped hair and a short, pointed, naval-looking beard. With a correct, but curiously precise enunciation, he asked:

“Your number, comrade?”

“Fourteen, gov’nor,” replied the other hoarsely.

“Correct.”

The door shut again.

“If that isn’t a Hun, I’m a Dutchman!” said Tommy to himself. “Lucky I didn’t roll in. I’d have given the wrong number, and there would have been the deuce to pay.”

The next visitor Tommy recognized as an Irish Sinn Feiner. Certainly Mr. Brown’s organization was a far-reaching concern. Truly a strange and sinister gathering! Who was this man who held in his finger these curiously variegated links of an unknown chain?

The procedure was exactly the same. The signal knock, the demand for a number, and the reply “Correct.”

Two knocks followed in quick succession. The first man Tommy put down as a city clerk: quiet, intelligent-looking, rather shabbily dressed. The second was of the working classes, and his face was vaguely familiar to the young man.

Three minutes later came another, a man of commanding appearance, exquisitely dressed, and evidently well born. His face, again, was not unknown to the watcher, though he could not for the moment put a name to it.

After a long wait Tommy was just cautiously creeping out from his hiding-place, when another knock sent him scuttling back to cover. This last-comer came up the stairs so quietly that he was almost abreast of Tommy before the young man had realized his presence.

He was a small man, very pale, with a gentle almost womanish air. The angle of the cheek-bones hinted at his Slavonic ancestry. As he passed the recess, he turned his head slowly. The strange light eyes seemed to burn through the curtain; Tommy could hardly believe that the man did not know he was there and in spite of himself he shivered. The creature reminded him of a venomous snake.

The new-comer knocked as all had done, but his reception was very different. The bearded man rose to his feet, and all the others followed suit. The German came forward and shook hands. His heels clicked together.

“We are honoured,” he said. “We are greatly honoured.”

The other answered in a low voice that had a kind of hiss in it:

“There were difficulties. But one meeting is essential--to define my policy. I can do nothing without--Mr. Brown. He is here?”

The German replied with slight hesitation:

“We have received a message. It is impossible for him to be present in person.” He stopped, giving a curious impression of having left the sentence unfinished.

A very slow smile overspread the face of the other. He looked round at a circle of uneasy faces.

“Ah! I understand. He works in the dark and trusts no one. But, all the same, it is possible that he is among us now....” He looked round him again, and again that expression of fear swept over the group. Each man seemed eyeing his neighbour doubtfully.

“So be it. Let us proceed.”

The German indicated the place at the head of the table. The Russian demurred, but the other insisted.

“It is the only possible place,” he said, “for--Number One. Perhaps Number Fourteen will shut the door?”

In another moment Tommy was once more confronting bare wooden panels, and the voices within had sunk to a mere undistinguishable murmur. He felt that, by hook or by crook, he must hear more.

The passage was deserted. Tommy removed his shoes, then, leaving them behind the curtain, he walked gingerly out on his stockinged feet and laid his ear to the crack of the door. He could distinguish only a chance word here and there.

Very slowly, a fraction of an inch at a time, holding his breath, he turned the handle, and pressed. The door did not budge. Finally, in desperation, he pushed with all his might, and at last the truth dawned upon him. It was locked or bolted on the inside.

“Well, I’m damned!” he said. “What a dirty trick!”

With the same infinite pains he restored the handle to its original position. There was a certain bulldog tenacity about Tommy that made him slow to admit defeat. As one plan had failed, he must hunt about for another.

A little farther along the passage on the left was a second door. It yielded, and he slipped inside. The room, untenanted, was furnished as a bedroom, and the dirt was, if anything, more abundant.

But what interested Tommy was the thing he had hoped to find, a communicating door between the two rooms. The bolt was very rusty, and had clearly not been used for some time. By gently wriggling it to and fro, Tommy managed to draw it back without making too much noise; the handle yielded this time with complete success. The door swung open--a crack, but enough for Tommy to hear. A velvet _portière_ on the inside prevented him from seeing, but he was able to recognize the voices with a reasonable amount of accuracy.

The Sinn Feiner was speaking. His rich Irish voice was unmistakable:

“That’s all very well. But more money is essential. No money--no results!”

Another voice which Tommy rather thought was that of Boris replied:

“Will you guarantee that there _are_ results?”

“In a month from now I will guarantee you such a reign of terror in Ireland as shall shake the British Empire to its foundations.”

Then came the soft, sibilant accents of Number One:

“Good! You shall have the money. Boris, you will see to that.”

Boris asked a question:

“Via the Irish Americans, and Mr. Potter as usual?”

“I guess that’ll be all right!” said a new voice, with a transatlantic intonation, “though things are getting a mite difficult. There’s not the sympathy there was.”

“The chief difficulty is the landing of the ammunition,” said the Sinn Feiner. “The money is conveyed in easily enough.”

“That is settled, then,” said the sibilant tones. “Now, in the matter of the loan to an English newspaper, you have arranged the details satisfactorily, Boris?”

“I think so,” Boris replied.

“That is good. An official denial from Moscow will be forthcoming if necessary.”

Then the clear voice of the German broke the silence:

“I am directed by--Mr. Brown, to place the summaries of the reports from the different unions before you. That of the miners is most satisfactory. We must hold back the railways.”

For a long time there was silence, broken only by the rustle of papers. Then Tommy heard the light tap-tap of fingers, drumming on the table.

“And--the date, my friend?” said Number One.

“The 29th,” the German answered.

The Russian seemed to consider:

“That is rather soon.”

“I know,” said the German. “But it was settled by the principal Labour leaders, and we cannot seem to interfere too much. They must believe it to be entirely their own show.”

The Russian laughed softly, as though amused.

“Yes, yes,” he said. “They must have no inkling that we are using them for our own ends. They are honest men--and that is their value to us. It is curious--but you cannot make a revolution without honest men.” He paused, and then repeated, as though the phrase pleased him: “Every revolution has had its honest men. They are soon disposed of afterwards.”

There was a sinister note in his voice.

The German resumed:

“Clymes must go. He is too far-seeing. Number Fourteen will see to that.”

There was a hoarse murmur.

“That’s all right, gov’nor. Suppose I’m nabbed.”

“You will have the best legal talent to defend you,” replied the German quietly. “But in any case you will wear gloves fitted with the finger-prints of a notorious housebreaker.”

“Oh, I ain’t afraid, gov’nor. The streets is going to run with blood, so they say.” He spoke with a grim relish. “Dreams of it, sometimes, I does. And diamonds and pearls rolling about in the gutter for anyone to pick up!”

Tommy heard a chair shifted. Then Number One spoke:

“Then all is arranged. We are assured of success?”

“I--think so.” But the German spoke with less than his usual confidence.

Number One’s voice held suddenly a dangerous quality:

“What has gone wrong?”

“Nothing; but----” the German began.

“But what?”

“The Labour leaders. If they do not declare a general strike on the 29th----”

“Why should they not?”

“As you’ve said, they’re honest. They abuse the Government unceasingly. But, on the whole, public opinion swings to the side of the Government. They will not go against it.”

Again the Russian’s fingers drummed on the table.

“To the point, my friend. I was given to understand that there was a certain document in existence which assured success.”

“That is so. If that document were placed before the leaders, they would declare for the revolution without a moment’s hesitation. The Government would be broken finally and completely.”

“Then what more do you want?”

“The document itself,” said the German bluntly.

“Ah! It is not in your possession? But you know where it is?”

“No.”

“Does anyone know where it is?”

“One person--perhaps. And we are not sure of that even.”

“Who is this person?”

“A girl.”

Tommy held his breath.

“A girl?” The Russian’s voice rose contemptuously. “And you have not made her speak? In Russia we have ways of making a girl talk.”

“This case is different,” said the German sullenly.

“How--different?” He paused a moment, then went on: “Where is the girl now?”

“She is----” the German began.

But Tommy heard no more. A crashing blow descended on his head, and all was darkness.

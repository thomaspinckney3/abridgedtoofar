## II

About halfway between West Egg and New York the motor road hastily joins the railroad and runs beside it for a quarter of a mile, so as to shrink away from a certain desolate area of land. This is a valley of ashes—a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens; where ashes take the forms of houses and chimneys and rising smoke and, finally, with a transcendent effort, of ash-grey men, who move dimly and already crumbling through the powdery air.

But above the grey land and the spasms of bleak dust which drift endlessly over it, you perceive, after a moment, the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg. They are blue and gigantic—their retinas are one yard high. They look out of no face, but from enormous yellow spectacles which pass over a nonexistent nose. Some oculist set them there to fatten his practice in Queens, and then sank into eternal blindness, or forgot them and moved away. But his eyes, dimmed by many paintless days, brood on over the solemn dumping ground.

The valley is bounded by a small foul river, and waiting trains always halt there at least a minute. It was because of this that I first met Tom Buchanan’s mistress.

Though I was curious to see her, I had no desire to meet her—but I did. Going up to New York with Tom one afternoon, I was literally forced from the car when we stopped by the ash-heaps.

“We’re getting off,” he insisted. “I want you to meet my girl.”

I think he’d tanked up at luncheon; his determination bordered on violence. I followed him under Doctor Eckleburg’s persistent stare to a yellow brick block on the edge of the waste land. One shop was a garage—Repairs. George B. Wilson. Cars bought and sold.

The interior was unprosperous and bare, with a dust-covered wreck of a Ford crouching in a corner. The proprietor appeared, wiping his hands on waste: a blond, spiritless man, anaemic, and faintly handsome. A damp gleam of hope sprang into his eyes.

“Hello, Wilson, old man,” said Tom, slapping his shoulder. “How’s business?”

“I can’t complain,” answered Wilson unconvincingly. “When are you going to sell me that car?”

“Next week; I’ve got my man working on it now.”

“Works pretty slow, don’t he?”

“No, he doesn’t,” said Tom coldly. “And if you feel that way about it, maybe I’d better sell it somewhere else after all.”

“I don’t mean that,” explained Wilson quickly.

Then his wife blocked out the light from the office door. She was in the middle thirties, faintly stout, but carried her flesh sensuously. Her face contained no gleam of beauty, but there was an immediately perceptible vitality about her as if the nerves of her body were continually smouldering. Walking through her husband as if he were a ghost, she shook hands with Tom, looking him flush in the eye.

“Get some chairs, why don’t you, so somebody can sit down,” she told her husband.

Wilson hurried into the office, mingling with the cement colour of the walls. White ashen dust veiled everything—except his wife, who moved close to Tom.

“I want to see you,” said Tom intently. “Get on the next train.”

“All right.”

“I’ll meet you by the newsstand on the lower level.”

We waited down the road and out of sight. It was a few days before the Fourth of July.

“Terrible place, isn’t it,” said Tom, exchanging a frown with Doctor Eckleburg. “It does her good to get away.”

“Doesn’t her husband object?” I asked.

“Wilson? He thinks she goes to see her sister in New York. He’s so dumb he doesn’t know he’s alive.”

Mrs. Wilson sat discreetly in another car. Tom deferred that much to the sensibilities of East Eggers who might be on the train.

In New York she bought Town Tattle, a moving-picture magazine, cold cream, and perfume, then let four taxicabs drive away before selecting a lavender-coloured one. Immediately she tapped on the front glass.

“I want to get one of those dogs,” she said earnestly. “I want to get one for the apartment.”

We backed up to an old man resembling John D. Rockefeller, with puppies in a basket. She asked for a police dog; he produced something more like an Airedale, with a brown washrag of a back and startlingly white feet. For ten dollars it settled into her lap.

“Is it a boy or a girl?” she asked delicately.

“That dog? That dog’s a boy.”

“It’s a bitch,” said Tom decisively. “Here’s your money. Go and buy ten more dogs with it.”

On Fifth Avenue I tried to leave.

“No you don’t,” interposed Tom. “Myrtle’ll be hurt if you don’t come up to the apartment. Won’t you, Myrtle?”

“Come on,” she urged. “I’ll telephone my sister. She’s said to be very beautiful by people who ought to know.”

At 158th Street we stopped at one slice in a long white cake of apartment-houses. Mrs. Wilson gathered her purchases and went haughtily in.

The top-floor apartment had a small living-room, dining-room, bedroom, and bath. Tapestried furniture too large for it made one stumble continually over scenes of ladies swinging in the gardens of Versailles. An enlarged photograph, apparently a hen on a blurred rock, resolved itself into a bonnet and the countenance of Mrs. Wilson’s mother. Scandal magazines lay beside Simon Called Peter. An elevator boy fetched straw, milk, and dog biscuits; Tom brought whisky from a locked bureau.

I have been drunk just twice in my life, and the second time was that afternoon; so everything that happened has a dim, hazy cast over it, although until after eight o’clock the apartment was full of cheerful sun. I went out for cigarettes; returning, I found Tom and Mrs. Wilson gone and discreetly read Simon Called Peter, which made no sense to me.

As they reappeared, company arrived: Myrtle’s sister, a worldly girl with sticky red hair and jingling pottery bracelets, and a photographer from downstairs with his wife. He was pale and feminine, with a spot of shaving lather on his cheekbone; she was shrill, languid, handsome, and horrible.

Mrs. Wilson had changed into cream-coloured chiffon. With the influence of the dress her personality had also undergone a change. The vitality of the garage was converted into impressive hauteur. Her laughter, gestures, assertions became more violently affected; as she expanded the room grew smaller around her.

Complimented on her dress by the photographer’s wife, she raised an eyebrow.

“It’s just a crazy old thing,” she said. “I just slip it on sometimes when I don’t care what I look like.”

The photographer considered the light and the modelling of her features until Tom yawned.

“Get some more ice and mineral water, Myrtle, before everybody goes to sleep.”

“I told that boy about the ice.” Myrtle raised her eyebrows in despair at the shiftlessness of the lower orders. “These people! You have to keep after them all the time.”

She kissed the dog with ecstasy and swept into the kitchen, implying that a dozen chefs awaited her orders.

Her sister sat beside me and asked where I lived.

“I live at West Egg.”

“Really? I was down there at a party about a month ago. At a man named Gatsby’s. Do you know him?”

“I live next door to him.”

“Well, they say he’s a nephew or a cousin of Kaiser Wilhelm’s. That’s where all his money comes from.”

She was scared of him, she said; she would hate to have him get anything on her.

The photographer wanted an entry into Long Island society. Tom suggested that Myrtle give him an introduction to her husband, for studies of “George B. Wilson at the Gasoline Pump.”

“Neither of them can stand the person they’re married to,” Myrtle’s sister whispered to me. “If I was them I’d get a divorce and get married to each other right away.”

“Doesn’t she like Wilson either?”

Myrtle overheard. Her answer was violent and obscene. Her sister confided that Tom’s wife was a Catholic and would not divorce him. Daisy was not a Catholic, and I was a little shocked at the elaborateness of the lie.

Myrtle presently explained her marriage.

“I married him because I thought he was a gentleman,” she said finally. “I thought he knew something about breeding, but he wasn’t fit to lick my shoe.”

“You were crazy about him for a while,” said her sister.

“Crazy about him!” cried Myrtle incredulously. “Who said I was crazy about him? I never was any more crazy about him than I was about that man there.”

She pointed at me, and everyone looked accusingly. I tried to show that I expected no affection.

“The only crazy I was was when I married him. I knew right away I made a mistake. He borrowed somebody’s best suit to get married in, and never even told me about it, and the man came after it one day when he was out: ‘Oh, is that your suit?’ I said. ‘This is the first I ever heard about it.’ But I gave it to him and then I lay down and cried to beat the band all afternoon.”

They had lived above the garage eleven years, her sister told me, and Tom was Myrtle’s first sweetie.

The second bottle of whisky was in constant demand. Tom sent for sandwiches. I wanted to walk eastward toward the park through the soft twilight, but each attempt to go entangled me in some wild, strident argument which pulled me back, as if with ropes, into my chair. Yet high over the city our line of yellow windows must have contributed their share of human secrecy to the casual watcher in the darkening streets, and I saw him too, looking up and wondering. I was within and without, simultaneously enchanted and repelled by the inexhaustible variety of life.

Myrtle drew close, pouring the story of her first meeting with Tom over me.

“It was on the two little seats facing each other that are always the last ones left on the train. I was going up to New York to see my sister and spend the night. He had on a dress suit and patent leather shoes, and I couldn’t keep my eyes off him, but every time he looked at me I had to pretend to be looking at the advertisement over his head. When we came into the station he was next to me, and his white shirtfront pressed against my arm, and so I told him I’d have to call a policeman, but he knew I lied. I was so excited that when I got into a taxi with him I didn’t hardly know I wasn’t getting into a subway train. All I kept thinking about, over and over, was ‘You can’t live forever; you can’t live forever.’ ”

She turned to the photographer’s wife, promising her the dress when she was through with it. She must make a list: a massage, a wave, a collar for the dog, a little spring ashtray, a wreath with a black silk bow for mother’s grave.

It was nine o’clock—almost immediately afterward I looked at my watch and found it was ten. The photographer slept with his fists clenched in his lap, like a photograph of a man of action. I wiped the dried lather from his cheek with my handkerchief.

The dog sat on the table looking blindly through the smoke, groaning faintly. People disappeared, reappeared, made plans, lost one another and found one another a few feet away. Toward midnight Tom and Mrs. Wilson stood face to face disputing whether she had any right to mention Daisy’s name.

“Daisy! Daisy! Daisy!” shouted Mrs. Wilson. “I’ll say it whenever I want to! Daisy! Dai—”

Making a short deft movement, Tom Buchanan broke her nose with his open hand.

Then there were bloody towels on the bathroom floor, women’s voices scolding, and high over the confusion a long broken wail of pain. The photographer woke and started toward the door. He turned and stared at the women stumbling among the furniture with articles of aid, and the despairing figure on the couch, bleeding fluently, trying to spread Town Tattle over the tapestry scenes of Versailles. Then he went out. Taking my hat from the chandelier, I followed.

“Come to lunch some day,” he suggested, as we groaned down in the elevator.

“Where?”

“Anywhere.”

“Keep your hands off the lever,” snapped the elevator boy.

“I beg your pardon,” said the photographer with dignity, “I didn’t know I was touching it.”

“All right,” I agreed, “I’ll be glad to.”

… I was standing beside his bed and he was sitting up between the sheets, clad in his underwear, with a great portfolio in his hands.

“Beauty and the Beast … Loneliness … Old Grocery Horse … Brook’n Bridge …”

Then I was lying half asleep in the cold lower level of the Pennsylvania Station, staring at the morning Tribune, and waiting for the four o’clock train.

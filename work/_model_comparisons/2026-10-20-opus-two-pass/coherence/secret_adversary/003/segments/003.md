TUPPENCE turned sharply, but the man’s appearance did not bear out her first and most natural assumption. He said quickly:

“I can assure you I mean no disrespect.”

Although she disliked and distrusted him instinctively, she was inclined to acquit him of that motive. He was a big man, clean shaven, with a heavy jowl, and small, cunning, shifty eyes.

“Well, what is it?” she asked.

“I happened to overhear part of your conversation in Lyons’. I think I may be of some use to you.”

“You followed me here?”

“I took that liberty.”

He handed her a card with a bow: “Mr. Edward Whittington, Esthonia Glassware Co.,” with the address of a city office.

“If you will call upon me to-morrow morning at eleven o’clock, I will lay the details of my proposition before you.”

Tuppence made up her mind. “Very well. I’ll be there.”

He raised his hat with a flourish and walked away. Tuppence gave a curious movement of her shoulders, rather as a terrier shakes himself.

“The adventures have begun,” she murmured. “There’s something about you, Mr. Whittington, that I don’t like at all. But I’m not the least bit afraid of you. And as I’ve said before, and shall doubtless say again, little Tuppence can look after herself, thank you!”

That night, after a supper of buns, she dreamed that Mr. Whittington had set her to washing up a pile of Esthonia Glassware, which bore an unaccountable resemblance to hospital plates!

On the stroke of eleven Tuppence entered the small, rather dirty outer office of the Esthonia Glassware Co.

A middle-aged clerk got down from a high stool and came towards her inquiringly.

“I have an appointment with Mr. Whittington,” said Tuppence.

The clerk opened a door marked “Private” and stood aside to let her pass in.

Mr. Whittington, behind a large desk covered with papers, looked as sleek and shifty as ever.

“So you’ve turned up all right? Sit down, will you?”

Tuppence sat down, looking particularly small and demure.

“Now, my dear young lady, what should you say to £100 down, and all expenses paid?”

“And the nature of the work?” Tuppence demanded warily.

“Nominal--purely nominal. A pleasant trip, that is all.”

“Where to?”

“Paris.”

“Oh!” said Tuppence thoughtfully. Father would have a fit!

“Yes,” continued Whittington. “To put the clock back a few years--a very few, I am sure--and re-enter one of those charming _pensionnats de jeunes filles_ with which Paris abounds----”

“A _pensionnat?_” Tuppence interrupted.

“Exactly. Madame Colombier’s in the Avenue de Neuilly.”

Tuppence knew it, through American friends, as most select. She was more than ever puzzled.

“For how long?” she asked.

“Possibly three months. You would go as my ward, hold no communication with your friends, and keep absolute secrecy. By the way, you are English, are you not?”

“Yes.”

“Yet you speak with a slight American accent?”

“My great pal in hospital was a little American girl. I can soon get out of it.”

“On the contrary, it might be simpler for you to pass as an American. Then----”

“One moment, Mr. Whittington! You seem to be taking my consent for granted.”

Whittington looked surprised. “Surely you are not thinking of refusing? The terms are most liberal.”

“Exactly,” said Tuppence. “The terms are almost too liberal, Mr. Whittington. I cannot see how I can be worth that amount of money to you.”

“No?” said Whittington softly. “I could doubtless obtain some one else for much less. I am paying for intelligence to sustain the part, and discretion not to ask too many questions.”

She felt that Whittington had scored.

“There’s another thing. Where does Mr. Beresford come in?”

“Mr. Beresford?”

“My partner,” said Tuppence with dignity. “You saw us together yesterday.”

“Ah, yes. But I’m afraid we shan’t require his services.”

“Then it’s off!” Tuppence rose. “It’s both or neither. Sorry--but that’s how it is. Good morning, Mr. Whittington.”

“Wait a minute. Let us see if something can’t be managed. Sit down again, Miss----” He paused interrogatively.

Tuppence’s conscience gave her a passing twinge as she remembered the archdeacon. She seized hurriedly on the first name that came into her head.

“Jane Finn,” she said hastily; and then paused open-mouthed at the effect of those two simple words.

All the geniality had faded out of Whittington’s face. It was purple with rage, and behind it lurked a sort of incredulous dismay. He hissed savagely:

“So that’s your little game, is it?”

Tuppence, utterly taken aback, had not the faintest comprehension of his meaning, but felt it imperative to “keep her end up,” as she phrased it.

“Been playing with me, have you, like a cat and mouse? Knew all the time what I wanted you for, but kept up the comedy?” He eyed her keenly. “Who’s been blabbing? Rita?”

Tuppence shook her head, careful not to drag an unknown Rita into it.

“No,” she replied with perfect truth. “Rita knows nothing about me.”

“How much do you know?” he shot out.

“Very little indeed,” answered Tuppence, and was pleased to note that Whittington’s uneasiness was augmented instead of allayed.

“Anyway,” snarled Whittington, “you knew enough to come in here and plump out that name.”

“It might be my own name,” Tuppence pointed out.

“Or I might just have hit upon it by chance,” continued Tuppence, intoxicated with the success of truthfulness.

Whittington brought his fist down upon the desk.

“Quit fooling! How much do you know? And how much do you want?”

The last five words took Tuppence’s fancy mightily, especially after a meagre breakfast and a supper of buns.

“My dear Mr. Whittington,” she said, “you heard me say yesterday that I proposed to live by my wits. It seems I have now proved I have some wits to live by! I admit I know a certain name, but perhaps my knowledge ends there.”

“Yes--and perhaps it doesn’t,” snarled Whittington. “You know a great deal more than you’re willing to admit.”

“I shouldn’t like to contradict you, Mr. Whittington,” said Tuppence softly.

“So we come to the usual question--how much?”

To name a palpably impossible sum might awaken his suspicions. An idea flashed across her brain.

“Suppose we say a little something down, and a fuller discussion of the matter later?”

Whittington gave her an ugly glance.

“Blackmail, eh?”

Tuppence smiled sweetly.

“Oh no! Shall we say payment of services in advance?”

“You see,” explained Tuppence still sweetly, “I’m so very fond of money!”

“You’re about the limit, that’s what you are,” growled Whittington, with a sort of unwilling admiration. “Thought you were quite a meek little kid.”

“All the same,” continued Whittington, “some one’s been talking. You say it isn’t Rita. Was it----? Oh, come in.”

The clerk followed his discreet knock into the room, and laid a paper at his master’s elbow.

“Telephone message just come for you, sir.”

Whittington snatched it up and read it. A frown gathered on his brow.

“That’ll do, Brown. You can go.”

The clerk withdrew. Whittington turned to Tuppence.

“Come to-morrow at the same time. I’m busy now. Here’s fifty to go on with.”

He pushed some notes across the table, obviously impatient for her to go. Tuppence counted them in a businesslike manner and rose.

“Good morning, Mr. Whittington,” she said politely. “At least, au revoir, I should say.”

“Exactly. Au revoir!” Whittington looked almost genial again, a reversion that aroused in Tuppence a faint misgiving.

Tuppence sped lightly down the stairs in wild elation. “Let’s give Tommy a surprise!” she murmured, and hailed a taxi.

The cab drew up outside the tube station. Tommy, his eyes opening to their fullest extent, hurried forward to help her alight. She remarked in a slightly affected voice:

“Pay the thing, will you, old bean? I’ve got nothing smaller than a five-pound note!”

Taken aback though he was by the man’s words, Tommy did not hesitate. He quietly passed into the filthy house and mounted the ramshackle staircase. By the time he reached the bend, he had heard the man below disappear into a back room. Clearly no suspicion attached to him as yet.

At the top of the stairs Tommy halted. From the nearest door on the left of a narrow passage came a low murmur of voices; it was this room he had been directed to enter. But what held his glance was a small recess immediately on his right, half concealed by a torn velvet curtain, directly opposite the left-handed door and commanding a good view of the staircase. He thought things over in his usual slow and steady way, deciding that the mention of “Mr. Brown” was not a request for an individual, but in all probability a password used by the gang.

Suppose he were boldly to enter? Perhaps a further password would be required. The doorkeeper clearly did not know all the gang by sight, but it might be different upstairs. There was such a thing as trusting luck too far, and to enter would be mere foolhardiness.

A repetition of the signal knock sounded below, and Tommy slipped into the recess and drew the curtain across. Rents in the ancient material afforded him a good view.

The man who came up the staircase with a furtive, soft-footed tread had the low beetling brows and criminal jaw of a type Scotland Yard would have recognized at a glance. As he gave the signal knock and passed in at the door opposite, Tommy glimpsed a long table. At its head sat a tall man with a short, pointed, naval-looking beard. With a correct, but curiously precise enunciation, he asked:

“Your number, comrade?”

“Fourteen, gov’nor,” replied the other hoarsely.

“Correct.”

The door shut again.

“If that isn’t a Hun, I’m a Dutchman!” said Tommy to himself. “Lucky I didn’t roll in. I’d have given the wrong number. Hullo, here’s another knock.”

Tommy recognized this visitor as an Irish Sinn Feiner. Certainly Mr. Brown’s organization was a far-reaching concern. Who was this man who held in his finger these curiously variegated links of an unknown chain?

Two knocks followed. The first came from a quiet, shabbily dressed man whom Tommy put down as a city clerk. The second came from a working man whose face was vaguely familiar to the young man.

Three minutes later came a man of commanding appearance, exquisitely dressed, and evidently well born. His face, again, was not unknown to the watcher, though he could not put a name to it.

After a long wait, another knock sent Tommy, half out of hiding, scuttling back to cover.

This last-comer came up so quietly that he was almost abreast of Tommy before he was noticed. He was a small man, very pale, with a gentle almost womanish air. The angle of the cheek-bones hinted at his Slavonic ancestry. As he passed the recess, he turned his head slowly. The strange light eyes seemed to burn through the curtain, and in spite of himself Tommy shivered. The creature reminded him of a venomous snake.

This time the reception was very different. The bearded man rose, and all the others followed suit. The German came forward and shook hands, his heels clicking together.

“We are honoured,” he said. “We are greatly honoured.”

The other answered in a low, hissing voice:

“There were difficulties. But one meeting is essential--to define my policy. I can do nothing without--Mr. Brown. He is here?”

The German hesitated. “We have received a message. It is impossible for him to be present in person.” He stopped, as if leaving the sentence unfinished.

A very slow smile overspread the other’s face as he looked round at a circle of uneasy faces.

“Ah! I understand. I have read of his methods. He works in the dark and trusts no one. But, all the same, it is possible that he is among us now....” Each man seemed eyeing his neighbour doubtfully.

The Russian tapped his cheek. “So be it. Let us proceed.”

The German insisted that he take the head of the table.

“It is the only possible place,” he said, “for--Number One. Perhaps Number Fourteen will shut the door?”

Once more Tommy confronted bare wooden panels. By hook or by crook, he must hear more. Leaving his shoes behind the curtain, he walked gingerly out on his stockinged feet and knelt with his ear to the crack, but caught only a chance word. Very slowly, a fraction of an inch at a time, he turned the handle. Then he pressed, finally with all his might. But the door remained firm. It was locked or bolted on the inside.

“Well, I’m damned!” he said. “What a dirty trick!”

As his indignation cooled, he restored the handle with the same infinite pains. There was a certain bulldog tenacity about Tommy that made him slow to admit defeat.

A second door along the passage yielded, and he slipped into an untenanted bedroom. It held the thing he had hoped to find, a communicating door between the two rooms. By gently wriggling its rusty bolt, Tommy drew it back without much noise. Then he repeated his manœuvres with the handle, this time with complete success. The door swung open a mere crack. A velvet _portière_ prevented him from seeing, but he could recognize the voices with reasonable accuracy.

The Sinn Feiner was speaking. His rich Irish voice was unmistakable:

“That’s all very well. But more money is essential. No money--no results!”

Another voice, which Tommy rather thought was that of Boris, replied:

“Will you guarantee that there _are_ results?”

“In a month from now--sooner or later as you wish--I will guarantee you such a reign of terror in Ireland as shall shake the British Empire to its foundations.”

Then came the soft, sibilant accents of Number One:

“Good! You shall have the money. Boris, you will see to that.”

“The chief difficulty is the landing of the ammunition,” said the Sinn Feiner. “The money is conveyed in easily enough--thanks to our colleague here.”

Another voice, which Tommy fancied was the commanding man’s, said:

“Think of the feelings of Belfast if they could hear you!”

“That is settled, then,” said the sibilant tones. “Now, the loan to an English newspaper--you have arranged the details, Boris?”

“I think so.”

“That is good. An official denial from Moscow will be forthcoming if necessary.”

Then the German’s clear voice broke the silence:

“I am directed by--Mr. Brown, to place the summaries of the reports from the different unions before you.”

There was a long silence, then the light tap-tap of fingers drumming on the table.

“And--the date, my friend?” said Number One.

“The 29th,” the German answered.

The Russian seemed to consider. “That is rather soon.”

“I know. But it was settled by the principal Labour leaders. They must believe it to be entirely their own show.”

The Russian laughed softly, as though amused.

“Yes, yes,” he said. “They must have no inkling that we are using them. They are honest men--and that is their value to us. You cannot make a revolution without honest men.” He paused, then repeated, as though the phrase pleased him: “Every revolution has had its honest men. They are soon disposed of afterwards.”

There was a sinister note in his voice.

The German resumed:

“Clymes must go. He is too far-seeing. Number Fourteen will see to that.”

There was a hoarse murmur.

“That’s all right, gov’nor.” And then after a moment: “Suppose I’m nabbed.”

“You will have the best legal talent to defend you,” replied the German quietly. “In any case you will wear gloves fitted with the finger-prints of a notorious housebreaker. You have little to fear.”

“Oh, I ain’t afraid, gov’nor. The streets is going to run with blood.” He spoke with a grim relish. “Dreams of it, sometimes, I does. And diamonds and pearls rolling about in the gutter for anyone to pick up!”

Then Number One spoke:

“Then all is arranged. We are assured of success?”

“I--think so.” But the German spoke with less than his usual confidence.

Number One’s voice held suddenly a dangerous quality. “What has gone wrong?”

“The Labour leaders,” said the German. “If they do not declare a general strike on the 29th----”

“Why should they not?”

“As you’ve said, they’re honest. In spite of everything, I’m not sure they haven’t a sneaking faith in the Government. They will not go against it.”

Again the Russian’s fingers drummed on the table.

“To the point, my friend. I was given to understand that there was a certain document in existence which assured success.”

“That is so. If that document were placed before the leaders, the Government would be broken finally and completely.”

“Then what more do you want?”

“The document itself,” said the German bluntly.

“Ah! It is not in your possession? But you know where it is?”

“No.”

“Does anyone know where it is?”

“One person--perhaps. And we are not sure of that even.”

“Who is this person?”

“A girl.”

Tommy held his breath.

“A girl?” The Russian’s voice rose contemptuously. “And you have not made her speak? In Russia we have ways of making a girl talk.”

“This case is different,” said the German sullenly.

“How--different?” He paused a moment, then went on: “Where is the girl now?”

“The girl?” the German repeated.

“Yes.”

“She is----”

But Tommy heard no more. A crashing blow descended on his head, and all was darkness.

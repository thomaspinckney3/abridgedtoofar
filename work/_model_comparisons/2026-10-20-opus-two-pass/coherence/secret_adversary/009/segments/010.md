Earlier that same morning, while Tommy had set off on the trail of two men seen leaving the second-floor flat—one slender thread, the name "Rita," having set the Young Adventurers once more upon the track of the abductors of Jane Finn—Tuppence had retraced her steps to the entrance hall of the mansions, now tenanted by a small lift-boy polishing brass and whistling the latest air. She had always got on well with small boys; an ally in the enemy's camp was not to be despised.

Glancing mysteriously round, she leaned in. "I want a word with you." She flung back her coat and exposed a small enamelled badge—in reality the device of a local training corps, pinned in for some flowers a day or two before—but she had noted the corner of a threepenny detective novel protruding from his pocket, and the immediate enlargement of his eyes told her that her tactics were good.

"American Detective Force!" she hissed.

He fell for it.

"One of the flats?" he demanded breathlessly.

"No. 20. Calls herself Vandemeyer. Ready Rita they call her in the States." She produced a long-drawn-out wink and elaborated: old man Rysdale's emeralds, finest collection in the world, worth a million dollars. The boy was ecstatic—it was just like the pictures. So was Tuppence: she was a great frequenter of the cinema.

"Mind you, sonny, not a word of this. In the States we know a smart lad when we see one." She tapped him on the shoulder. "Now—how would it be if a friend of yours had a cousin that might suit the place? The maid here is leaving."

"You leave it to me, miss. I'll fix it in two ticks."

She walked briskly back to the Ritz, well pleased, and dispatched a few brief words to Mr. Carter. Tommy had not returned, which did not surprise her. She set off on a shopping expedition: a cheap clothing store, two second-hand establishments, and a well-known hairdresser's. In the seclusion of her bedroom that evening she smiled at her reflection. An actress's pencil had altered the line of her eyebrows; that, taken with the new luxuriant growth of fair hair above, so changed her appearance that even Whittington would not recognize her. With elevators in her shoes and the cap and apron of service, the disguise would be complete; from hospital experience she knew that a nurse out of uniform was frequently unrecognized by her patients.

"You'll do," she told the pert reflection.

The morning post brought a note from Mr. Carter:

"DEAR MISS TUPPENCE,—You have made a splendid start, and I congratulate you. I should like to point out once more the risks you are running. Those people are absolutely desperate and incapable of either mercy or pity. If you choose to withdraw now no one could blame you.

"If you decide to go through with it, everything is arranged. You have lived for two years with Miss Dufferin, The Parsonage, Llanelly, and Mrs. Vandemeyer can apply to her for a reference. A word of advice—stick as near to the truth as possible. I suggest you represent yourself as what you are, a former V.A.D. who has chosen domestic service. There are many such at present. That explains away any incongruities of voice or manner.

"Good luck.—Your sincere friend, MR. CARTER."

Tuppence's spirits rose mercurially; the warnings passed unheeded. The post also brought a somewhat dirty postcard with "It's O.K." scrawled on it—Tommy.

At ten-thirty she surveyed with pride a battered, artistically corded tin trunk, drove to Paddington, deposited it in the cloak room, and repaired with a handbag to the ladies' waiting-room. Ten minutes later a metamorphosed Tuppence walked demurely out and boarded a bus.

A few minutes past eleven she rang the bell of No. 20. A smart young woman opened the door.

"It's a rotten place," she said without hesitation. "Regular old cat. Accused me of tampering with her letters. There's never anything in the waste-paper basket—she burns everything. Swell clothes, but no class. Cook knows something about her, but won't tell—scared to death of her—"

"Annie!" called a clear voice with a peculiarly steely ring.

The young woman jumped as if shot. "Yes, ma'am."

"Show her in. At once."

Tuppence was ushered into a room on the right of the long passage. A woman stood by the fireplace—no longer in her first youth, the beauty she undeniably possessed hardened and coarsened. Her pale gold hair, owing slight assistance to art, was coiled low on her neck; her eyes, of a piercing electric blue, seemed to possess a faculty of boring into the very soul of the person she was looking at. Her exquisite figure was enhanced by a wonderful gown of indigo charmeuse. And yet, despite her swaying grace and the almost ethereal beauty of her face, one felt instinctively the presence of something hard and menacing, a kind of metallic strength that found expression in the tones of her voice and in that gimlet-like quality of her eyes.

For the first time Tuppence felt afraid. She had not feared Whittington; this woman was different. Mr. Carter's warning recurred to her. Here, indeed, she might expect no mercy.

Fighting down the instinct to turn tail, she returned the lady's gaze firmly and respectfully. As though that first scrutiny had been satisfactory, Mrs. Vandemeyer motioned to a chair.

"You can sit down. How did you hear I wanted a house-parlourmaid?"

"Through a friend who knows the lift boy here. He thought the place might suit me."

Again that basilisk glance. "You speak like an educated girl."

Glibly enough Tuppence ran through her imaginary career on the lines suggested by Mr. Carter. The tension in Mrs. Vandemeyer's attitude relaxed.

"I see. Is there anyone I can write to for a reference?"

"Miss Dufferin, The Parsonage, Llanelly. I was with her two years."

"And then you thought you would get more money in London. Well, it doesn't matter to me. £50—£60—whatever you want. You can come in at once?"

"Yes, ma'am. To-day, if you like. My box is at Paddington."

"Fetch it in a taxi, then. It's an easy place; I am out a good deal. What's your name?"

"Prudence Cooper, ma'am."

"Very well, Prudence. I shall be out to lunch. The cook will show you where everything is."

"Thank you, ma'am."

Tuppence withdrew. The adventure had begun, but she felt less elated than earlier in the morning. It crossed her mind that if the unknown Jane Finn had fallen into the hands of Mrs. Vandemeyer, it was likely to have gone hard with her.

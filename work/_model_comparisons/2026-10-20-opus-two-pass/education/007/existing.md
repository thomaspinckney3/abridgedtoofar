A fourth child has the strength of his weakness. Being of no great value, he may throw himself away if he likes, and never be missed. Charles Francis Adams, the father, felt no love for Europe, which, as he and all the world agreed, unfitted Americans for America; yet a captious critic might have replied that all the success he or his father or his grandfather achieved was chiefly due to the field that Europe gave them. So much more timid are parents for their children than for themselves that Mr. and Mrs. Adams would have been content to see their children remain forever in Mount Vernon Street, could they have relied on the moral influences of Boston itself. The boy Henry wanted Europe; he observed conventions when he could not escape them; he seemed well-behaved when any one was looking at him; and what he was at heart no one could say, least of all himself. Therefore, when he presented to an exceedingly indulgent father and mother his request to begin at a German university the study of the Civil Law—although neither he nor they knew what the Civil Law was, or any reason for his studying it—the parents dutifully consented, and walked with him to the Quincy station to bid him good-bye, with a smile which he almost thought a tear.

Whether he deserved such indulgence, or was worth it, he knew as little as a professor at Harvard College; but whether worthy or not, he began his third or fourth attempt at education in November, 1858, by sailing on the steamer Persia, pride of Captain Judkins and the Cunard Line. Several of his college companions sailed with him, and the world looked cheerful until, on the third day, the world ran into a heavy storm. He learned then a lesson that stood by him better than any university teaching—the meaning of a November gale on the mid-Atlantic—which, for mere physical misery, passed endurance. He could never see the humor of sea-sickness; but the gale and the week's confusions made the first month of travel altogether the rapidest school of education he had yet found. One began to see that a great many impressions were needed to make very little education; how many could be crowded into one day without making any education at all became the pons asinorum of tourist mathematics.

The ocean, the Persia, Captain Judkins, and Mr. G. P. R. James, the most distinguished passenger, vanished in a furious gale in the Mersey to make place for the drearier picture of a Liverpool street from the Adelphi coffee-room in November murk, followed by the passionate delights of Chester and the romance of red-sandstone architecture. In days before tourists, when the romance was a reality, not a picture, the emotions were overwhelming. At Eaton Hall the boys were awed as Thackeray or Dickens would have felt in the presence of a Duke. Aristocracy was real. The long suite of lofty, gilded rooms; the portraits; the terraces and gardens; the sense of superiority in the England of the fifties, set the rich nobleman apart, above Americans and shopkeepers. Oliver Twist and Little Nell lurked in every churchyard shadow, alive. Even Charles the First was not very shadowy, standing on the tower to see his army defeated.

Then came the journey up through Birmingham and the Black District, a plunge into darkness lurid with flames; the sense of unknown horror in this weird gloom which then existed nowhere else, except in volcanic craters; the violent contrast between dense, smoky, impenetrable night and the soft green charm one glided into as one emerged. Karl Marx stood there in the mist, had he but known it; but the boy ran away from everything he disliked. He took the long, muddy, sordid, gas-lit dreariness of Oxford Street as another lesson that needed more to be rightly felt, and noticed one peculiarity: London was still London. Heavy, clumsy, arrogant, purse-proud, but not cheap; insular but large; absolutely self-confident. Boys in the street made such free comments on American clothes and figures that the travellers hurried to tall hats and long overcoats. No stranger had rights even in the Strand. History muttered down Fleet Street, like Dr. Johnson, in his ear; Vanity Fair was alive on Piccadilly in yellow chariots; blackened great houses bore funereal hatchments; and the most insolent structures in the world were the Royal Exchange and the Bank of England. He liked it best when he hated it.

Education began at the end, or perhaps would end at the beginning. Crossing to Antwerp, the Baron Osy steamed up the Scheldt through morning mists while a travelling band played and peasants dropped their tools to dance. Ostade and Teniers were alive; the thirteenth-century cathedral towered above a sixteenth-century mass of tiled roofs; the taste of the town was thick, rich, ripe, like a sweet wine—mediaeval so that Rubens seemed modern. He might as well have drunk out his excitement in old Malmsey, for all the education he got from it. One lesson he did learn, and immediately lost: he felt his middle ages and the sixteenth century alive. He was only too happy to feel himself kneeling at the foot of the Cross; he learned only to loathe the sordid necessity of getting up again and going about his stupid business.

Dropped into Berlin one morning without guide or direction, he floundered in a mere mess of misunderstandings. A student at twenty takes easily to anything, even to Berlin, and he would have accepted the thirteenth century pure and simple since his guides assured him this was the path; but a week's experience left him dazed and dull. Within a day or two he was running to beer-cellars and music-halls and dance-rooms, smoking bad tobacco, drinking poor beer, eating sauerkraut and sausages. This was easy. Trouble came when he asked for education. His friends registered him at the university; selected professors and courses; bought the Institutes of Gaius and German tomes on the Civil Law; and led him to his first lecture.

His first lecture was his last. He needed no more than one hour to see that he had made another failure in education, and this time a fatal one. That the language would require at least three months' hard work before he could touch the Law was annoying; but the shock that upset him was the university itself. Harvard had been torpid; Berlin was torpor made method. The Civil Law existed as lecture in its deadliest thirteenth-century form. The professor mumbled; the students made, or seemed to make, notes; they could have learned from books or discussion in a day more than they could learn from him in a month; yet to get a degree they must pay his fees, follow his course, be his scholars. To an American the result was worthless. Without some notion of the Common Law, the Civil Law failed him; and the student who knew enough Common Law had only to read the Pandects or commentators in America and be his own professor. Neither method nor matter nor manner could profit an American education.

This discovery shocked none of the students. They dutifully went to lectures, made notes, read textbooks, never taking the professor seriously. They were more serious in reading Heine. They were learning the language, they said. So the young man fell back on the language; being slow, he fell behind his friends; the gloom of a Berlin winter and Berlin architecture settled on his spirits like soot. One day on the Linden he caught sight of Charles Sumner in a cab and ran after him. They dined and went to hear William Tell. Sumner encouraged him: he had come to Berlin unable to say a word in the language; three months later he talked it to his cabman. Adams felt quite unable to attain such social advantages. Mr. Robert Apthorp, wintering in Berlin for its music, told how he had entered a public school and sat for months with ten-year-old boys, catching their phrases. The idea suited Adams's desperate mind. Mr. Apthorp took trouble to negotiate with the head-master of the Friedrichs-Wilhelm-Werdersches Gymnasium for permission to attend the Ober-tertia, a class of boys twelve or thirteen years old; there Adams went for three months as though he had not always avoided high schools. He never did anything else so foolish, but he was given a bit of education that served some purpose.

It was not merely the language, though three months in such fashion would teach a poodle enough to talk with a cabman; foreign students could expect no more, for they never by any chance would come in contact with German society, if society existed, about which they knew nothing. He thought himself quite Germanized in 1859 and deluded himself that he read as though it were English, which proved he knew little; but the success of his own experiment interested him less than contact with German education.

He had revolted at the American school and university; he had instantly rejected the German university; as a last experience he tried the German high school. In 1858 Berlin was a poor, keen-witted, provincial town, simple, dirty, uncivilized. Life was primitive beyond what an American boy could have imagined. Overridden by military methods and bureaucratic pettiness, Prussia was only beginning to free her hands. The future Kaiser Wilhelm I, regent for his insane brother Friedrich Wilhelm IV, seemed to pass his time looking at the passers-by from a palace window on the Linden. German manners, even at Court, were sometimes brutal; German thoroughness at school apt to be routine. Bismarck himself was struggling to begin a career against the inertia of the system. All earnest Germans were turning energies to reforming it from top to bottom; and Adams walked into a great public school to get educated at precisely the time when the Germans wanted most to get rid of the education they were forced to follow.

The head-master was excellent in his Prussian way; the instructors no worse than elsewhere; it was the system that struck the systemless American with horror. The arbitrary training given to the memory was stupefying; the strain it endured a form of torture; and the feats the boys performed without complaint pitiable. No other faculty than the memory seemed to be recognized. Least of all was any use made of reason, either analytic, synthetic, or dogmatic. The German government did not encourage reasoning.

All State education is a sort of dynamo machine for polarizing the popular mind; for turning and holding its lines of force in the direction supposed to be most effective for State purposes. The German machine was terribly efficient. Its effect on the children was pathetic. The school served the small tradesmen or bourgeoisie of the neighborhood; the children were Berliner-kinder if ever there were such, and of a class suspected of sympathy in the troubles of 1848. None was noble or connected with good society. Personally they were rather sympathetic; as objects of education they were proofs of nearly all the evils that a bad system could give. Their physique showed it first, though not wholly from school. German food was bad at best, and a diet of sauerkraut, sausage, and beer was never good; but it was not the food alone that made faces white and flesh flabby. They never breathed fresh air; they had never heard of a playground; in all Berlin not a cubic inch of oxygen was admitted in winter into an inhabited building; in school every room was tightly closed and had no ventilation; the air was foul beyond decency; and when the American opened a window in the five minutes between hours, he violated rules and was rebuked. If the boys had a holiday, they were apt to be taken on long tramps in the Thiergarten, ending in over-fatigue, tobacco-smoke, sausages, and beer. With this, they were required to prepare daily lessons that would have quickly broken down strong men of a healthy habit, and which they could learn only because their minds were morbid. The German university had seemed a failure, but the German high school was something very near an indictable nuisance.

Before April arrived, the experiment had reached its point. Nothing was left of education except the ghost of the Civil Law shut up in the darkest of closets, never to gibber again. The derisive Jew laughter of Heine ran through the university and everything else in Berlin. Of course, at twenty life is bound to be full, if only of Berlin beer; though small fragments remained of the education so promising—or promised—this is what most often happens when by-products turn out to be more valuable than staples.

German society, in an American sense, did not exist, or if it existed, never showed itself; the theatre was excellent; German opera, with the ballet, almost worth a journey; but the student's only clear gain—his single step to a higher life—came from time wasted; studies neglected; vices indulged; education reversed. It came from the despised beer-garden and music-hall, and it was accidental, unintended, unforeseen.

His companions insisted on passing two or three afternoons a week at music-halls, drinking beer, smoking German tobacco, looking at fat German women knitting, while an orchestra played dull music. Adams went for the sake of the company, with no pretence of enjoyment; when Mr. Apthorp gently protested that he exaggerated his indifference—for of course he enjoyed Beethoven—Adams replied simply that he loathed Beethoven, and felt a slight surprise when Mr. Apthorp and the others laughed as though they thought it humor. He saw no humor in it. He supposed that, except musicians, every one thought Beethoven a bore, as every one except mathematicians thought mathematics a bore. Sitting thus, mentally impassive, he was one day surprised to notice that his mind followed the movement of a Sinfonie. He could not have been more astonished had he suddenly read a new language. A prison-wall that barred his senses on one great side of life suddenly fell, of its own accord, without his knowing when it happened. Amid the fumes of coarse tobacco and poor beer, surrounded by the commonest of German Haus-frauen, a new sense burst out like a flower in his life, so superior to the old senses, so bewildering, so astonished at its own existence, that he could not credit it, and watched it as something apart, accidental, not to be trusted.

He slowly came to admit that Beethoven had partly become intelligible to him, but was the more inclined to think that Beethoven must be overrated to be so easily followed. This could not be called education, for he had never so much as listened. He had been thinking of other things. Mere mechanical repetition of certain sounds had stuck to his unconscious mind. Beethoven might have this power, but not Wagner, or not the Wagner later than Tannhäuser. Near forty years passed before he reached the Götterdämmerung.

No other sense awoke. His sense of line and color remained dull and far below an artist's level. His metaphysical sense did not spring into life to leap the bars of German expression into sympathy with the idealities of Kant and Hegel. He insisted that his faith in German thought and literature was exalted; he failed to approach German thought; he shed never a tear over the pages of Goethe and Schiller. When his father rashly ventured from time to time to write a word of common sense, the young man would listen to none, insisting that Berlin was the best of educations in the best of Germanies; yet when April came, and some genius suggested a tramp in Thüringen, his heart sang like a bird. He realized what a nightmare he had suffered, and made up his mind that, wherever else in the infinities of space and time he might seek for education, it should not be again in Berlin.

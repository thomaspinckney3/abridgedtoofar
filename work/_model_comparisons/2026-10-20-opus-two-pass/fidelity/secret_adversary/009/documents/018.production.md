The present's troubles pressed harder than the future's — chiefly, hunger. Tommy prowled his prison, pounded the door, was answered by no one.

"Hang it all!" he said. "They can't mean to starve me to death." Conrad's spite, he decided. A bright idea flashed: why not wait in ambush behind the door, and bring one of the decrepit pictures down on Conrad's egg-shaped head? Intoxicated by the plan, Tommy unhooked the picture of the Devil and Faust and settled in position.

Conrad did not come. At ten he flung himself on the bed and slept.

The key woke him at eight. The door swung open, and too late he remembered his scheme. A moment later he was glad: it was not Conrad who entered, but a girl carrying a tray. In the feeble gaslight he decided at once that she was one of the most beautiful girls he had ever seen — rich brown hair shot with gold, hazel eyes wide-set, a wild-rose face.

"Are you Jane Finn?" he asked breathlessly.

The girl shook her head. "My name is Annette, monsieur." She spoke in soft, broken English.

*"Française?"*

"Oui, monsieur."

He inspected the tray — loaf, margarine, coffee. "The living is not equal to the *Ritz*. But for what we are about to receive the Lord has made me truly thankful. Amen. — What are you doing in this house?"

"I do the *service*, monsieur."

"Have you heard the name Jane Finn?"

"I have heard people speak of her, I think. — I must go."

The key turned. *That girl might help me*, mused Tommy.

That evening he tried. She was alone. "Annette, help me get out."

"Impossible. There are three of them on the floor below."

"But you would, if you could?"

"No, monsieur. They are my own people. You have spied on them."

"They're a bad lot. I'll take you away. A good whack of money."

"I dare not. I am afraid."

"Wouldn't you save another girl from their clutches? Jane Finn?"

She passed her hand across her forehead. "Jane Finn. Always I hear that name. It is familiar."

"You must know *something*?"

"I know nothing — only the name." She walked toward the door, then uttered a cry. She had caught sight of the picture against the wall. For a moment terror flashed in her eyes; as inexplicably it changed to relief. Then she was gone.

Three more days passed in dreary inaction. The girl had become dumb; a dark suspicion smouldered in her eyes. They were waiting, Tommy gathered, on orders from Mr. Brown.

The third evening brought a rude awakening. Conrad entered with the evil-looking Number 14 and a length of fine cord. In a minute Tommy was a helpless bundle.

"Thought you'd bluffed us, did you?" Conrad spat. "All the time it was bluff. Your number's up now."

"Why not cut my throat without delay?"

"And have the police nosing round? We've ordered the carriage for your lordship to-morrow morning."

An hour later the key turned softly. Annette — with Conrad's voice behind her: "He doesn't want supper to-night."

"But I must take the other tray."

She crossed to the table, raised a hand, turned out the light.

"Curse you — why did you do that?"

"I always turn it out."

"Le beau petit monsieur," she cried gaily, pausing by the bed in the darkness. "You have tied him up well, *hein?* He is like a trussed chicken!" — and as her hand ran lightly over his bonds, she pressed something small and cold into his palm.

The door shut. The object was a penknife, blade open. From her studied avoidance of his eyes and her trick with the light, he understood: there was a peep-hole. He had been observed all along.

He sawed at the cord. The blade cut his wrist badly, but at last it slackened. He bound the wound, unhooked the famous picture again, and waited.

At last, footsteps. The door opened; Conrad went straight to the gas. Number 14 followed — and as he stepped across the threshold, Tommy brought the picture down with terrific force on his head. Number 14 went down amidst a stupendous crash of broken glass. Tommy slipped out, pulled the door, turned the key as Conrad hurled himself against it from inside.

The German's voice came up the stairs. "Gott im Himmel! Conrad, what is it?"

Annette's hand was in his, pointing up a rickety ladder. "Quick — up here!"

In the dusty garret she put her finger to her lips. "They will think you are still inside. The door is too thick." She bent, fastening string to the handle of a cracked jug. "Give me the key. Swing behind the ladder. There's a cupboard in the shadow of the landing — when I've let the others out, *pull!*"

She flitted down with a loud cry: "Mon Dieu! Qu'est-ce qu'il y a?"

Tommy crouched behind the cupboard. "Mon Dieu, voilà la clef!" The German snatched it; Conrad stumbled out swearing.

"Where is he?"

"We have seen no one."

"He's got away."

"Impossible. He would have passed us."

Tommy pulled the string. A crash of crockery sounded above; in a trice the men were scrambling up the ladder.

He leapt out and dashed down, pulling the girl with him. He fumbled the bolts; the door swung open. He turned. Annette had disappeared.

He fumed, but would not go without her. Suddenly her voice floated down, clear and high:

"Ma foi, he has escaped! And quickly! Who would have thought it?"

Was that a command to him to go?

Then, louder still: "This is a terrible house. I want to go back to Marguerite. To Marguerite. *To Marguerite!*"

He ran back to the stairs — but Conrad was leaping down with a savage cry. Tommy stopped his rush with a straight blow on the point of the jaw; Conrad fell like a log. The second man tripped over him. From higher up, a flash; a bullet grazed Tommy's ear.

He leapt for the door. A baker's van waited at the kerb — evidently meant to bear his body out of London. He felled the driver and took to his heels as a hail of bullets followed. He stumbled over a prostrate figure who started up with a yell of alarm and dashed off down the street; Tommy drew back into a doorway and had the pleasure of seeing his pursuers track the red herring.

Half-past five; growing light. A passing policeman eyed him suspiciously, and Tommy laughed — he had not shaved or washed in three days. A Turkish bath, then breakfast at an A.B.C. shop. Reading the morning paper, he stiffened. A long article on Kramenin — the "man behind Bolshevism," just arrived in London — and in the centre of the page his portrait.

"So that's who Number 1 is," said Tommy with his mouth full of eggs and bacon.

At Whitehall he was admitted to the man who did not there go by the name of Mr. Carter, and detailed the last few days. Halfway through, Carter gave cryptic orders by telephone.

"Number 1 is Kramenin? Important. We want something against him badly. Look through these photos."

Tommy held one up.

"Ah, Westway! Poses as moderate." Carter handed him another. Tommy exclaimed. "I'm right then. Irishman. Prominent Unionist M.P. — all a blind. The 29th gives us very little time. We can deal with the General Strike menace — a sporting chance. But if that draft treaty turns up, England will be plunged in anarchy."

The Soho house had been emptied of every paper. No sign of Annette.

"You believe she deliberately went back?"

"It would seem so."

"H'm. She must belong to the gang — but, being a woman, didn't feel like standing by to see a personable young man killed."

"I can't believe she's really one of them, sir. She seemed so different —"

"Good-looking, I suppose?" said Carter, with a smile that made Tommy flush. "By the way — have you shown yourself to Miss Tuppence? She's been bombarding me with letters."

"Did she go to the police?"

Carter shook his head.

"Then I wonder how they twigged me."

"Curious. Unless the mention of the *Ritz* was accidental. — Remember, you're a marked man now."

In the taxi to the *Ritz* Tommy dwelt on the pleasure of startling Tuppence. Marguerite must be Mrs. Vandemeyer; the thought saddened him — it seemed to prove Annette was on intimate terms with that woman.

The taxi drew up. Tommy burst into the sacred portals — and his enthusiasm received a check. Miss Cowley had gone out a quarter of an hour ago.

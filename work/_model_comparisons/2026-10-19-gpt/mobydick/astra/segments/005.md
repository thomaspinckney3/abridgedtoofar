### The Carpet-Bag.

I stuffed a shirt or two into my old carpet-bag, tucked it under my arm, and started for Cape Horn and the Pacific. Quitting the good city of old Manhatto, I duly arrived in New Bedford. It was a Saturday night in December. Much was I disappointed upon learning that the little packet for Nantucket had already sailed, and that no way of reaching that place would offer, till the following Monday.

My mind was made up to sail in no other than a Nantucket craft, because there was a fine, boisterous something about everything connected with that famous old island, which amazingly pleased me. Though New Bedford has been gradually monopolising the business of whaling, Nantucket was her great original—the Tyre of this Carthage. Where else did those aboriginal whalemen, the Red-Men, first sally out in canoes to give chase to the Leviathan?

Having two nights and a day before me in New Bedford, it became a matter of concernment where I was to eat and sleep. It was a very dark and dismal night, bitingly cold and cheerless. I knew no one in the place. With anxious grapnels I had sounded my pocket, and only brought up a few pieces of silver. Wherever you may conclude to lodge, my dear Ishmael, said I to myself, be sure to inquire the price, and don’t be too particular.

I passed “The Crossed Harpoons”—but it looked too expensive and jolly there. From the red windows of the “Sword-Fish Inn” came rays that seemed to have melted the snow and ice before the house. Too expensive and jolly, again thought I, hearing the tinkling glasses within. Go on, Ishmael; your patched boots are stopping the way. I now by instinct followed the streets that took me waterward, for there, doubtless, were the cheapest, if not the cheeriest inns.

Such dreary streets! blocks of blackness, not houses, on either hand, and here and there a candle, like a candle moving about in a tomb. Presently a low, wide building stood invitingly open. Entering, I stumbled over an ash-box. Ha! thought I, are these ashes from that destroyed city, Gomorrah? Hearing a loud voice within, I opened a second door.

It seemed the great Black Parliament sitting in Tophet. A hundred black faces turned round in their rows to peer; and beyond, a black Angel of Doom was beating a book in a pulpit. It was a negro church; and the preacher’s text was about the blackness of darkness, and the weeping and wailing and teeth-gnashing there. Ha, Ishmael, muttered I, backing out, Wretched entertainment at the sign of ‘The Trap!’

Near the docks I saw a swinging sign, faintly representing a tall straight jet of misty spray, with the innkeeper’s name beneath—“The Spouter Inn:—Peter Coffin.”

Coffin?—Spouter?—Rather ominous in that particular connexion, thought I. But it is a common name in Nantucket, they say. The dim light, dilapidated wooden house, and poverty-stricken creak of the sign promised cheap lodgings, and the best of pea coffee.

It stood on a sharp bleak corner, where that tempestuous wind Euroclydon kept up its howling. Yet, as an old writer—of whose works I possess the only copy extant—says, it maketh a marvellous difference whether thou lookest out from a glass window where the frost is all outside, or from that sashless window where the frost is on both sides, and the wight Death the only glazier. Yes, these eyes are windows, and this body of mine is the house. But it’s too late to make improvements now. The universe is finished; the copestone is on, and the chips were carted off a million years ago.

Poor Lazarus, the beggar, chattering his teeth against the curbstone for his pillow, could not keep out Euroclydon. The rich Dives, in his red silken wrapper—(he had a redder one afterwards)—might praise the frosty night, and make his own summer with his own coals.

But what thinks Lazarus? Can he warm his blue hands by holding them up to the grand northern lights? Would he not rather go down to the fiery pit itself, in order to keep out this frost?

Yet Dives himself, he too lives like a Czar in an ice palace made of frozen sighs, and being a president of a temperance society, he only drinks the tepid tears of orphans.

But no more of this blubbering now, we are going a-whaling, and there is plenty of that yet to come. Let us scrape the ice from our frosted feet, and see what sort of a place this “Spouter” may be.

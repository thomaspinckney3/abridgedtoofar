The Carpet-Bag.

I stuffed a shirt or two into my old carpet-bag, tucked it under my arm, and started for Cape Horn and the Pacific. Quitting the good city of old Manhatto, I duly arrived in New Bedford. It was a Saturday night in December. The little packet for Nantucket had already sailed, and no way of reaching that place would offer, till the following Monday.

My mind was made up to sail in no other than a Nantucket craft. Though New Bedford has of late been gradually monopolising the business of whaling, Nantucket was her great original—the Tyre of this Carthage. Where else did those aboriginal whalemen, the Red-Men, first sally out in canoes to give chase to the Leviathan?

Having a night, a day, and still another night before me, it became a matter of concernment where I was to eat and sleep. It was a dark and dismal night, bitingly cold. I knew no one in the place. With anxious grapnels I had sounded my pocket, and only brought up a few pieces of silver. Wherever you go, Ishmael, said I to myself, be sure to inquire the price, and don’t be too particular.

I passed “The Crossed Harpoons”—too expensive and jolly. From the red windows of the “Sword-Fish Inn” came rays that seemed to have melted the snow before the house. Too expensive and jolly, again thought I. Go on, Ishmael; your patched boots are stopping the way. By instinct I followed the streets waterward, for there were doubtless the cheapest inns.

Such dreary streets! blocks of blackness, not houses, on either hand. Presently I came to a smoky light from a low building with an invitingly open door. Entering, I stumbled over an ash-box. Were these ashes from that destroyed city, Gomorrah? This must needs be the sign of “The Trap.” Hearing a loud voice, I opened an interior door.

It seemed the great Black Parliament sitting in Tophet. A hundred black faces turned round in their rows to peer; and beyond, a black Angel of Doom was beating a book in a pulpit. It was a negro church; and the preacher’s text was about the blackness of darkness, and the weeping and wailing and teeth-gnashing there. Ha, Ishmael, muttered I, backing out, Wretched entertainment at the sign of ‘The Trap!’

Near the docks I saw a swinging sign, faintly representing a tall straight jet of misty spray, with these words underneath—“The Spouter Inn:—Peter Coffin.”

Coffin?—Spouter?—Rather ominous in that particular connexion, thought I. But it is a common name in Nantucket, they say. The dim light, dilapidated house, and poverty-stricken creak of the sign promised cheap lodgings, and the best of pea coffee.

It stood on a sharp bleak corner, where that tempestuous wind Euroclydon kept up its howling. Yet it is a mighty pleasant zephyr to any one in-doors, with his feet on the hob. “In judging of that tempestuous wind called Euroclydon,” says an old writer—of whose works I possess the only copy extant—“it maketh a marvellous difference, whether thou lookest out at it from a glass window where the frost is all on the outside, or whether thou observest it from that sashless window, where the frost is on both sides, and of which the wight Death is the only glazier.”

Yes, these eyes are windows, and this body of mine is the house. What a pity they didn’t stop up the chinks! But it’s too late. The universe is finished; the copestone is on, and the chips were carted off a million years ago.

Poor Lazarus, chattering his teeth against the curbstone for his pillow, could not keep out Euroclydon. But rich old Dives, in his red silken wrapper—(he had a redder one afterwards)—would cry, What a fine frosty night! give me the privilege of making my own summer with my own coals.

But what thinks Lazarus? Can he warm his blue hands by holding them up to the grand northern lights? That Lazarus should lie stranded before the door of Dives is more wonderful than that an iceberg should be moored to one of the Moluccas. Yet Dives himself lives like a Czar in an ice palace made of frozen sighs, and being a president of a temperance society, he only drinks the tepid tears of orphans.

But no more of this blubbering now, we are going a-whaling, and there is plenty of that yet to come. Let us scrape the ice from our frosted feet, and see what sort of a place this “Spouter” may be.

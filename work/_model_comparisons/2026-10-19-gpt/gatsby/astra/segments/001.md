# I

In my younger and more vulnerable years my father gave me some advice that I’ve been turning over in my mind ever since.

“Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone,” he told me, “just remember that all the people in this world haven’t had the advantages that you’ve had.”

We’ve always been unusually communicative in a reserved way, and I understood that he meant a great deal more than that. In consequence, I’m inclined to reserve all judgements, a habit that has opened up many curious natures to me and also made me the victim of not a few veteran bores. Most of the confidences were unsought; the intimate revelations of young men, or at least the terms in which they express them, are usually plagiaristic and marred by obvious suppressions. Reserving judgements is a matter of infinite hope. I am still a little afraid of missing something if I forget that, as my father snobbishly suggested, and I snobbishly repeat, a sense of the fundamental decencies is parcelled out unequally at birth.

And, after boasting this way of my tolerance, I come to the admission that it has a limit. When I came back from the East last autumn I felt that I wanted the world to be in uniform and at a sort of moral attention forever. Only Gatsby, the man who gives his name to this book, was exempt from my reaction—Gatsby, who represented everything for which I have an unaffected scorn. If personality is an unbroken series of successful gestures, then there was something gorgeous about him, some heightened sensitivity to the promises of life, as if he were related to one of those intricate machines that register earthquakes ten thousand miles away. It was an extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness such as I have never found in any other person and which it is not likely I shall ever find again. No—Gatsby turned out all right at the end; it is what preyed on Gatsby, what foul dust floated in the wake of his dreams that temporarily closed out my interest in the abortive sorrows and short-winded elations of men.

---

My family, the Carraways, have been prominent, well-to-do people in this Middle Western city for three generations. I graduated from New Haven in 1915, just a quarter of a century after my father, and a little later I participated in that delayed Teutonic migration known as the Great War. I enjoyed the counter-raid so thoroughly that I came back restless. Instead of being the warm centre of the world, the Middle West now seemed like the ragged edge of the universe—so I decided to go East and learn the bond business. Father agreed to finance me for a year, and I came East, permanently, I thought, in the spring of twenty-two.

A young man at the office suggested that we take a house in a commuting town, but the firm ordered him to Washington, and I went out alone. I had a weather-beaten cardboard bungalow at eighty a month, an old Dodge and a Finnish woman who cooked breakfast and muttered Finnish wisdom over the electric stove.

It was lonely until a newcomer asked me the way to West Egg village. I was a guide, a pathfinder, an original settler. He had casually conferred on me the freedom of the neighbourhood. With the sunshine and the great bursts of leaves growing on the trees, I had that familiar conviction that life was beginning over again with the summer.

I bought a dozen volumes on banking and credit and investment securities, and had the high intention of reading many other books besides. I was rather literary in college, and now I was going to become again that most limited of all specialists, the “well-rounded man.” This isn’t just an epigram—life is much more successfully looked at from a single window, after all.

Twenty miles from New York a pair of enormous eggs, identical in contour and separated only by a courtesy bay, jut out into Long Island Sound. To the wingless their dissimilarity in every particular except shape and size is the more interesting phenomenon.

I lived at West Egg, the—well, the less fashionable of the two, though this is a most superficial tag to express the bizarre and not a little sinister contrast between them. My house was squeezed between two huge places. The one on my right was a factual imitation of some Hôtel de Ville in Normandy, spanking new under a thin beard of raw ivy, with a marble swimming pool and more than forty acres of lawn and garden. It was Gatsby’s mansion. Or, rather, as I didn’t know Mr. Gatsby, it was a mansion inhabited by a gentleman of that name. My own house was a small eyesore, overlooked, with a view of the water and the consoling proximity of millionaires—all for eighty dollars a month.

Across the courtesy bay the white palaces of fashionable East Egg glittered along the water. The history of the summer really begins on the evening I drove over there to have dinner with the Tom Buchanans. Daisy was my second cousin once removed, and I’d known Tom in college.

Her husband had been one of the most powerful ends that ever played football at New Haven—one of those men who reach such an acute limited excellence at twenty-one that everything afterward savours of anticlimax. His family were enormously wealthy; now he’d left Chicago and come East with a string of polo ponies. They had drifted here and there unrestfully wherever people played polo and were rich together. This was a permanent move, said Daisy, but I felt that Tom would drift on forever seeking, a little wistfully, the dramatic turbulence of some irrecoverable football game.

Their Georgian Colonial mansion overlooked the bay. The lawn started at the beach and ran towards the front door for a quarter of a mile, jumping over sundials and brick walks and burning gardens. Tom stood on the porch in riding clothes, a sturdy straw-haired man of thirty, with a rather hard mouth and a supercilious manner. Two shining arrogant eyes gave him the appearance of always leaning aggressively forward. It was a body capable of enormous leverage—a cruel body. His gruff husky tenor carried a touch of paternal contempt, even toward people he liked.

“I’ve got a nice place here,” he said, his eyes flashing about restlessly.

Turning me around by one arm, he swept a broad flat hand along the vista; then he turned me around again, politely and abruptly, and took me inside.

A breeze blew through the rosy-coloured room, blew curtains in at one end and out the other like pale flags, twisting them up toward the frosted wedding-cake of the ceiling. On an enormous couch two young women were buoyed up as though upon an anchored balloon. They were both in white. Then there was a boom as Tom shut the rear windows, and the curtains and the rugs and the two young women ballooned slowly to the floor.

The younger was a stranger, motionless, her chin raised as if she were balancing something on it. Daisy leaned slightly forward, then laughed an absurd, charming little laugh.

“I’m p-paralysed with happiness.”

She held my hand, looking up into my face, promising that there was no one in the world she so much wanted to see. She hinted in a murmur that the surname of the balancing girl was Baker. Miss Baker nodded almost imperceptibly and tipped her head back again. Almost any exhibition of complete self-sufficiency draws a stunned tribute from me.

My cousin began to ask me questions in her low, thrilling voice. Her face was sad and lovely with bright things in it, bright eyes and a bright passionate mouth, but there was an excitement in her voice that men who had cared for her found difficult to forget: a singing compulsion, a whispered “Listen,” a promise that she had done gay, exciting things just a while since and that there were gay, exciting things hovering in the next hour.

She spoke of her three-year-old baby, whom I had never seen; then Tom rested his hand on my shoulder.

“What you doing, Nick?”

“I’m a bond man.”

When I named my firm he remarked decisively that he had never heard of them.

“You will,” I answered shortly. “You will if you stay in the East.”

“Oh, I’ll stay in the East, don’t you worry,” he said, glancing at Daisy. “I’d be a God damned fool to live anywhere else.”

Miss Baker rose and refused a cocktail: she was absolutely in training. I enjoyed looking at her—a slender, small-breasted girl, her shoulders thrown backward like a young cadet’s, with grey sun-strained eyes in a wan, charming, discontented face. I had seen her, or a picture of her, somewhere before.

“You live in West Egg,” she remarked contemptuously. “I know somebody there.”

“I don’t know a single—”

“You must know Gatsby,” said Miss Baker.

“Gatsby?” demanded Daisy. “What Gatsby?”

Before I could reply that he was my neighbour dinner was announced; Tom compelled me from the room as though he were moving a checker to another square.

On the porch four candles flickered in the diminished wind. Daisy snapped them out.

“In two weeks it’ll be the longest day in the year,” she said. “Do you always watch for the longest day of the year and then miss it? I always watch for the longest day in the year and then miss it.”

Miss Baker suggested planning something, but Daisy turned helplessly to me to ask what people planned. Then her eyes fastened on her little finger. The knuckle was black and blue.

“You did it, Tom,” she said accusingly. “I know you didn’t mean to, but you did do it. That’s what I get for marrying a brute of a man, a great, big, hulking physical specimen of a—”

“I hate that word ‘hulking,’” objected Tom crossly, “even in kidding.”

“Hulking,” insisted Daisy.

Sometimes the women talked at once, with a bantering inconsequence as cool as their white dresses and their impersonal eyes in the absence of all desire. Presently dinner would be over and a little later the evening too would be over and casually put away.

“You make me feel uncivilized, Daisy,” I confessed. “Can’t you talk about crops or something?”

“Civilization’s going to pieces,” broke out Tom violently. “Have you read The Rise of the Coloured Empires by this man Goddard?”

“Why, no,” I answered.

“Well, it’s a fine book, and everybody ought to read it. The idea is if we don’t look out the white race will be—will be utterly submerged. It’s all scientific stuff; it’s been proved.”

“Tom’s getting very profound,” said Daisy, with unthoughtful sadness. “He reads deep books with long words in them.”

“This fellow has worked out the whole thing,” Tom insisted. “It’s up to us, who are the dominant race, to watch out or these other races will have control of things.”

“We’ve got to beat them down,” whispered Daisy, winking ferociously toward the fervent sun.

“This idea is that we’re Nordics,” Tom continued. “I am, and you are, and you are, and—” After an infinitesimal hesitation he included Daisy with a slight nod. “—And we’ve produced all the things that go to make civilization—oh, science and art, and all that. Do you see?”

There was something pathetic in his concentration, as if his complacency was not enough to him any more. The telephone rang inside. Presently the butler murmured something in Tom’s ear, and he frowned and went in.

Daisy leaned forward, her voice glowing and singing.

“I love to see you at my table, Nick. You remind me of a—of a rose, an absolute rose.”

This was untrue. I am not even faintly like a rose. She was only extemporizing, but a stirring warmth flowed from her. Then suddenly she threw her napkin on the table and followed Tom.

Miss Baker leaned forward unashamed, trying to hear a subdued impassioned murmur from the room beyond.

“This Mr. Gatsby you spoke of is my neighbour—” I began.

“Don’t talk. I want to hear what happens.”

“Is something happening?” I inquired innocently.

“You mean to say you don’t know?” said Miss Baker. “I thought everybody knew.”

“I don’t.”

“Tom’s got some woman in New York,” she said. “She might have the decency not to telephone him at dinner time. Don’t you think?”

Tom and Daisy returned almost before I had grasped her meaning.

“It couldn’t be helped!” cried Daisy with tense gaiety. She spoke of a nightingale on the lawn. “It’s romantic, isn’t it, Tom?”

“Very romantic,” he said, and then miserably offered to show me the stables after dinner.

The telephone rang again, startlingly. Daisy shook her head decisively at Tom, and all subjects vanished into air. I remember the candles being lit again, pointlessly, and wanting to look squarely at everyone, and yet to avoid all eyes. Even Miss Baker’s hardy scepticism could hardly put this fifth guest’s shrill metallic urgency out of mind. My own instinct was to telephone immediately for the police.

Tom and Miss Baker strolled into the library, while I followed Daisy to the front porch. We sat side by side in the deep gloom. Seeing that turbulent emotions possessed her, I asked some sedative questions about her little girl.

“We don’t know each other very well, Nick,” she said. “Even if we are cousins. You didn’t come to my wedding.”

“I wasn’t back from the war.”

“That’s true.” She hesitated. “Well, I’ve had a very bad time, Nick, and I’m pretty cynical about everything.”

I returned rather feebly to the subject of her daughter.

“Listen, Nick; let me tell you what I said when she was born,” Daisy said. “Well, she was less than an hour old and Tom was God knows where. I woke up out of the ether with an utterly abandoned feeling, and asked the nurse right away if it was a boy or a girl. She told me it was a girl, and so I turned my head away and wept. ‘All right,’ I said, ‘I’m glad it’s a girl. And I hope she’ll be a fool—that’s the best thing a girl can be in this world, a beautiful little fool.’

“You see I think everything’s terrible anyhow,” she went on. “Everybody thinks so—the most advanced people. And I know. I’ve been everywhere and seen everything and done everything.” She laughed with thrilling scorn. “Sophisticated—God, I’m sophisticated!”

The instant her voice broke off, ceasing to compel my attention, my belief, I felt the basic insincerity of what she had said. It made me uneasy, as though the whole evening had been a trick to exact a contributory emotion from me. In a moment she looked at me with an absolute smirk on her lovely face, as if she had asserted her membership in a rather distinguished secret society to which she and Tom belonged.

---

Inside, Miss Baker was reading aloud to Tom from the Saturday Evening Post. She tossed the magazine down and rose to go to bed.

“Jordan’s going to play in the tournament tomorrow,” explained Daisy, “over at Westchester.”

“Oh—you’re Jordan Baker.”

Her pleasing contemptuous expression had looked out at me from many rotogravure pictures of the sporting life. I had heard some critical, unpleasant story of her too, but had forgotten it.

As she said good night, Daisy promised to fling us together, to lock us accidentally in linen closets and push us out to sea in a boat.

“They oughtn’t to let her run around the country this way,” Tom said when she had gone.

“Who oughtn’t to?” Daisy inquired coldly.

“Her family.”

“Her family is one aunt about a thousand years old,” Daisy replied. Jordan would spend weekends here; the home influence would be very good for her.

Daisy and Tom looked at each other in silence. I asked quickly where Jordan came from.

“From Louisville,” Daisy answered. “Our white girlhood was passed together there. Our beautiful white—”

“Did you give Nick a little heart to heart talk on the veranda?” demanded Tom.

Daisy professed to remember only a discussion of the Nordic race.

“Don’t believe everything you hear, Nick,” he advised me.

I said I had heard nothing at all. As I was leaving, Daisy asked about a rumoured engagement out West.

“It’s a libel. I’m too poor.”

“But we heard it,” she insisted. “We heard it from three people, so it must be true.”

I wasn’t even vaguely engaged. The gossip was one of the reasons I had come East; I had no intention of being rumoured into marriage.

Their interest touched me—nevertheless, I drove away confused and a little disgusted. Daisy should rush out, child in arms—but apparently she had no such intentions. Tom’s woman was less surprising than his depression over a book, as if his sturdy physical egotism no longer nourished his peremptory heart.

At home I sat on a grass roller in the yard. The wind had blown off, leaving a loud, bright night, with wings beating in the trees. Fifty feet away a figure emerged from my neighbour’s mansion and stood regarding the silver pepper of the stars. His leisurely movements and secure feet suggested Mr. Gatsby himself, come out to determine what share was his of our local heavens.

I decided to call to him. Miss Baker had mentioned him at dinner, and that would do for an introduction. But I didn’t call to him, for he gave a sudden intimation that he was content to be alone—he stretched out his arms toward the dark water in a curious way, and, far as I was from him, I could have sworn he was trembling. Involuntarily I glanced seaward—and distinguished nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock. When I looked once more for Gatsby he had vanished, and I was alone again in the unquiet darkness.

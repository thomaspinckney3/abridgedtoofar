# I

In my younger and more vulnerable years my father gave me some advice that I’ve been turning over in my mind ever since.

“Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone,” he told me, “just remember that all the people in this world haven’t had the advantages that you’ve had.”

He didn’t say any more, but we’ve always been unusually communicative in a reserved way, and I understood that he meant a great deal more than that. In consequence, I’m inclined to reserve all judgements, a habit that has opened up many curious natures to me and also made me the victim of not a few veteran bores. Most of the confidences were unsought; the intimate revelations of young men, or at least the terms in which they express them, are usually plagiaristic and marred by obvious suppressions. Reserving judgements is a matter of infinite hope. I am still a little afraid of missing something if I forget that, as my father snobbishly suggested, and I snobbishly repeat, a sense of the fundamental decencies is parcelled out unequally at birth.

And, after boasting this way of my tolerance, I come to the admission that it has a limit. When I came back from the East last autumn I felt that I wanted the world to be in uniform and at a sort of moral attention forever; I wanted no more riotous excursions with privileged glimpses into the human heart. Only Gatsby, the man who gives his name to this book, was exempt from my reaction—Gatsby, who represented everything for which I have an unaffected scorn. If personality is an unbroken series of successful gestures, then there was something gorgeous about him, some heightened sensitivity to the promises of life, as if he were related to one of those intricate machines that register earthquakes ten thousand miles away. It was an extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness such as I have never found in any other person and which it is not likely I shall ever find again. No—Gatsby turned out all right at the end; it is what preyed on Gatsby, what foul dust floated in the wake of his dreams that temporarily closed out my interest in the abortive sorrows and short-winded elations of men.

---

My family, the Carraways, have been prominent, well-to-do people in this Middle Western city for three generations. I graduated from New Haven in 1915, just a quarter of a century after my father, and a little later I participated in that delayed Teutonic migration known as the Great War. I enjoyed the counter-raid so thoroughly that I came back restless. Instead of being the warm centre of the world, the Middle West now seemed like the ragged edge of the universe—so I decided to go East and learn the bond business. Father agreed to finance me for a year, and I came East, permanently, I thought, in the spring of twenty-two.

A young man at the office suggested that we take a house together in a commuting town, but at the last minute the firm ordered him to Washington, and I went out to the country alone. I had an old Dodge and a Finnish woman who made my bed and cooked breakfast. It was lonely until a newer arrival asked me the way to West Egg village. I told him, and was lonely no longer. I was a guide, a pathfinder, an original settler.

And so with the sunshine and the great bursts of leaves growing on the trees, just as things grow in fast movies, I had that familiar conviction that life was beginning over again with the summer.

I bought volumes on banking and credit and investment securities, which stood on my shelf in red and gold like new money from the mint. I had been rather literary in college, and now I was going to become again that most limited of all specialists, the “well-rounded man.” This isn’t just an epigram—life is much more successfully looked at from a single window, after all.

Twenty miles from New York a pair of enormous eggs, identical in contour and separated only by a courtesy bay, jut out into Long Island Sound. To the wingless their interesting feature is their dissimilarity in every particular except shape and size.

I lived at West Egg, the—well, the less fashionable of the two, though this is a most superficial tag to express the bizarre and not a little sinister contrast between them. My house was squeezed between two huge places. The one on my right was a factual imitation of some Hôtel de Ville in Normandy, spanking new under a thin beard of raw ivy, with a marble swimming pool and more than forty acres of lawn and garden. It was Gatsby’s mansion, though I didn’t know Mr. Gatsby. My own house was a small eyesore, overlooked, with a view of the water and the consoling proximity of millionaires—all for eighty dollars a month.

Across the courtesy bay the white palaces of fashionable East Egg glittered along the water, and the history of the summer really begins on the evening I drove over there to have dinner with the Tom Buchanans. Daisy was my second cousin once removed, and I’d known Tom in college.

Her husband had been one of the most powerful ends that ever played football at New Haven—one of those men who reach such an acute limited excellence at twenty-one that everything afterward savours of anticlimax. His family were enormously wealthy; now he had brought a string of polo ponies East from Lake Forest. They had drifted unrestfully wherever people played polo and were rich together. This was a permanent move, Daisy said, but I felt Tom would drift on forever seeking the dramatic turbulence of some irrecoverable football game.

Their red-and-white Georgian Colonial mansion overlooked the bay. The lawn ran from the beach towards the front door, jumping over sundials and brick walks and burning gardens. Tom stood on the porch in riding clothes, a sturdy straw-haired man of thirty with a hard mouth and a supercilious manner. Two shining arrogant eyes gave him the appearance of always leaning aggressively forward. His muscles shifted under his thin coat. It was a body capable of enormous leverage—a cruel body. His gruff husky tenor had a touch of paternal contempt even toward people he liked.

“I’ve got a nice place here,” he said, sweeping a broad flat hand over the garden and a motorboat offshore. Then he turned me around, politely and abruptly. “We’ll go inside.”

A breeze blew through the rosy-coloured room, twisting the white curtains toward the frosted wedding-cake of the ceiling, rippling over the wine-coloured rug. Two young women in white were buoyed up on an enormous couch as though upon an anchored balloon. Then Tom shut the rear windows with a boom, and the curtains and the rugs and the two young women ballooned slowly to the floor.

The stranger lay motionless, her chin raised as if balancing something upon it. Daisy leaned slightly forward, then laughed an absurd, charming little laugh.

“I’m p-paralysed with happiness.”

She held my hand, looking up into my face, promising that there was no one in the world she so much wanted to see. She murmured that the other girl’s surname was Baker. Miss Baker nodded almost imperceptibly and tipped her head back again. Almost any exhibition of complete self-sufficiency draws a stunned tribute from me.

Daisy began asking questions in her low, thrilling voice. Her face was sad and lovely with bright things in it, but there was an excitement in her voice that men who had cared for her found difficult to forget: a singing compulsion, a whispered “Listen,” a promise that she had done gay, exciting things just a while since and that there were gay, exciting things hovering in the next hour.

I brought her greetings from Chicago; she cried that we ought to go back tomorrow, then told me I ought to see her three-year-old daughter, who was asleep.

“What you doing, Nick?” Tom asked, resting a hand on my shoulder.

“I’m a bond man.”

He had never heard of my firm.

“You will,” I answered shortly. “You will if you stay in the East.”

“Oh, I’ll stay in the East, don’t you worry,” he said, glancing at Daisy.

Miss Baker declined a cocktail because she was in training. I enjoyed looking at her: slender, with an erect carriage like a young cadet, grey sun-strained eyes and a wan, charming, discontented face. I thought I had seen her picture somewhere.

“You live in West Egg,” she remarked contemptuously. “I know somebody there.”

“I don’t know a single—”

“You must know Gatsby.”

“Gatsby?” demanded Daisy. “What Gatsby?”

Before I could reply that he was my neighbour, dinner was announced. Tom wedged his tense arm under mine and compelled me from the room as though moving a checker to another square.

On the porch Daisy snapped out the candles.

“In two weeks it’ll be the longest day in the year,” she said. “Do you always watch for the longest day of the year and then miss it? I always watch for the longest day in the year and then miss it.”

“We ought to plan something,” yawned Miss Baker.

“All right,” said Daisy. “What’ll we plan?” Then her eyes fastened on her bruised little finger. “You did it, Tom. I know you didn’t mean to, but you did do it. That’s what I get for marrying a brute of a man, a great, big, hulking physical specimen of a—”

“I hate that word ‘hulking,’” objected Tom crossly, “even in kidding.”

“Hulking,” insisted Daisy.

The women talked with a cool, bantering inconsequence, accepting us, making only a polite effort to entertain or be entertained. Presently dinner would be over and then the evening too would be casually put away. It was sharply different from the West, with its continually disappointed anticipation or nervous dread of the moment itself.

“You make me feel uncivilized, Daisy,” I confessed. “Can’t you talk about crops or something?”

“Civilization’s going to pieces,” broke out Tom violently. “Have you read The Rise of the Coloured Empires by this man Goddard?”

“Why, no,” I answered.

“Well, it’s a fine book, and everybody ought to read it. The idea is if we don’t look out the white race will be—will be utterly submerged. It’s all scientific stuff; it’s been proved.”

“Tom’s getting very profound,” said Daisy, with unthoughtful sadness. “He reads deep books with long words in them.”

“It’s up to us, who are the dominant race, to watch out or these other races will have control of things,” Tom insisted.

“We’ve got to beat them down,” whispered Daisy, winking ferociously toward the fervent sun.

“This idea is that we’re Nordics. I am, and you are, and you are, and—” After an infinitesimal hesitation Tom included Daisy. “—And we’ve produced all the things that go to make civilization—oh, science and art, and all that. Do you see?”

There was something pathetic in his concentration, as if his complacency was not enough to him any more.

The telephone rang inside. Presently the butler murmured in Tom’s ear, and Tom frowned and went into the house. Daisy leaned forward, her voice glowing and singing, and told me I reminded her of an absolute rose. This was untrue; she was only extemporizing, but a stirring warmth flowed from her. Suddenly she threw her napkin on the table and followed Tom.

Miss Baker leaned forward unashamed, listening to a subdued, impassioned murmur within.

“This Mr. Gatsby you spoke of is my neighbour—” I began.

“Don’t talk. I want to hear what happens.”

“Is something happening?”

“You mean to say you don’t know?” said Miss Baker. “Tom’s got some woman in New York. She might have the decency not to telephone him at dinner time. Don’t you think?”

Tom and Daisy returned almost before I had grasped her meaning.

“It couldn’t be helped!” cried Daisy with tense gaiety. She spoke of a nightingale on the lawn. “It’s romantic, isn’t it, Tom?”

“Very romantic,” he said, then suggested showing me the stables.

The telephone rang again. Daisy shook her head decisively at Tom, and all subjects vanished into air. I remember the candles being lit again, pointlessly, and wanting to look squarely at everyone, yet avoid all eyes. Even Miss Baker’s hardy scepticism could scarcely put this fifth guest’s shrill metallic urgency out of mind. My own instinct was to telephone immediately for the police.

Tom and Miss Baker strolled into the library. I followed Daisy to the front porch, where we sat side by side in the velvet dusk. I asked some sedative questions about her little girl.

“We don’t know each other very well, Nick,” she said suddenly. “Even if we are cousins. You didn’t come to my wedding.”

“I wasn’t back from the war.”

“That’s true.” She hesitated. “Well, I’ve had a very bad time, Nick, and I’m pretty cynical about everything.”

I returned feebly to her daughter.

“Listen, Nick; let me tell you what I said when she was born,” Daisy said. “Well, she was less than an hour old and Tom was God knows where. I woke up out of the ether with an utterly abandoned feeling, and asked the nurse right away if it was a boy or a girl. She told me it was a girl, and so I turned my head away and wept. ‘All right,’ I said, ‘I’m glad it’s a girl. And I hope she’ll be a fool—that’s the best thing a girl can be in this world, a beautiful little fool.’

“You see I think everything’s terrible anyhow. Everybody thinks so—the most advanced people. And I know. I’ve been everywhere and seen everything and done everything.” She laughed with thrilling scorn. “Sophisticated—God, I’m sophisticated!”

The instant her voice broke off I felt the basic insincerity of what she had said, as though the evening had been a trick to exact a contributory emotion from me. Presently she looked at me with an absolute smirk on her lovely face, as if asserting her membership in a distinguished secret society to which she and Tom belonged.

---

Inside, Miss Baker was reading aloud to Tom from the Saturday Evening Post. She tossed it aside and stood up, declaring it was bedtime.

“Jordan’s going to play in the tournament tomorrow,” Daisy explained, “over at Westchester.”

“Oh—you’re Jordan Baker.”

I recognized the face from sporting pictures. I had heard a critical, unpleasant story about her, too, but had forgotten it.

Daisy announced that she would arrange a marriage between us, locking us accidentally in linen closets or pushing us out to sea in a boat.

“She’s a nice girl,” Tom said after Jordan had gone upstairs. “They oughtn’t to let her run around the country this way.”

Daisy explained coldly that Jordan’s family was one ancient aunt, and that I should look after her during her summer weekends here. They had grown up together in Louisville.

“Did you give Nick a little heart to heart talk on the veranda?” Tom suddenly demanded.

Daisy said we had talked about the Nordic race.

“Don’t believe everything you hear, Nick,” he advised me.

I said I had heard nothing at all, and soon got up to go. At the door Daisy called me back to ask about my supposed engagement out West.

“It’s a libel. I’m too poor.”

“But we heard it,” she insisted. “We heard it from three people, so it must be true.”

I wasn’t even vaguely engaged. Gossip was one reason I had come East; I had no intention of being rumoured into marriage.

Their interest touched me, making them less remotely rich, but I drove away confused and a little disgusted. It seemed Daisy ought to rush out, child in arms, but apparently she had no such intentions. Tom’s woman surprised me less than his depression over a book. His physical egotism no longer seemed to nourish his peremptory heart.

At West Egg I sat on an abandoned grass roller in the yard. The wind had dropped, leaving a loud, bright night, wings beating in the trees and an organ sound as the earth’s full bellows blew the frogs full of life. A cat’s silhouette wavered across the moonlight. Turning to watch, I saw a figure fifty feet away emerge from my neighbour’s mansion, hands in his pockets, regarding the silver pepper of the stars. His leisurely movements and secure feet suggested Gatsby, come out to determine what share was his of our local heavens.

I decided to call to him. Miss Baker’s mention would do for an introduction. But he gave a sudden intimation that he was content to be alone—he stretched out his arms toward the dark water in a curious way, and, far as I was from him, I could have sworn he was trembling. Involuntarily I glanced seaward—and distinguished nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock. When I looked once more for Gatsby he had vanished, and I was alone again in the unquiet darkness.

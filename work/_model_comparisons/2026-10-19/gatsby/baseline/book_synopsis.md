In the summer of 1922, Midwesterner Nick Carraway moves to Long Island to learn the bond business and rents a shabby cottage in West Egg, beside a palatial mansion owned by the elusive Jay Gatsby. Across the bay in old-money East Egg live Nick’s cousin Daisy Buchanan and her hulking, arrogant husband Tom, whose brittle charm and casual cruelty unsettle every gathering. Over dinner with Daisy and the coolly self-possessed golfer Jordan Baker, a phone call hints at Tom’s brazen affair, and Nick’s first excursion into the city with Tom exposes the squalid “valley of ashes” and Tom’s mistress, Myrtle Wilson. In a tawdry Manhattan apartment, drink loosens inhibitions until Tom, stung by Myrtle’s taunts, breaks her nose, and Nick retreats from the spectacle of wealth corroded by carelessness. Back home, he watches his neighbor reach toward a solitary green light across the water, a gesture that seems to fuse longing, distance, and promise. Through the season, music and laughter spill nightly from Gatsby’s “blue gardens,” where half of New York comes uninvited to revel under strings of colored lights.

Nick is one of the few actually invited to a party and, after hearing a swirl of rumors—spy, killer, nobleman—meets the courteous young host who calls him “old sport” and seems both theatrical and sincere. In town, Gatsby introduces Nick to Meyer Wolfshiem, a shadowy fixer said to have rigged the 1919 World Series, deepening the mystery of Gatsby’s fortune. Jordan confides that Gatsby and Daisy fell in love in Louisville before the war and that Gatsby bought his West Egg castle to be near her, hoping chance would do the rest. At Gatsby’s request, Nick arranges a tea, and the awkward reunion in his cottage dissolves into wonder as Daisy and Gatsby recover their lost intimacy. Gatsby displays his rooms like offerings—shirts thrown in bright cascades, rooms perfumed with flowers—and the lovers seem to stand within the very dream he has constructed. Yet the green light that once stood for all he desired becomes merely a lamp on Daisy’s dock, and the difference between dream and possession subtly begins to show.

Nick learns that Gatsby was born James Gatz, a restless farm boy who reinvented himself under the tutelage of the millionaire Dan Cody and later amassed wealth through dubious “drugstore” businesses with Wolfshiem. Tom, suspicious, visits Gatsby’s party, and through Daisy’s chilled eyes the carnival splendor seems tawdry, while Gatsby clings to the notion that he can repeat the past and erase her marriage. He fires his gossiping servants and begins secret afternoon visits with Daisy, but the charade collapses on the hottest day of the year when the group drives to Manhattan. In a stifling suite at the Plaza Hotel, Gatsby demands that Daisy say she never loved Tom; she wavers, confessing she loved them both, and the moral geometry of the triangle buckles. They split for home with tempers raw, Daisy driving Gatsby’s powerful yellow car to steady her nerves. Near the ash heaps, Myrtle Wilson—desperate and misled—rushes into the road, and Daisy strikes her and speeds away, leaving death and dust in the wake.

Gatsby waits outside the Buchanans’ house to shield Daisy, resolved to take the blame, while across the bay her husband and she share a cold, complicit intimacy over chicken and beer. Tormented by grief and armed with Tom’s insinuations about the car and its owner, George Wilson traces the yellow machine to West Egg and finds Gatsby floating on an air mattress in his pool, trying to taste summer at last. Wilson shoots Gatsby and then himself, and the fantastical house falls suddenly quiet, the dream’s architect silenced. Reporters descend, rumors harden, and when Nick tries to assemble mourners he discovers that Wolfsheim will not “get mixed up,” partygoers vanish, and Daisy and Tom have fled without a forwarding address. Only Gatsby’s bewildered father, Henry Gatz, arrives, proud of the boy who wrote schedules and resolves in a tattered Hopalong Cassidy, and one bespectacled party guest—Owl Eyes—comes to the wet, spare funeral. The man who hosted hundreds goes to his grave almost alone, a last indictment of the glittering crowds who fed upon his hospitality.

Disgusted, Nick breaks with Jordan and later confronts Tom on Fifth Avenue, realizing that Tom’s righteous fury and Daisy’s helpless charm mask a brutal carelessness that lets others clean up their messes. He returns to the Midwest, the East now warped in his memory like a night scene by El Greco, all splendour tilted and wrong. From the quiet shore by Gatsby’s empty house, Nick imagines the island as the Dutch first saw it and senses how the nation’s promise once met the limits of human desire. Gatsby, who believed in the green light and in the power of self-invention, had reached for an ideal that reality could not hold without breaking. Around him, the fixed hierarchies of East Egg and the crude energies of West Egg exposed the class snobbery, moral drift, and corrosive materialism beneath the Jazz Age shine. In the end, Nick understands that the American dream’s forward surge is forever checked by the undertow of the past, and that Gatsby’s faith—magnificent and doomed—was both his making and his ruin.

How I Began My Shore Adventure

The appearance of the island when I came on deck next morning was altogether changed. The breeze had ceased; we lay becalmed half a mile to the south-east of the low eastern coast. Grey-coloured woods covered most of the surface, broken by streaks of yellow sand-break and pines, but the colouring was uniform and sad. The hills ran up in spires of naked rock, and the Spy-glass, the tallest, was the strangest in configuration—running up sheer and then suddenly cut off at the top like a pedestal.

The HISPANIOLA was rolling scuppers under in the swell, creaking and jumping like a manufactory; I clung to the backstay, queasy.

Whether for that or for the look of the island—grey, melancholy woods, wild stone spires, surf thundering on the steep beach—my heart sank into my boots; and from that first look, I hated the very thought of Treasure Island.

There was a dreary morning's work before us. The boats had to be got out and the ship warped round the corner into the haven behind Skeleton Island. I volunteered for one of the boats, where I had no business. The heat was sweltering; the men grumbled fiercely. The boatswain in command of my boat grumbled as loud as the worst. "Well," he said with an oath, "it's not forever." Up to that day the men had gone briskly about their business; the very sight of the island had relaxed the cords of discipline.

Long John stood by the steersman and conned the ship, knowing the passage like the palm of his hand. We brought up just where the anchor was in the chart, about a third of a mile from each shore.

The place was entirely land-locked, buried in woods, the trees coming right down to high-water mark. Two little rivers emptied into this pond; the foliage had a kind of poisonous brightness. There was not a breath of air, nor a sound but the surf booming half a mile away. A peculiar stagnant smell hung over the anchorage—of sodden leaves and rotting tree trunks. The doctor sniffed and sniffed, like one tasting a bad egg.

"I don't know about treasure," he said, "but I'll stake my wig there's fever here."

The men's conduct, alarming in the boat, became truly threatening when they came aboard. They lay about the deck growling, every order received with a black look. Mutiny hung over us like a thunder-cloud.

Long John was hard at work going from group to group, all smiles, with the cheeriest "Aye, aye, sir!" in the world; he kept up one song after another, as if to conceal the discontent of the rest. Of all the gloomy features of that gloomy afternoon, this obvious anxiety on his part appeared the worst.

We held a council in the cabin.

"Sir," said the captain, "if I risk another order, the whole ship'll come about our ears. We've only one man to rely on—Silver. He's as anxious as we to smother things up. Let's allow the men an afternoon ashore. If they all go, we'll fight the ship. If none go, we hold the cabin. If some go, mark my words, Silver'll bring 'em aboard again as mild as lambs."

It was so decided. Loaded pistols were served to the sure men; the captain went on deck and offered the crew a turn ashore. They cheered like fellows who thought they'd break their shins over treasure as soon as landed.

The captain whipped out of sight, leaving Silver to arrange the party. Silver was the captain, and a mighty rebellious crew he had of it.

Six were to stay on board; the remaining thirteen, including Silver, began to embark. Then came the first of the mad notions that contributed so much to save our lives. With six left, our party could not take the ship; with only six left, the cabin had no present need of me. In a jiffy I had slipped over the side and curled up in the fore-sheets of the nearest boat, and almost at the same moment she shoved off.

No one took notice of me, only the bow oar saying, "Is that you, Jim? Keep your head down." But Silver, from the other boat, called sharply to know if that were me; and from that moment I began to regret what I had done.

The crews raced for the beach, but my boat shot far ahead. The bow struck among the shore-side trees; I caught a branch, swung myself out, and plunged into the nearest thicket while Silver and the rest were still a hundred yards behind.

"Jim, Jim!" I heard him shouting.

But I paid no heed; jumping, ducking, breaking through, I ran straight before my nose till I could run no longer.

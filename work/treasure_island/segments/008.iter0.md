Black Dog Appears and Disappears

It was not very long after this that there occurred the first of the mysterious events that rid us at last of the captain, though not, as you will see, of his affairs. It was a bitter cold winter, and my poor father sank daily; my mother and I had all the inn upon our hands, and little heed to spare for our unpleasant guest.

One January morning, very early—a pinching, frosty morning—the cove all grey with hoar-frost and the sun still low, the captain set out down the beach, his cutlass swinging under the broad skirts of the old blue coat, his brass telescope under his arm, his hat tilted back upon his head; I remember his breath hanging like smoke in his wake as he strode off.

I was laying the breakfast-table against his return when the parlour door opened and a man stepped in on whom I had never set eyes. He was a pale, tallowy creature, wanting two fingers of the left hand; though he wore a cutlass, he did not look much like a fighter. He asked for rum; and then, motioning me to draw near, “Come here, sonny,” says he. “Come nearer here. Is this here table for my mate Bill?”

I told him I did not know his mate Bill; this was for a person who stayed in our house whom we called the captain.

“Well,” said he, “my mate Bill would be called the captain, as like as not. He has a cut on one cheek and a mighty pleasant way with him, particularly in drink, has my mate Bill. We’ll put it, for argument like, that your captain has a cut on one cheek—and we’ll put it, if you like, that that cheek’s the right one. Ah, well! I told you. Now, is my mate Bill in this here house?”

I told him he was out walking and pointed the rock and how he might return. “Ah,” said he, with an expression not at all pleasant, “this’ll be as good as drink to my mate Bill.”

He kept hanging about just inside the inn door, peering round the corner like a cat at a mouse. When I stepped into the road he called me back with an oath that made me jump; then, half fawning, half sneering, he patted me on the shoulder. “I have a son of my own,” said he, “as like you as two blocks. But the great thing for boys is discipline, sonny—discipline. Now, if you had sailed along of Bill, you wouldn’t have stood there to be spoke to twice—no, nor the way of sich as sailed with him. And here, sure enough, is my mate Bill, with a spy-glass under his arm, bless his old ’art. You and me’ll just go back into the parlour, sonny, and get behind the door, and we’ll give Bill a little surprise—bless his ’art, I say again.”

So saying, he backed me into the parlour and put me behind him by the open door. I saw he was frightened himself; he cleared the hilt of his cutlass and kept swallowing as if there were a lump in his throat.

At last in strode the captain, slammed the door, and marched straight to his breakfast.

“Bill,” said the stranger, in a voice he tried to make bold.

The captain spun on his heel; all the brown had gone out of his face and even his nose was blue. “Black Dog!” said he.

“And who else?” returned the other, getting more at his ease. “Black Dog as ever was, come for to see his old shipmate Billy, at the Admiral Benbow inn. Ah, Bill, Bill, we have seen a sight of times, us two, since I lost them two talons,” holding up his mutilated hand.

“Now, look here,” said the captain; “you’ve run me down; here I am; well, then, speak up; what is it?”

“That’s you, Bill,” returned Black Dog. “I’ll have a glass of rum from this dear child here, as I’ve took such a liking to; and we’ll sit down, if you please, and talk square, like old shipmates.”

When I returned with the rum they sat on either side the breakfast-table—Black Dog next the door, sitting sideways to have one eye on his old shipmate and one, as I thought, on his retreat. “None of your keyholes for me, sonny,” he bade me; so I left the door wide and went into the bar.

Long I heard nothing but a low gattling; then the voices rose—“No, no, no, no; and an end of it!” cried the captain once; and again, “If it comes to swinging, swing all, say I.” On a sudden there was a tremendous explosion of oaths and other noises; chair and table went over, a clash of steel, a cry of pain, and the next instant Black Dog burst through, streaming blood from the left shoulder, the captain hotly pursuing, both with drawn cutlasses. At the door the captain aimed one last tremendous cut, which would have split him to the chine had it not been intercepted by our big signboard of Admiral Benbow—you may see the notch on the lower side of the frame to this day. Once in the road, Black Dog, for all his wound, showed a wonderful clean pair of heels and was over the hill in half a minute.

The captain stared at the signboard like a bewildered man, passed his hand over his eyes, and turned back. “Jim,” says he, “rum”; and as he spoke, he reeled a little and caught himself by the wall. “I must get away from here. Rum! Rum!”

I ran to fetch it, but being all unsteadied, I broke a glass and fouled the tap; and while I was yet in my own way, there came a loud fall. We ran in and found the captain lying full length upon the floor, breathing loud and hard, his face a horrible colour. We thought he had got his death-hurt; I tried the rum, but his teeth were tight and his jaws as strong as iron. It was a happy relief when Doctor Livesey came in on his visit to my father.

“Oh, doctor,” we cried, “what shall we do? Where is he wounded?”

“Wounded? A fiddle-stick’s end!” said the doctor. “No more wounded than you or I. The man has had a stroke, as I warned him. Now, Mrs. Hawkins, upstairs to your husband, and tell him, if possible, nothing. For my part, I must do my best to save this fellow’s trebly worthless life. Jim, a basin.”

When I brought it he had ripped up the sleeve and laid bare the great sinewy arm, tattooed in several places. “Here’s luck,” “A fair wind,” and “Billy Bones his fancy,” were very neatly and clearly executed; and up near the shoulder a gallows and a man hanging from it—done, as I thought, with great spirit. “Prophetic,” said the doctor, touching it with his finger. “And now, Master Billy Bones, if that be your name, we’ll have a look at the colour of your blood. Jim, are you afraid of blood?”

“No, sir,” said I.

“Well, then, you hold the basin”; and with that he took his lancet and opened a vein. A great deal was taken before the captain opened his eyes and looked mistily about him. He frowned at the doctor; then, seeing me, looked relieved; and suddenly he cried, trying to raise himself, “Where’s Black Dog?”

“There is no Black Dog here,” said the doctor, “except what you have on your own back. You have been drinking rum; you have had a stroke, precisely as I told you; and I have just, very much against my own will, dragged you headforemost out of the grave. Now, Mr. Bones—”

“That’s not my name,” he interrupted.

“Much I care,” returned the doctor. “It’s the name of a buccaneer of my acquaintance; and I call you by it for the sake of shortness; and what I have to say to you is this: one glass of rum won’t kill you, but if you take one you’ll take another and another, and I stake my wig if you don’t break off short, you’ll die—do you understand that?—die, and go to your own place, like the man in the Bible. Come, now; I’ll help you to your bed for once.”

Between us, with much trouble, we got him upstairs and laid him down, his head falling back as if he were almost fainting. “Now, mind you,” said the doctor, “I clear my conscience—the name of rum for you is death.” And with that he took me by the arm to my father. “This is nothing,” he said as soon as the door was closed. “I have drawn blood enough to keep him quiet awhile; he should lie for a week where he is—that is the best thing for him and you; but another stroke would settle him.”

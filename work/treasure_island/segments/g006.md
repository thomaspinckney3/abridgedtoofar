## Chapter XVI

*Narrative Continued by the Doctor: How the Ship Was Abandoned*

It was about half past one—three bells in the sea phrase—that the two boats went ashore from the HISPANIOLA. The captain, the squire, and I were talking matters over in the cabin. Had there been wind, we should have fallen on the six mutineers left aboard and away to sea. But the wind was wanting; and worse, came word that Jim Hawkins had slipped ashore with the rest.

We were alarmed for his safety; with the men in the temper they were in, it seemed an even chance if we should see the lad again. We ran on deck. The pitch was bubbling in the seams; if ever a man smelt fever and dysentery, it was in that abominable anchorage. The six scoundrels grumbled under a sail in the forecastle; ashore we could see the gigs made fast, and a man in each. One of them was whistling "Lillibullero."

Waiting was a strain, and it was decided I should go ashore in the jolly-boat in quest of information.

I pulled straight in toward the stockade upon the chart. The two guarding the boats seemed in a bustle at our appearance; "Lillibullero" stopped off. Had they told Silver, all might have turned out differently; but they had their orders, and harked back again to "Lillibullero."

I jumped out and came as near running as I durst, a brace of pistols primed for safety. A hundred yards on I reached the stockade. A spring of clear water rose at the top of a knoll. On the knoll, enclosing the spring, they had clapped a stout loghouse, loopholed for musketry, and round it cleared a wide space, set with a paling six feet high without door or opening. Short of a complete surprise, the people in it might have held the place against a regiment.

What particularly took my fancy was the spring. For though we had a good place of it in the HISPANIOLA's cabin, with arms and wines, one thing was overlooked—we had no water. I was thinking this over when there came ringing over the island the cry of a man at the point of death. I was not new to violent death—I have served his Royal Highness the Duke of Cumberland, and got a wound myself at Fontenoy—but my pulse went dot and carry one. "Jim Hawkins is gone," was my first thought.

It is something to have been an old soldier, but more still to have been a doctor. There is no time to dilly-dally in our work. I returned to the shore and jumped on board the jolly-boat.

I found them all shaken. The squire sat white as a sheet, thinking of the harm he had led us to, the good soul.

"There's a man," says Captain Smollett, nodding to one of the hands, "new to this work. Another touch of the rudder and that man would join us."

We settled on the plan. We put a servant in the gallery between cabin and forecastle with loaded muskets and a mattress for protection, brought the boat round under the stern-port, and set to loading her with powder, muskets, biscuit, pork, cognac, and my invaluable medicine chest.

Meanwhile the captain hailed the coxswain on deck.

"Mr. Hands," he said, "here are two of us with a brace of pistols each. If any one of you six make a signal of any description, that man's dead."

They were taken aback, and after a consultation tumbled down the fore companion, no doubt to take us on the rear. But when they saw our man waiting with muskets in the galley, they went about ship at once, and a head popped out on deck.

"Down, dog!" cries the captain.

And the head popped back.

We touched land and tossed our stores over the palisade. Left a man to guard them; sculled back, loaded again. By the second trip the watchers had roused; "Lillibullero" was dropped, and one whipped off and disappeared. The final loading at the schooner was pork, powder, biscuit, and only a musket and cutlass apiece for the four of us. The rest of the arms we dropped overboard in two fathoms of water, where we could see the bright steel shining on the clean sandy bottom.

The tide was beginning to ebb. Voices halloaed from the direction of the gigs; it warned us to be off. Our servant retreated from his place and dropped into the boat, which we brought round to the ship's counter for Captain Smollett.

"Now, men," said he, "do you hear me?"

No answer from the forecastle.

"It's to you, Abraham Gray—it's to you I am speaking."

Still no reply.

"Gray," resumed Mr. Smollett, a little louder, "I am leaving this ship, and I order you to follow your captain. I know you are a good man at bottom. I have my watch here in my hand; I give you thirty seconds to join me in."

A pause.

"Come, my fine fellow," continued the captain; "don't hang so long in stays. I'm risking my life and the lives of these good gentlemen every second."

There was a sudden scuffle, a sound of blows, and out burst Abraham Gray with a knife cut on the cheek, and came running to the captain like a dog to the whistle.

"I'm with you, sir," said he.

The next moment he and the captain had dropped aboard, and we had shoved off and given way.

This fifth trip was different from the others. Our gallipot was gravely overloaded—five grown men and the cargo. The gunwale was lipping astern; my breeches were soaking before we had gone a hundred yards. The ebb was making, a strong current that swept us from our proper landing-place; if we let it have its way we should come ashore beside the gigs.

"I cannot keep her head for the stockade, sir," said I. "The tide keeps washing her down."

"Bear up, sir," said the captain, "until you see you're gaining."

I tried, and found I had to lay her head due east, square to our true course. "We'll never get ashore at this rate."

"If it's the only course we can lie, we must even lie it. The current must slacken, and then we can dodge back along the shore."

Suddenly the captain spoke up again, his voice a little changed.

"The gun!" said he.

"They could never get the gun ashore," said I.

"Look astern, doctor."

We had entirely forgotten the long nine; and there were the rogues busy about her, getting off her tarpaulin cover.

"Israel was Flint's gunner," said Gray hoarsely.

At any risk, we put the boat's head direct for the landing-place. But now we turned our broadside instead of our stern to the HISPANIOLA, and offered a target like a barn door. I could hear that brandy-faced rascal Hands plumping a round-shot on the deck.

"Who's the best shot?" asked the captain.

"Mr. Trelawney, out and away."

"Mr. Trelawney, will you please pick me off one of these men, sir? Hands, if possible."

Trelawney was as cool as steel. "Easy with that gun, sir, or you'll swamp the boat. All hands stand by to trim her when he aims."

The squire raised his gun, the rowing ceased, and we leaned to balance her. Hands at the muzzle was the most exposed; but just as Trelawney fired, down he stooped, and it was one of the others who fell. The cry was echoed from the shore; the rest of the pirates trooped from the trees and tumbled into their boats.

"Give way!" cried the captain. "We mustn't mind if we swamp her now."

We were close in. The one source of danger was the gun.

"Ready!" cried the squire.

"Hold!" cried the captain, quick as an echo.

He and the rowers backed with a great heave that sent her stern under water. The report fell at the same instant. The ball must have passed over our heads; the wind of it may have helped our disaster. The boat sank gently in three feet of water, leaving the captain and myself on our feet. The others came up drenched and bubbling.

No lives were lost; we could wade ashore. But our stores were at the bottom, and only two guns of five remained serviceable. Voices drew near in the woods. We waded ashore as fast as we could, leaving the poor jolly-boat and a good half of our powder and provisions.

## Chapter XVIII

*Narrative Continued by the Doctor: End of the First Day's Fighting*

We made our best speed across the wood that divided us from the stockade, the buccaneers' voices ringing nearer at every step.

"Captain," said I, "Trelawney is the dead shot. Give him your gun; his own is useless."

They exchanged guns; Trelawney, silent and cool, hung a moment to see all was fit. Observing Gray unarmed, I handed him my cutlass—our new hand was worth his salt.

Forty paces on we came to the edge of the wood. We struck the enclosure about the middle of the south side; almost at the same time seven mutineers appeared in full cry at the southwestern corner. They paused; before they recovered our four shots came in a scattering volley. One of the enemy fell, and the rest plunged into the trees.

We were rejoicing when a pistol cracked in the bush; a ball whistled past my ear, and poor Tom Redruth stumbled and fell his length on the ground. We returned the shot but had nothing to aim at.

The captain and Gray examined him; all was over. We carried him, groaning, into the log-house. He was the oldest of our party by a score of years, sullen and faithful, who had not uttered one word of complaint from the beginning of our troubles. The squire dropped on his knees, kissed his hand, and cried like a child.

"Be I going, doctor?" he asked.

"Tom, my man," said I, "you're going home."

"I wish I had had a lick at them with the gun first," he replied. After a silence he asked someone read a prayer—"It's the custom, sir," he added apologetically—and not long after passed away.

Meanwhile the captain had turned out stores from his swollen pockets—colours, Bible, rope, pen, ink, log-book, tobacco. He found a felled fir-tree, set it at the corner of the log-house, and ran up the colours with his own hand. Then he spread another flag on Tom's body.

"Don't you take on, sir," he said, shaking the squire's hand. "All's well with him; no fear for a hand shot down in his duty. It mayn't be good divinity, but it's a fact."

He pulled me aside. "Dr. Livesey, in how many weeks do you expect the consort?"

I told him months, not weeks—the end of August at earliest.

"Making large allowance for Providence, we're pretty close hauled. Powder and shot we'll do for; but rations are short—so short we're perhaps as well without that extra mouth." And he pointed to the body under the flag.

Just then, with a roar and a whistle, a round-shot passed high above the roof and plumped far beyond us in the wood.

"Oho!" said the captain. "Blaze away! You've little enough powder already, my lads."

The second trial was better; the ball descended inside the stockade, scattering sand but doing no damage.

"Captain," said the squire, "the house is invisible from the ship. It must be the flag they aim at. Would it not be wiser to take it in?"

"Strike my colours!" cried the captain. "No, sir, not I." And we all agreed with him. It was stout, seamanly good feeling, and good policy besides—it showed our enemies we despised their cannonade.

All evening they thundered away. The shot fell so high it buried itself in the sand; we had no ricochet to fear, and though one popped through the roof and out the floor, we soon minded it no more than cricket.

"There is one good thing," observed the captain; "the wood in front is likely clear. The ebb has made; our stores should be uncovered. Volunteers to bring in pork."

Two went, but the mission proved useless. The mutineers were bolder than we fancied: four or five were carrying our stores out to one of the gigs, Silver in the stern-sheets in command, every man now armed from some secret magazine.

The captain sat down to his log, and here is the beginning of the entry:

> Alexander Smollett, master; David Livesey, ship's doctor; Abraham Gray, carpenter's mate; John Trelawney, owner; and two servants—being all that is left faithful of the ship's company—with stores for ten days, came ashore this day and flew British colours on the log-house in Treasure Island. Thomas Redruth, owner's servant, shot by the mutineers; James Hawkins, cabin-boy—

And at the same time, I was wondering over poor Jim Hawkins' fate.

A hail on the land side.

"Doctor! Squire! Captain! Hullo!" came the cries.

I ran to the door in time to see Jim Hawkins, safe and sound, come climbing over the stockade.

## Chapter XIX

*Narrative Resumed by Jim Hawkins: The Garrison in the Stockade*

As soon as Ben Gunn saw the colours he stopped me by the arm and sat down.

"Now," said he, "there's your friends, sure enough."

"Far more likely the mutineers," I answered.

"That!" he cried. "Where nobody puts in but gen'lemen of fortune, Silver would fly the Jolly Roger. No, that's your friends. There's been blows, and your friends has had the best of it; and here they are in the old stockade, as was made years ago by Flint."

"All the more reason I should hurry and join them."

"Nay, mate—not you. You're on'y a boy, all told. Now, Ben Gunn is fly. Rum wouldn't bring me there—not till I see your born gen'leman and gets it on his word of honour. And you'll say this: 'Ben Gunn,' says you, 'has reasons of his own.'"

He pinched me again with the same air of cleverness.

"And when Ben Gunn is wanted, you know where to find him, Jim. Just wheer you found him today. And him that comes is to have a white thing in his hand, and he's to come alone."

He was interrupted by a loud report; a cannonball tore through the trees and pitched in the sand not a hundred yards off. We took to our heels in different directions.

For a good hour reports shook the island, and balls kept crashing through the woods. I moved from hiding-place to hiding-place, pursued, or so it seemed, by these terrifying missiles. Towards the end of the bombardment, after a long detour east, I crept down among the shore-side trees.

The sun had just set; the sea breeze ruffled the grey surface of the anchorage. The HISPANIOLA still lay at anchor, but flying from her peak now was the Jolly Roger—the black flag of piracy. As I looked, the last red flash came, and the last round-shot whistled through the air.

Men were demolishing the poor jolly-boat with axes on the beach. Near the river-mouth a great fire glowed among the trees; one of the gigs went back and forth between that point and the ship, the men shouting at the oars like children. There was a sound in their voices that suggested rum.

I was on the low, sandy spit east of the anchorage. Further down I saw a peculiarly white isolated rock, and thought this might be the one Ben Gunn had spoken of: some day a boat might be wanted.

Then I skirted the woods to the rear of the stockade, and was warmly welcomed.

I had soon told my story and began to look about. The log-house was unsquared pine trunks—roof, walls, and floor. Under a porch at the door the spring welled up into a great ship's kettle of iron, sunk among the sand. A stone slab for a hearth, an old rusty iron basket for the fire.

The cold evening breeze whistled through every chink and sprinkled the floor with a continual rain of fine sand. There was sand in our eyes, sand in our teeth, sand in our suppers, sand dancing in the spring at the bottom of the kettle, for all the world like porridge beginning to boil. Our chimney was a hole in the roof; little smoke found its way out, the rest eddied about and kept us coughing and piping the eye.

Add to this Gray's face tied up in a bandage from his break with the mutineers, and poor old Tom Redruth, still unburied, lying along the wall under the Union Jack.

Captain Smollett was never the man to let us sit idle. He divided us into watches, sent men for firewood and to dig Redruth's grave, named the doctor cook, put me sentry, and went from one to another keeping up our spirits.

From time to time the doctor came to the door for air, and had a word for me.

"That man Smollett," he said once, "is a better man than I am. And when I say that it means a deal, Jim."

Another time he was silent, then looked at me.

"Is this Ben Gunn a man?"

"I am not sure he's sane."

"If there's any doubt about the matter, he is," returned the doctor. "A man who has been three years biting his nails on a desert island, Jim, can't expect to appear as sane as you or me. It doesn't lie in human nature."

Before supper we buried old Tom in the sand bare-headed in the breeze. Then, after pork and a stiff brandy grog, the three chiefs took counsel.

They were at their wits' end—the stores so low we must have been starved before help came. Our best hope was to kill off the buccaneers till they hauled down their flag or ran with the HISPANIOLA. From nineteen they were reduced to fifteen, two wounded, one severely. We had two allies besides—rum and the climate. We could hear them roaring and singing late into the night; the doctor staked his wig that half, camped in the marsh without remedies, would be on their backs in a week.

"So," he added, "if we are not all shot down they'll be glad to pack in the schooner. It's a ship; they can get to buccaneering again."

"First ship that ever I lost," said Captain Smollett.

I was dead tired and slept like a log. The rest were up and breakfasted when I was wakened by a bustle and voices.

"Flag of truce!" I heard someone say; and then, with a cry of surprise, "Silver himself!"

Up I jumped, rubbed my eyes, and ran to a loophole in the wall.

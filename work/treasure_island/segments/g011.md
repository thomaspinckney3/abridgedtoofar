## Chapter XXX — On Parole

I was wakened by a clear, hearty voice hailing us from the margin of the wood: "Block house, ahoy! Here's the doctor."

And the doctor it was. Glad as I was, my gladness was not without admixture. I remembered my insubordinate and stealthy conduct, and felt ashamed to look him in the face.

"You, doctor! Top o' the morning to you, sir!" cried Silver, broad awake and beaming with good nature in a moment. "We've quite a surprise for you, sir — a noo boarder and lodger, looking fit and taut as a fiddle; slep' like a supercargo, right alongside of John."

I could hear the alteration in the doctor's voice as he said, "Not Jim?"

"The very same Jim as ever was," says Silver.

The doctor stopped outright. "Well, well," he said at last, "duty first and pleasure afterwards, as you might have said yourself, Silver. Let us overhaul these patients of yours."

He entered with one grim nod to me and proceeded among the sick, rattling on as if paying an ordinary professional visit in a quiet English family. His manner reacted on the men, who behaved as if he were still ship's doctor and they faithful hands before the mast.

"Because, you see, since I am mutineers' doctor, or prison doctor as I prefer to call it," says he, "I make it a point of honour not to lose a man for King George (God bless him!) and the gallows."

The rogues swallowed the home-thrust in silence. He dosed them round, and they took his prescriptions with really laughable humility, more like charity schoolchildren than blood-guilty mutineers.

"Well, that's done for today. And now I should wish to have a talk with that boy, please."

A mutineer at the door swung round with a deep flush and cried "No!" and swore.

Silver struck the barrel with his open hand. "Si-lence!" he roared, looking about him positively like a lion. "Hawkins, will you give me your word of honour as a young gentleman not to slip your cable?"

I readily gave the pledge.

"Then, doctor, you just step outside o' that stockade, and I'll bring the boy down on the inside, and I reckon you can yarn through the spars."

The explosion of disapproval that broke out as the doctor left accused Silver of playing double — the identical thing he was doing. But he called them all the fools and dolts you can imagine, fluttered the chart in their faces, asked if they could afford to break the treaty the very day they were bound a-treasure-hunting.

"No, by thunder!" he cried. "It's us must break the treaty when the time comes; and till then I'll gammon that doctor, if I have to ile his boots with brandy."

Then, hand on my shoulder, he stalked out upon his crutch. "Slow, lad, slow," he said. "They might round upon us in a twinkle of an eye if we was seen to hurry."

When we reached the stockade, Silver stopped. "You'll make a note of this, doctor. When a man's steering as near the wind as me, you wouldn't think it too much to give him one good word? It's not my life only now — it's that boy's into the bargain."

Silver was a changed man now his back was to his friends; his cheeks seemed fallen in, his voice trembled. Never was a soul more dead in earnest. He stepped back out of earshot and sat upon a tree-stump, whistling, spinning round to keep both me and his ruffians in view.

"So, Jim," said the doctor sadly, "here you are. As you have brewed, so shall you drink. When Captain Smollett was well, you dared not have gone off; and when he was ill and couldn't help it, by George, it was downright cowardly!"

I began to weep. "Doctor, I have blamed myself enough; my life's forfeit anyway, and I should have been dead but for Silver. What I fear is torture. If they come to torture me—"

"Jim," the doctor interrupted, his voice quite changed, "whip over, and we'll run for it."

"Doctor, I passed my word."

"I know, I know. I'll take it on my shoulders, holus bolus. Jump! One jump, and we'll run for it like antelopes."

"No," I replied; "you know right well you wouldn't do the thing yourself. Silver trusted me; I passed my word, and back I go. But, doctor — if they torture me, I might let slip where the ship is. I got the ship; she lies in North Inlet, on the southern beach, and just below high water."

"The ship!" exclaimed the doctor.

I described my adventures, and he heard me out in silence. "There is a kind of fate in this. Every step, it's you that saves our lives; do you suppose we are going to let you lose yours? You found the plot; you found Ben Gunn. Silver!" he cried as the cook drew near. "Don't be in any great hurry after that treasure."

"Why, sir, I can only save my life and the boy's by seeking for that treasure."

"Well, Silver — if that is so, I'll go one step further: look out for squalls when you find it."

"Sir, as between man and man, that's too much and too little. What you're after, why you left the block house, why you given me that there chart, I don't know. If you won't tell me plain out, just say so and I'll leave the helm."

"I've no right to say more; it's not my secret. But if we both get alive out of this wolf-trap, I'll do my best to save you, short of perjury. Keep the boy close beside you, and when you need help, halloo. I'm off to seek it for you. Good-bye, Jim."

Dr. Livesey shook hands with me through the stockade, nodded to Silver, and set off at a brisk pace into the wood.

## Chapter XXXI — The Treasure-hunt—Flint's Pointer

"Jim," said Silver, "if I saved your life, you saved mine; and I'll not forget it. I seen the doctor waving you to run for it, and seen you say no, as plain as hearing. Jim, that's one to you. This is the first glint of hope I had since the attack failed. Now, you and me must stick close, back to back like, and we'll save our necks in spite o' fate and fortune."

A man hailed us that breakfast was ready. They had lit a fire fit to roast an ox, cooked three times more than we could eat, and one of them, with an empty laugh, threw what was left into the flames. I never saw men so careless of the morrow; bold enough for a brush, I could see their entire unfitness for prolonged campaign.

Even Silver, eating away with Captain Flint upon his shoulder, had not a word of blame, though I thought he had never shown himself so cunning.

"Aye, mates," said he, "it's lucky you have Barbecue to think for you with this here head. Sure enough, they have the ship. Once we hit the treasure, we'll find out where; and then, mates, us that has the boats has the upper hand. As for hostage, we'll keep him like so much gold, in case of accidents. Once we got the ship and treasure both, and off to sea like jolly companions, then we'll talk Mr. Hawkins over, and give him his share for all his kindness."

For my part, I was horribly cast down. Silver, already doubly a traitor, would not hesitate; he had still a foot in either camp, and would prefer wealth with the pirates to a bare escape from hanging. Even if he kept faith with the doctor, what a moment when his followers' suspicion turned to certainty and he and I — a cripple and a boy — must fight for our lives against five seamen!

We made a curious figure, all in soiled sailor clothes and all but me armed to the teeth — Silver with two guns, the great cutlass, and a pistol in each pocket, Captain Flint gabbling on his shoulder. I had a line about my waist and followed after the sea-cook like a dancing bear. The others carried picks and shovels, or pork and bread and brandy from our stock; for had Silver not bargained with the doctor, his mutineers, deserted by the ship, must have been driven to clear water and the proceeds of their hunting.

We pulled over the anchorage in two gigs, and as we went there was some discussion of the chart. The note ran thus:

> Tall tree, Spy-glass shoulder, bearing a point to the N. of N.N.E.
> Skeleton Island E.S.E. and by E.
> Ten feet.

A tall tree was the principal mark. The anchorage was bounded by a plateau dotted thickly with pines, here and there one rising clear above its neighbours. Which was Flint's tree could only be decided on the spot. Every man had picked a favourite ere we were half-way over, Long John alone shrugging his shoulders and bidding them wait.

Landing at the mouth of a wooded river, we began to ascend. Heavy, miry ground delayed us; but by little and little the hill steepened, and the wood changed character — heavy-scented broom and flowering shrubs taking the place of grass, thickets of green nutmeg dotted with the red columns and broad shadow of pines. The air was fresh and stirring.

The party spread fan-shape, shouting and leaping. Silver and I followed in the centre, well behind — I tethered by my rope, he ploughing among the sliding gravel, sometimes needing my hand.

We had proceeded half a mile when a man on the farthest left began to cry aloud, as in terror. The others ran in his direction.

It was not the treasure. At the foot of a pretty big pine, involved in a creeper, lay a human skeleton with a few shreds of clothing. A chill struck to every heart.

"He was a seaman," said one, examining the rags. "This is good sea-cloth."

"Aye, like enough," said Silver. "But what sort of a way is that for bones to lie? 'Tain't in natur'."

On a second glance, it seemed impossible to fancy the body in a natural position. The man lay perfectly straight — his feet pointing one way, his hands raised above his head like a diver's, the opposite.

"I've taken a notion into my old numbskull," observed Silver. "Here's the compass; there's the tip-top p'int o' Skeleton Island, stickin' out like a tooth. Just take a bearing along the line of them bones."

It was done. The body pointed straight in the direction of the island, and the compass read duly E.S.E. and by E.

"I thought so," cried the cook. "This here is a p'inter. But, by thunder! If it don't make me cold inside to think of Flint. This is one of HIS jokes, and no mistake. Him and six was alone here; he killed 'em every man, and laid this one down by compass, shiver my timbers!"

"There ain't a thing left here," said another, feeling round; "not a copper doit nor a baccy box. It don't look nat'ral."

"No, by gum, it don't," agreed Silver. "Messmates, but if Flint was living, this would be a hot spot for you and me. Six they were, and six are we; and bones is what they are now."

"Dead and gone below," said the fellow with the bandage; "but if ever sperrit walked, it would be Flint's. Dear heart, but he died bad, did Flint!"

"Come, come," said Silver; "stow this talk. He's dead, and he don't walk. Care killed a cat. Fetch ahead for the doubloons."

We started; but in spite of the staring daylight, the pirates no longer ran separate and shouting, but kept side by side and spoke with bated breath. The terror of the dead buccaneer had fallen on their spirits.

## Chapter XXXII — The Treasure-hunt—The Voice Among the Trees

Partly from the alarm, partly to rest Silver and the sick, we sat down on the brow. The plateau commanded a wide prospect: the Cape of the Woods fringed with surf before us; behind, the anchorage and Skeleton Island, and open sea on the east. Sheer above rose the Spy-glass.

Silver took bearings with his compass. "There are three 'tall trees,'" said he, "about in the right line from Skeleton Island. It's child's play to find the stuff now."

The men muttered of Flint, of his blue face, of the rum that had taken him. All of a sudden, out of the trees, a thin, high, trembling voice struck up the well-known air:

> "Fifteen men on the dead man's chest—
> Yo-ho-ho, and a bottle of rum!"

I never have seen men more dreadfully affected. The colour went from their faces like enchantment; some leaped to their feet, some clawed hold of others; one grovelled on the ground.

"It's Flint, by ——!" cried a mutineer.

The song stopped as suddenly as it began, broken off as if a hand had been laid upon the singer's mouth. It had sounded airily and sweetly to me; the effect on my companions was the stranger.

"Come," said Silver, struggling with his ashen lips. "This won't do. Stand by to go about. It's someone skylarking — someone that's flesh and blood, and you may lay to that."

His courage came back as he spoke. The others had begun to lend an ear when the same voice broke out again — a faint distant hail that echoed fainter among the clefts of the Spy-glass.

"Darby M'Graw!" it wailed. "Darby M'Graw! Darby M'Graw!" again and again; and then, with an oath I leave out: "Fetch aft the rum, Darby!"

The buccaneers remained rooted, eyes starting from their heads.

"That fixes it!" gasped one. "Let's go."

"They was his last words," moaned another, "his last words above board."

Still Silver was unconquered. "Nobody in this here island ever heard of Darby," he muttered. "Shipmates, I'll not be beat by man or devil. I never was feared of Flint alive, and, by the powers, I'll face him dead. There's seven hundred thousand pound not a quarter of a mile from here. When did ever a gentleman o' fortune show his stern to that much dollars for a boozy old seaman with a blue mug — and him dead too?"

But there was no sign of reawakening courage; rather, growing terror at the irreverence of his words.

"Belay there, John!" said one. "Don't you cross a sperrit."

The rest were too terrified to reply; fear kept them close by John, as if his daring helped them. He had pretty well fought his weakness down.

"Sperrit? Well, maybe," he said. "But there's one thing not clear to me. There was an echo. Now, no man ever seen a sperrit with a shadow; what's he doing with an echo? That ain't in natur', surely?"

This argument seemed weak enough to me. But to my wonder, the men were greatly relieved.

"You've a head upon your shoulders, John, and no mistake. 'Bout ship, mates! It was like Flint's voice, but not just so clear-away. It was liker somebody else's voice—"

"By the powers, Ben Gunn!" roared Silver.

"Aye, and so it were!" cried another. "Ben Gunn it were!"

"Why, nobody minds Ben Gunn," cried a third; "dead or alive, nobody minds him."

It was extraordinary how their spirits returned. They shouldered the tools and set forth, walking by Silver's compass. He had said the truth: dead or alive, nobody minded Ben Gunn.

Striking near north-west, the first tall tree by the bearings proved wrong. So with the second. The third rose nearly two hundred feet above a clump of underwood — a giant of a vegetable, with a red column as big as a cottage, and a wide shadow around.

But it was not its size that now impressed my companions; it was the knowledge that seven hundred thousand pounds in gold lay buried below its shadow. The thought swallowed up their terrors. Their eyes burned; their feet grew speedier; their whole soul was bound up in that fortune.

Silver hobbled, grunting, on his crutch; his nostrils quivered; he plucked furiously at the line and turned his eyes upon me with a deadly look. I read his thoughts like print. His promise and the doctor's warning were things of the past; he hoped to seize the treasure, board the Hispaniola by night, cut every honest throat on the island, and sail away laden with crimes and riches.

Shaken with these alarms, it was hard to keep up. Now and again I stumbled, and Silver plucked roughly at the rope. I was haunted by the thought of the tragedy once acted on that plateau, when the buccaneer with the blue face had cut down his six accomplices with his own hand.

We were now at the margin of the thicket.

"Huzza, mates, all together!" shouted one in the lead; and the foremost broke into a run.

And suddenly, not ten yards further, we beheld them stop. A low cry arose. Silver doubled his pace, digging away like one possessed; next moment he and I had come also to a dead halt.

Before us was a great excavation, the sides fallen in and grass sprouted on the bottom. In it were the shaft of a broken pick and the boards of several packing-cases strewn around. On one I saw, branded with a hot iron, the name WALRUS — the name of Flint's ship.

All was clear to probation. The cache had been found and rifled; the seven hundred thousand pounds were gone!

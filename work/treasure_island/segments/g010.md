## Chapter XXVIII

In the Enemy's Camp

The red glare of the torch showed me the worst of my apprehensions realized. The pirates were in possession of the block house and stores: the cask of cognac, the pork and bread, and—what tenfold increased my horror—not a sign of any prisoner. I could only judge that all had perished, and my heart smote me sorely that I had not been there to perish with them.

There were six buccaneers, all told. Five were on their feet, flushed and swollen with drink suddenly broken. The sixth had only risen upon his elbow, deadly pale, a blood-stained bandage round his head; I doubted not he was the man shot in the great attack. The parrot sat preening on Long John's shoulder. Silver himself looked paler and sterner than I was used to, his fine broadcloth bitterly the worse for wear, daubed with clay and torn with briers.

"So," said he, "here's Jim Hawkins, shiver my timbers! Dropped in, like, eh? Well, come, I take that friendly."

He sat down across the brandy cask and began to fill a pipe.

"Now, you see, Jim, so be as you ARE here," says he, "I'll give you a piece of my mind. I've always liked you, for a lad of spirit, and the picter of my own self when I was young and handsome. I always wanted you to jine and take your share, and die a gentleman, and now, my cock, you've got to. Cap'n Smollett's a fine seaman, but stiff on discipline. The doctor himself is gone dead again' you—'ungrateful scamp' was what he said. You can't go back to your own lot, for they won't have you; and without you start a third ship's company all by yourself, you'll have to jine with Cap'n Silver."

So far so good. My friends, then, were still alive; and though I half believed Silver's claim that they were incensed at my desertion, I was more relieved than distressed.

"I'm all for argyment," he continued; "I never seen good come o' threatening. If you like the service, you'll jine; and if you don't, Jim, why, you're free to answer no—free and welcome, shipmate."

"Am I to answer, then?" I asked, my voice tremulous. Through all this sneering talk I felt the threat of death overhung me.

"Lad, no one's a-pressing of you. Time goes so pleasant in your company, you see."

"Well," said I, growing bolder, "if I'm to choose, I have a right to know what's what, and where my friends are."

Silver told me how Doctor Livesey had come down with a flag of truce that morning to find the schooner gone from the bay; how a bargain had been struck for stores and block house; how the doctor's party had tramped off to where Silver did not know.

"And lest you take it into your head you was included in the treaty," he added, "here's the last word the doctor said: 'As for that boy, I don't know where he is, confound him, nor I don't much care. We're about sick of him.' These was his words."

"And now I am to choose?"

"And now you are to choose, and you may lay to that."

"Well," said I, by this time quite excited, "I am not such a fool but I know what I have to look for. Let the worst come to the worst, it's little I care. I've seen too many die since I fell in with you. But there's a thing or two I have to tell you. Here you are, in a bad way—ship lost, treasure lost, men lost; and if you want to know who did it—it was I! I was in the apple barrel the night we sighted land, and I heard you, John, and Dick, and Hands, who is now at the bottom of the sea, and told every word you said before the hour was out. As for the schooner, it was I who cut her cable, and I that killed the men you had aboard, and it was I who brought her where you'll never see her more, not one of you. The laugh's on my side; I no more fear you than I fear a fly. Kill me, if you please, or spare me. But if you spare me, when you fellows are in court for piracy, I'll save you all I can. It is for you to choose. Kill another and do yourselves no good, or spare me and keep a witness to save you from the gallows."

I stopped, out of breath, and to my wonder not a man of them moved, but all sat staring at me like as many sheep.

"And now, Mr. Silver," I said, "I believe you're the best man here, and if things go to the worst, I'll take it kind of you to let the doctor know the way I took it."

"I'll bear it in mind," said Silver, with an accent so curious I could not decide whether he were laughing at my request or favourably affected by my courage.

A mahogany-faced seaman called out that I was the boy who had known Black Dog; Silver added that I was the one who had faked the chart from Billy Bones. "First and last, we've split upon Jim Hawkins!"

"Then here goes!" the seaman cried, springing up with his knife.

"Avast, there!" roared Silver. "Cross me, and you'll go where many a good man's gone before you—some to the yard-arm, and some by the board, and all to feed the fishes. There's never a man looked me between the eyes and seen a good day a'terwards." A hoarse murmur rose from the others.

"Did any of you gentlemen want to have it out with ME?" he bellowed, bending forward from the keg, pipe still glowing. "Take a cutlass, him that dares, and I'll see the colour of his inside, crutch and all, before that pipe's empty."

Not a man stirred.

"I'm cap'n here by 'lection. I'm cap'n here because I'm the best man by a long sea-mile. You won't fight, as gentlemen o' fortune should; then, by thunder, you'll obey. I like that boy; he's more a man than any pair of rats of you in this here house. Let me see him that'll lay a hand on him."

There was a long pause. I stood straight against the wall, my heart going like a sledge-hammer, but with a ray of hope shining now. Silver leant back, arms crossed, calm as though he had been in church—yet his eye kept wandering, the tail of it on his unruly followers. They drew gradually together at the far end of the block house, the low hiss of their whispering sounding in my ear like a stream. Presently a long, ill-looking, yellow-eyed man stepped forward with an elaborate sea-salute and claimed his right to a council outside. One after another the rest followed, each making salute as he passed, and so all marched out and left Silver and me alone with the torch.

The sea-cook instantly removed his pipe.

"Now, look you here, Jim Hawkins," he said in a steady whisper, "you're within half a plank of death, and what's a long sight worse, of torture. They're going to throw me off. But you mark, I stand by you through thick and thin. I didn't mean to; not till you spoke up. But I see you was the right sort. Back to back, says I. You save your witness, and he'll save your neck!"

I began dimly to understand. "You mean all's lost?"

"Aye, by gum, I do! Ship gone, neck gone—that's the size of it. Once I looked into that bay, Jim Hawkins, and seen no schooner—well, I'm tough, but I gave out. I'll save your life, if so be as I can, from them. But, see here, Jim—tit for tat—you save Long John from swinging."

"What I can do, that I'll do," I said.

"It's a bargain!"

He hobbled to the torch and lit his pipe afresh. "I'm on squire's side now. I know you've got that ship safe somewheres. How you done it, I don't know, but safe it is. I ask no questions, nor I won't let others. Ah, you that's young—you and me might have done a power of good together!"

He drew some cognac into a tin cannikin. "And talking o' trouble, why did that doctor give me the chart, Jim?"

My face expressed a wonder so unaffected he saw the needlessness of further questions.

"Ah, well, he did, though. And there's something under that, no doubt—something, surely, under that, Jim—bad or good." He took another swallow, shaking his great fair head like a man who looks forward to the worst.

## Chapter XXIX

The Black Spot Again

The council had lasted some time when one of them re-entered, with a repetition of the same ironical salute, and begged a moment's loan of the torch. Silver agreed, and the man retired, leaving us in the dark.

"There's a breeze coming, Jim," said Silver, friendly and familiar now.

I turned to the loophole. The embers of the great fire had burned themselves so low that I understood why these conspirators desired a torch. About half-way down the slope they were collected in a group; one held the light, another knelt in their midst with the blade of an open knife shining in his hand, and a book as well. The kneeling figure rose, and the whole party began moving toward the house.

"Here they come," said I.

"Let 'em come, lad—let 'em come," said Silver cheerily. "I've still a shot in my locker."

The five pushed one of their number forward, who held his closed right hand before him.

"Step up, lad," cried Silver. "I won't eat you. Hand it over, lubber. I know the rules; I won't hurt a depytation."

The buccaneer stepped up, passed something to Silver, and slipped back to his companions.

"The black spot! I thought so," Silver observed. "Why, hillo! You've gone and cut this out of a Bible. What fool's cut a Bible?"

"It was Dick," said one.

"Dick, was it? Then Dick can get to prayers. He's seen his slice of luck, has Dick, and you may lay to that."

But here the yellow-eyed man cut in. "Belay that talk, John Silver. This crew has tipped you the black spot in full council, as in dooty bound; just you turn it over and see what's wrote there. Then you can talk."

"Thanky, George. Ah! 'Deposed'—that's it, is it? Very pretty wrote, like print, I swear. Your hand o' write, George? Why, you'll be cap'n next, I shouldn't wonder."

"Come, now," said George, "you don't fool this crew no more. You're over now, and you'll maybe step down off that barrel and help vote."

"I thought you said you knowed the rules," returned Silver contemptuously. "I'm still your cap'n, mind, till you outs with your grievances and I reply. In the meantime, your black spot ain't worth a biscuit."

"Oh," replied George, "WE'RE all square. First, you've made a hash of this cruise. Second, you let the enemy out o' this trap for nothing. Third, you wouldn't let us go at them upon the march. And fourth, there's this here boy."

"Is that all?" asked Silver quietly.

"Enough, too. We'll all swing for your bungling."

"Well now, I'll answer these four p'ints. I made a hash o' this cruise? You all know what I wanted, and you all know if that had been done we'd 'a been aboard the HISPANIOLA this night as ever was, every man alive, and the treasure in the hold of her, by thunder! Who crossed me? Why, it was Anderson, and Hands, and you, George Merry! And you're the last above board of that meddling crew, and you have the Davy Jones's insolence to up and stand for cap'n over me—you, that sank the lot of us!"

George wiped the sweat from his brow.

"Why, I give you my word, I'm sick to speak to you. We're that near the gibbet that my neck's stiff with thinking on it. You've seen 'em, hanged in chains, birds about 'em—'Who's that?' says one. 'That's John Silver. I knowed him well,' says another. Now, that's where we are, every mother's son of us, thanks to him, and Hands, and other ruination fools of you. And if you want to know about number four, and that boy—isn't he a hostage? Are we to waste a hostage? He might be our last chance. As for why I made a bargain—well, you came crawling on your knees to me to make it; you'd have starved too if I hadn't. But that's a trifle. You look there—that's why!"

And he cast down upon the floor a paper that I instantly recognized—the chart on yellow paper, with the three red crosses, that I had found in the oilcloth at the bottom of the captain's chest. Why the doctor had given it to him was more than I could fancy.

The surviving mutineers leaped on it like cats on a mouse. It went from hand to hand, with oaths and cries and childish laughter; you would have thought, not only they fingered the very gold, but were at sea with it, besides, in safety.

"Mighty pretty," said George. "But how are we to get away with it, and us no ship?"

Silver sprang up, supporting himself with a hand against the wall. "Now I give you warning, George! One more word of your sauce, and I'll call you down and fight you. You and the rest, that lost me my schooner with your interference, burn you. Civil you can speak, and shall."

"That's fair enow," said the old man Morgan.

"Fair! I reckon so. You lost the ship; I found the treasure. Who's the better man? And now I resign, by thunder! Elect whom you please to be your cap'n now; I'm done with it."

"Silver!" they cried. "Barbecue forever! Barbecue for cap'n!"

"So that's the toon, is it? George, I reckon you'll have to wait another turn, friend; lucky for you I'm not a revengeful man. And now, shipmates, this black spot? 'Tain't much good now, is it? Dick's crossed his luck and spoiled his Bible, and that's about all."

"It'll do to kiss the book on still, won't it?" growled Dick, evidently uneasy at the curse he had brought upon himself.

"A Bible with a bit cut out!" returned Silver derisively. "Not it. It don't bind no more'n a ballad-book."

"Here, Jim—here's a cur'osity for you," said Silver, and he tossed me the paper.

It was about the size of a crown piece. One side was blank; the other contained a verse or two of Revelation—these words among the rest, which struck sharply home upon my mind: "Without are dogs and murderers." The printed side had been blackened with wood ash, which already began to come off and soil my fingers; on the blank side had been written with the same material the one word "Depposed." I have that curiosity beside me at this moment, but not a trace of writing now remains beyond a single scratch, such as a man might make with his thumb-nail.

That was the end of the night's business. Soon after, with a drink all round, we lay down to sleep, and the outside of Silver's vengeance was to put George Merry up for sentinel and threaten him with death if he should prove unfaithful.

It was long ere I could close an eye, and heaven knows I had matter enough for thought in the man whom I had slain that afternoon, in my own most perilous position, and above all, in the remarkable game that I saw Silver now engaged upon—keeping the mutineers together with one hand and grasping with the other after every means, possible and impossible, to make his peace and save his miserable life. He himself slept peacefully and snored aloud, yet my heart was sore for him, wicked as he was, to think on the dark perils that environed him and the shameful gibbet that awaited him.

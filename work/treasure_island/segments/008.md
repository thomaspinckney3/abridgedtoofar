It was not long after this that there occurred the first of the mysterious events that rid us at last of the captain, though not of his affairs. It was a bitter cold winter, and my poor father was little likely to see the spring.

One January morning, very early—a pinching, frosty morning—the captain had risen earlier than usual and set out down the beach, his cutlass swinging under the broad skirts of his old blue coat, his brass telescope under his arm. I was laying the breakfast-table when the parlour door opened and a man stepped in on whom I had never set my eyes before. He was a pale, tallowy creature, wanting two fingers of the left hand, and though he wore a cutlass, he did not look much like a fighter.

I asked him what was for his service, and he said he would take rum; but as I was going out, he motioned me to draw near.

"Come here, sonny," says he. "Is this here table for my mate Bill?"

I told him I did not know his mate Bill, and this was for a person we called the captain.

"Well," said he, "my mate Bill would be called the captain, as like as not. He has a cut on one cheek and a mighty pleasant way with him, particularly in drink. Now, is my mate Bill in this here house?"

I told him he was out walking, and pointed out the way he was likely to return.

"Ah," said he, "this'll be as good as drink to my mate Bill."

The expression of his face was not pleasant. The stranger kept hanging about just inside the door, peering round the corner like a cat waiting for a mouse. Once I stepped out into the road, but he called me back with an oath that made me jump. Then he returned to his fawning manner, patted me on the shoulder, and told me I was a good boy. "And here, sure enough, is my mate Bill, with a spy-glass under his arm. You and me'll just go back into the parlour, sonny, and get behind the door, and we'll give Bill a little surprise."

So saying, he backed with me into the parlour and put me behind him in the corner so that we were both hidden by the open door. The stranger was certainly frightened himself; he loosened the blade in his sheath and kept swallowing.

At last in strode the captain, slammed the door behind him, and marched straight across the room to where his breakfast awaited him.

"Bill," said the stranger.

The captain spun round on his heel; all the brown had gone out of his face, and even his nose was blue; he had the look of a man who sees a ghost.

"Come, Bill, you know me; you know an old shipmate, Bill, surely," said the stranger.

The captain made a sort of gasp.

"Black Dog!" said he.

"And who else?" returned the other. "Black Dog as ever was, come for to see his old shipmate Billy, at the Admiral Benbow inn."

"Now, look here," said the captain; "you've run me down; here I am; well, then, speak up; what is it?"

"That's you, Bill," returned Black Dog. "I'll have a glass of rum, and we'll sit down and talk square, like old shipmates."

When I returned with the rum, they were already seated on either side of the table, Black Dog next the door so as to have an eye on his retreat. He bade me leave the door wide open and retire. For a long time I could hear nothing but a low gattling; but at last the voices began to grow higher.

"No, no, no, no; and an end of it!" cried the captain once. And again, "If it comes to swinging, swing all, say I."

Then all of a sudden there was a tremendous explosion of oaths—the chair and table went over, a clash of steel followed, and a cry of pain, and the next instant I saw Black Dog in full flight, and the captain pursuing, both with drawn cutlasses, the former streaming blood from the shoulder. Just at the door the captain aimed one last tremendous cut, which would certainly have split him to the chine had it not been intercepted by our big signboard of Admiral Benbow. You may see the notch on the lower side of the frame to this day.

That blow was the last of the battle. Black Dog showed a wonderful clean pair of heels and disappeared over the edge of the hill in half a minute. The captain stood staring at the signboard like a bewildered man, then passed his hand over his eyes and turned back into the house.

"Jim," says he, "rum"; and as he spoke, he reeled, and caught himself with one hand against the wall.

"Are you hurt?" cried I.

"Rum," he repeated. "I must get away from here. Rum! Rum!"

I ran to fetch it, but I was unsteadied, and while I was still getting in my own way, I heard a loud fall in the parlour, and running in, beheld the captain lying full length upon the floor. My mother came running downstairs. He was breathing very loud and hard, his face a horrible colour.

It was a happy relief when the door opened and Doctor Livesey came in, on his visit to my father.

"Oh, doctor," we cried, "where is he wounded?"

"Wounded? A fiddle-stick's end!" said the doctor. "No more wounded than you or I. The man has had a stroke, as I warned him. Now, Mrs. Hawkins, just you run upstairs to your husband and tell him, if possible, nothing about it. For my part, I must do my best to save this fellow's trebly worthless life; Jim, you get me a basin."

When I got back, the doctor had ripped up the captain's sleeve and exposed his great sinewy arm, tattooed in several places. "Here's luck," "A fair wind," and "Billy Bones his fancy," were neatly executed on the forearm; and up near the shoulder there was a sketch of a gallows and a man hanging from it.

"Prophetic," said the doctor. He took his lancet and opened a vein.

A great deal of blood was taken before the captain opened his eyes. He recognized the doctor with a frown, then looked at me with relief; but suddenly his colour changed, and he tried to raise himself, crying, "Where's Black Dog?"

"There is no Black Dog here," said the doctor, "except what you have on your own back. You have been drinking rum; you have had a stroke, and I have just dragged you headforemost out of the grave. Now, Mr. Bones—"

"That's not my name," he interrupted.

"Much I care," returned the doctor. "It's the name of a buccaneer of my acquaintance; and what I have to say is this: one glass of rum won't kill you, but if you take one you'll take another, and I stake my wig if you don't break off short, you'll die—do you understand that?—die, and go to your own place, like the man in the Bible."

Between us we hoisted him upstairs and laid him on his bed.

"Now, mind you," said the doctor, "I clear my conscience—the name of rum for you is death." And he took me aside. "I have drawn blood enough to keep him quiet awhile; he should lie for a week where he is; but another stroke would settle him."

The Old Sea-dog at the Admiral Benbow

Squire Trelawney, Dr. Livesey, and the rest of these gentlemen having asked me to write down the whole particulars about Treasure Island, from the beginning to the end, keeping nothing back but the bearings of the island, and that only because there is still treasure not yet lifted, I take up my pen in the year of grace 17—, and go back to the time when my father kept the Admiral Benbow inn and the brown old seaman with the sabre cut first took up his lodging under our roof.

I remember him, as if it were yesterday, plodding to the door with his sea-chest on a hand-barrow—a tall, strong, heavy, nut-brown man, tarry pigtail over a soiled blue coat, hands ragged and scarred, black broken nails, and a dirty, livid sabre cut across one cheek. He looked round the cove, whistled, and broke out in the old sea-song he sang so often afterwards:

“Fifteen men on the dead man’s chest--
   Yo-ho-ho, and a bottle of rum!”

in the high, old tottering voice that seemed tuned and broken at the capstan bars. He rapped with a stick like a handspike, called roughly for rum, and, having tasted it like a connoisseur and eyed our signboard, said, “This is a handy cove, and a pleasant sittyated grog-shop. Much company, mate?” On being told “very little,” he answered, “Well, then, this is the berth for me. Here you, matey—bring up alongside and help up my chest. I’m a plain man; rum and bacon and eggs is what I want, and that head up there for to watch ships off. What you mought call me? You mought call me captain.” And he threw down three or four gold pieces: “You can tell me when I’ve worked through that,” says he, looking as fierce as a commander.

Ragged as he was and coarse in speech, he had the air of a mate or skipper accustomed to be obeyed or to strike. The barrow-man told us the mail had set him down at the Royal George; he had asked for lonely inns and chosen ours. That was all we learned of him.

By custom he was very silent. All day he hung about the cove or the cliffs with a brass telescope; all evening he sat by the parlour fire and drank rum and water very strong. When spoken to, he would only look up sudden and fierce and blow through his nose like a fog-horn, so we soon learned to let him be. Each day, returning from his stroll, he asked if any seafaring men had gone by the road; at first we supposed he wanted company of his own kind, but soon saw he desired to avoid them. If a seaman put up at the Benbow, he peered through the curtained door and was silent as a mouse while the man was present. He took me aside, promised a silver fourpenny on the first of every month if I would keep my “weather-eye open for a seafaring man with one leg” and let him know the moment he appeared. Often, on the first, he would blow through his nose and stare me down; but before the week was out he thought better of it, brought me my four-penny piece, and repeated his orders to look out for “the seafaring man with one leg.”

How that personage haunted my dreams! On stormy nights, when the wind shook the four corners of the house and the surf roared along the cove and up the cliffs, I saw him in a thousand forms, with a thousand diabolical expressions; now the leg cut off at knee or hip, now a monstrous creature with but the one leg in the middle of his body, leaping and pursuing me over hedge and ditch—the worst of nightmares. I paid dear for my monthly fourpenny in these abominable fancies.

Yet I was far less afraid of the captain than were others. There were nights when he took more rum and water than his head would carry; then he would sing his wicked, wild sea-songs, minding nobody; or else call for glasses round and force the trembling company to listen to his stories or bear a chorus. Often the house shook with “Yo-ho-ho, and a bottle of rum,” neighbours joining for dear life, each louder than the other to avoid remark. In such fits he was the most overriding companion ever known; he slapped for silence, flew into a passion at a question—or because none was put—and would let no one leave till he had drunk himself sleepy and reeled to bed.

His stories frightened people worst: dreadful tales of hanging, walking the plank, storms, the Dry Tortugas, wild deeds and places on the Spanish Main; his very language shocked our plain country folk. My father said the inn would be ruined; yet I believe the captain did us good. People were frightened at the time, but, looking back, rather liked it; a fine excitement in a quiet country life. There was even a party of younger men who called him a “true sea-dog” and a “real old salt,” saying such men made England terrible at sea.

In one way he bade fair to ruin us: he stayed week after week, month after month, till all the money was long exhausted, and still my father had not the heart to insist on more. If he mentioned it, the captain blew through his nose till you might say he roared, and stared my poor father out of the room. I have seen my father wringing his hands after such a rebuff, and am sure the annoyance and terror he lived in hastened his early and unhappy death.

All the time he made no change in dress save to buy stockings of a hawker. One cock of his hat having fallen, he let it hang for ever after, though it plagued him when it blew. His coat, patched by his own hand, became nothing but patches. He never wrote or received a letter; he spoke with none but neighbours, and those for the most part only when drunk on rum. The great sea-chest none of us had ever seen open.

He was only once crossed, and that toward the end, when my poor father was far gone in a decline. Dr. Livesey came of an afternoon to see him, took a bit of dinner from my mother, and sat in the parlour to smoke till his horse came down from the hamlet—for we had no stabling at the old Benbow. I followed, and marked the contrast the neat, bright doctor, with powder white as snow, bright black eyes, and pleasant manners, made with the coltish country folk, and above all with that filthy, heavy, bleared scarecrow of a pirate of ours, far gone in rum, arms on the table. Suddenly the captain piped up his eternal song:

“Fifteen men on the dead man’s chest--
   Yo-ho-ho, and a bottle of rum!
 Drink and the devil had done for the rest--
   Yo-ho-ho, and a bottle of rum!”

By this time none of us heeded it; it was new only to the doctor, on whom it made no agreeable impression, for he looked up angrily and went on with his talk to old Taylor the gardener on a new cure for the rheumatics. The captain brightened at his own music, flapped his hand for silence; the voices stopped at once, all but Dr. Livesey’s, who spoke clear and kind, drawing at his pipe. The captain glared, flapped again, glared harder, and at last broke out with a villainous low oath, “Silence, there, between decks!”

“Were you addressing me, sir?” says the doctor; and on the ruffian’s oath that it was so: “I have only one thing to say to you, sir—that if you keep on drinking rum, the world will soon be quit of a very dirty scoundrel!”

The old fellow’s fury was awful. He sprang up, drew a sailor’s clasp-knife, and, balancing it open on his palm, threatened to pin the doctor to the wall.

The doctor never so much as moved. He spoke over his shoulder, in the same tone, high enough for all the room: “If you do not put that knife this instant in your pocket, I promise, upon my honour, you shall hang at the next assizes.”

They battled with looks; the captain knuckled under, put up his weapon, and resumed his seat, grumbling like a beaten dog.

“And now, sir,” continued the doctor, “since I know there’s such a fellow in my district, you may count I’ll have an eye upon you day and night. I’m not a doctor only; I’m a magistrate; and if I catch a breath of complaint against you—if it’s only for a piece of incivility like tonight’s—I’ll take effectual means to have you hunted down and routed out of this. Let that suffice.”

Soon after, Dr. Livesey’s horse came to the door and he rode away; but the captain held his peace that evening, and for many evenings to come.

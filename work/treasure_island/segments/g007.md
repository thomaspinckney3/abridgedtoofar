## Chapter 1

XX

Silver's Embassy


Sure enough, two men stood outside the stockade, one waving a white cloth, the other Silver himself, standing placidly by.

It was the coldest morning I was ever abroad in. The sky was bright overhead, but where Silver stood, all was still in shadow, and they waded knee-deep in a low white vapour that had crawled out of the morass. The chill and the vapour told a poor tale of the island: a damp, feverish, unhealthy spot.

"Keep indoors, men," said the captain. "Ten to one this is a trick." Then he hailed: "Who goes? Stand, or we fire."

"Flag of truce," cried Silver.

The captain set the watch and called the rest to load muskets. "What do you want with your flag of truce?"

"Cap'n Silver, sir, to come on board and make terms," shouted the other.

"Cap'n Silver! Don't know him. Who's he?" And we heard him add, "Cap'n, is it? My heart, and here's promotion!"

Long John answered for himself. "Me, sir. These poor lads have chosen me cap'n, after your desertion, sir"—laying a particular emphasis upon the word *desertion*. "All I ask is your word, Cap'n Smollett, to let me safe and sound out of this here stockade, and one minute to get out o' shot before a gun is fired."

"My man," said Captain Smollett, "I have not the slightest desire to talk to you. If you wish to talk to me, you can come, that's all. If there's any treachery, it'll be on your side, and the Lord help you."

"That's enough, Cap'n," shouted Long John cheerily. "A word from you's enough. I know a gentleman, and you may lay to that."

Silver threw over his crutch, surmounted the fence, and dropped to our side. He had terrible hard work up the knoll, but at last arrived before the captain—tricked out in his best, an immense blue coat thick with brass buttons, a fine laced hat on the back of his head. He saluted in the handsomest style.

"You had better sit down," said the captain.

"Why, Silver," he went on, when Silver complained of the cold sand, "if you had pleased to be an honest man, you might have been sitting in your galley. You're either my ship's cook—and then you were treated handsome—or Cap'n Silver, a common mutineer and pirate, and then you can go hang!"

"Well, well, Cap'n," returned the sea-cook, sitting on the sand.

"If you have anything to say, my man, better say it," said the captain.

"Dooty is dooty. That was a good lay of yours last night. Maybe my people was shook—maybe I was shook myself. But mark me, Cap'n, it won't do twice. He wasn't dead when I got round to him, not he."

Silver's words were a riddle to the captain, but I began to have an inkling—Ben Gunn had paid the buccaneers a visit while they lay drunk, and we had only fourteen enemies to deal with.

"Well, here it is," said Silver. "We want that treasure, and we'll have it. Give us the chart, and drop shooting poor seamen and stoving of their heads in while asleep, and we'll offer you a choice: come aboard once the treasure's shipped, and I'll give my affy-davy to clap you somewhere safe ashore; or stay here, divide stores man for man, and I'll speak the first ship I sight to send 'em to pick you up."

Captain Smollett rose and knocked out the ashes of his pipe. "Is that all?"

"Every last word, by thunder! Refuse that, and you've seen the last of me but musket-balls."

"Very good. Now you'll hear me. If you'll come up one by one, unarmed, I'll engage to clap you all in irons and take you home to a fair trial in England. If you won't, my name is Alexander Smollett, I've flown my sovereign's colours, and I'll see you all to Davy Jones. You can't find the treasure. You can't sail the ship. You can't fight us. Your ship's in irons, Master Silver; you're on a lee shore. And in the name of heaven, I'll put a bullet in your back when next I meet you. Bundle out of this, hand over hand, and double quick."

Silver's face was a picture; his eyes started in his head with wrath. "Give me a hand up!" he cried.

"Not I," returned the captain.

"Who'll give me a hand up?" he roared.

Not a man among us moved. Growling the foulest imprecations, he crawled along the sand till he got hold of the porch and could hoist himself again upon his crutch. Then he spat into the spring.

"There! That's what I think of ye. Before an hour's out, I'll stove in your old block house like a rum puncheon. Them that die'll be the lucky ones."

And with a dreadful oath he stumbled off and disappeared among the trees.

## Chapter 2

XXI

The Attack


As soon as Silver disappeared, the captain turned and found not a man at his post but Gray. It was the first time we had ever seen him angry.

"Quarters!" he roared; and as we slunk back, "Gray, I'll put your name in the log; you've stood by your duty like a seaman. Mr. Trelawney, I'm surprised at you, sir. Doctor, I thought you had worn the king's coat!"

"My lads," said he when all was clear, "I've given Silver a broadside. I pitched it in red-hot on purpose; and before the hour's out, we shall be boarded. We're outnumbered, but we fight in shelter; and I've no manner of doubt we can drub them."

He set the defence: doctor at the door, men at the loopholes east and west, Trelawney and Gray at the north side where the danger lay; Jim and the captain to load. The fire was tossed out, brandy served round; and as the sun climbed and drank up the vapours at a draught, we stood at our posts in a fever of heat and anxiety.

An hour passed.

"Hang them!" said the captain. "This is as dull as the doldrums. Gray, whistle for a wind."

And just at that moment came the first shot. The report was repeated from without in a scattering volley, like a string of geese, from every side of the enclosure. Several bullets struck the log-house, but not one entered; and as the smoke cleared, the woods looked as quiet and empty as before.

From the count of flashes, the attack would clearly come from the north. The captain made no change; if the mutineers crossed the stockade they could shoot us down like rats in our own stronghold.

Suddenly, with a loud huzza, a cloud of pirates leaped from the woods on the north side and ran on the stockade. A rifle ball sang through the doorway and knocked the doctor's musket into bits. The boarders swarmed over the fence like monkeys. Squire and Gray fired again and yet again; three men fell, one forwards into the enclosure, two back on the outside. One of these was up again in a crack and disappeared. Two had bit the dust, one had fled, four had made good their footing inside.

The four ran straight for the building. A pirate boatswain's head appeared at the middle loophole.

"At 'em, all hands—all hands!" he roared.

At the same moment another wrenched a musket from a servant's hands through the loophole and laid the poor fellow senseless on the floor; a third, running unharmed round the house, fell with his cutlass on the doctor.

Our position was utterly reversed. A moment since we were firing under cover at an exposed enemy; now it was we who lay uncovered.

"Out, lads, out, and fight 'em in the open! Cutlasses!" cried the captain.

I snatched a cutlass from the pile, and someone gave me a cut across the knuckles which I hardly felt. I dashed out into the sunlight. The doctor was pursuing his assailant down the hill, and as I looked, beat down his guard and sent him sprawling on his back with a great slash across the face.

"Round the house, lads! Round the house!" cried the captain.

I ran round the corner and was face to face with the boatswain. He roared, his hanger flashing above his head. I had not time to be afraid, but as the blow still hung impending, leaped to one side, missed my foot in the soft sand, and rolled headlong down the slope.

When I had first sallied from the door, the other mutineers had been swarming up the palisade. One man, in a red night-cap, with his cutlass in his mouth, had even thrown a leg across. So short had been the interval that when I found my feet again, all was in the same posture—and yet, in this breath of time, the fight was over and the victory was ours.

Gray, following close behind me, had cut down the boatswain ere he could recover. Another had been shot at a loophole in the act of firing into the house. A third the doctor had disposed of at a blow. Of the four who had scaled the palisade, only one remained, and he was now clambering out again with the fear of death upon him.

"Fire—fire from the house!" cried the doctor. "And you, lads, back into cover."

But his words were unheeded, and the last boarder made good his escape. In three seconds nothing remained of the attacking party but the five who had fallen, four on the inside and one on the outside.

The doctor and Gray and I ran for shelter. The house was cleared of smoke, and we saw at a glance the price of victory. One servant lay stunned beside his loophole, another shot through the head; while right in the centre, the squire was supporting the captain, one as pale as the other.

"The captain's wounded," said Mr. Trelawney.

"Have they run?"

"All that could," returned the doctor. "But there's five of them will never run again."

"Five!" cried the captain. "Come, that's better. Five against three leaves us four to nine. That's better odds than we had at starting."

## Chapter 3

XXII

How I Began My Sea Adventure


There was no return of the mutineers—not so much as another shot. They had "got their rations for that day," as the captain put it. Of the eight who had fallen, only three still breathed—one of the pirates and two of our own; and of these the first two were as good as dead. The mutineer died under the doctor's knife; one servant lingered all day and went to his Maker in the night.

The captain's wounds were grievous but not dangerous. A ball had broken his shoulder-blade and touched the lung; another had torn muscles in the calf. He would recover, the doctor said, but must not walk nor move his arm, nor so much as speak when he could help it. My own cut across the knuckles was a flea-bite; Doctor Livesey patched it with plaster and pulled my ears for me into the bargain.

After dinner the squire and doctor consulted by the captain's side; then the doctor took his hat and pistols, girt on a cutlass, pocketed the chart, and crossed the palisade with a musket over his shoulder, setting off briskly through the trees.

Gray took his pipe out of his mouth, fairly thunder-struck. "Why, in the name of Davy Jones, is Dr. Livesey mad?"

"He's about the last of this crew for that," said I. "I take it he's going to see Ben Gunn."

I was right; but meanwhile the house was stifling and the sand within the palisade ablaze, and I began to envy the doctor in the cool shadow of the woods. With so much blood about me and dead bodies all around, I took a disgust of the place almost as strong as fear. At last, with no one observing me, I took the first step of my escapade: I filled my pockets with biscuit, laid hold of a brace of pistols, and felt myself well supplied.

My scheme was to go down the sandy spit, find the white rock I had observed last evening, and see whether Ben Gunn had hidden his boat there. As I should not be allowed to leave, my only plan was to take French leave—so bad a way as made the thing itself wrong. But I was only a boy, and I had made up my mind. The squire and Gray were busy with the captain's bandages; the coast was clear; I made a bolt over the stockade and was out of cry before my absence was observed. This was my second folly, far worse than the first; but like the first, it was a help towards saving all of us.

I took my way for the east coast. Soon I came forth into the open, and saw the sea blue and sunny to the horizon, the surf tumbling along the beach. I have never seen the sea quiet round Treasure Island. The sun might blaze overhead, the air be without a breath, but still these great rollers ran along the external coast, thundering by day and night.

I walked along the surf till, thinking I was far enough south, I crept up to the ridge of the spit. The breeze had blown itself out, succeeded by light airs carrying great banks of fog; the anchorage lay still and leaden. The HISPANIOLA, in that unbroken mirror, was exactly portrayed from truck to waterline, the Jolly Roger hanging from her peak. Alongside lay a gig, Silver in the stern-sheets—him I could always recognize. All at once there began the most horrid, unearthly screaming, which startled me, though I soon remembered the voice of Captain Flint.

Soon the jolly-boat pulled for shore. The sun went down behind the Spy-glass, and as the fog collected rapidly, it began to grow dark. I saw I must lose no time.

The white rock was still an eighth of a mile down the spit. Night had almost come when I laid my hand on its rough sides. Below was a hollow of green turf hidden by banks and underwood; and in the centre, a little tent of goat-skins. I lifted the side, and there was Ben Gunn's boat—a rude, lop-sided framework stretched with goat-skin, the hair inside. There was one thwart set low, a stretcher in the bows, and a double paddle. It was like the first and worst coracle ever made by man—but exceedingly light and portable.

Now I had taken another notion, and become so obstinately fond of it that I would have carried it out in the teeth of Captain Smollett himself: to slip out under cover of night, cut the HISPANIOLA adrift, and let her go ashore where she fancied. The mutineers, after their repulse, had nothing nearer their hearts than to up anchor and away; and now I had seen they left their watchmen unprovided with a boat, I thought it might be done with little risk.

Down I sat to wait for darkness and made a hearty meal of biscuit. The fog had buried all heaven. When at last I shouldered the coracle and groped my way out of the hollow, there were but two points visible on the whole anchorage: the great fire on shore where the defeated pirates caroused; and a mere blur of light marking the anchored ship.

The ebb had run some time, and I had to wade through a belt of swampy sand before I came to the water; and wading a little way in, I set my coracle, keel downwards, on the surface.

I was wakened by a clear, hearty voice hailing us from the margin of the wood: "Block house, ahoy! Here's the doctor."

And the doctor it was. Glad as I was, my gladness was not without admixture. I remembered my insubordinate and stealthy conduct, and felt ashamed to look him in the face.

"You, doctor! Top o' the morning to you, sir!" cried Silver, broad awake and beaming with good nature in a moment. "We've quite a surprise for you, sir — a noo boarder and lodger, looking fit and taut as a fiddle; slep' like a supercargo, right alongside of John."

I could hear the alteration in the doctor's voice as he said, "Not Jim?"

"The very same Jim as ever was," says Silver.

The doctor stopped outright. "Well, well," he said at last, "duty first and pleasure afterwards, as you might have said yourself, Silver. Let us overhaul these patients of yours."

He entered with one grim nod to me and proceeded among the sick, rattling on as if paying an ordinary professional visit in a quiet English family. His manner reacted on the men, who behaved as if he were still ship's doctor and they faithful hands before the mast.

"Because, you see, since I am mutineers' doctor, or prison doctor as I prefer to call it," says he, "I make it a point of honour not to lose a man for King George (God bless him!) and the gallows."

The rogues swallowed the home-thrust in silence. He dosed them round, and they took his prescriptions with really laughable humility, more like charity schoolchildren than blood-guilty mutineers.

"Well, that's done for today. And now I should wish to have a talk with that boy, please."

A mutineer at the door swung round with a deep flush and cried "No!" and swore.

Silver struck the barrel with his open hand. "Si-lence!" he roared, looking about him positively like a lion. "Hawkins, will you give me your word of honour as a young gentleman not to slip your cable?"

I readily gave the pledge.

"Then, doctor, you just step outside o' that stockade, and I'll bring the boy down on the inside, and I reckon you can yarn through the spars."

The explosion of disapproval that broke out as the doctor left accused Silver of playing double — the identical thing he was doing. But he called them all the fools and dolts you can imagine, fluttered the chart in their faces, asked if they could afford to break the treaty the very day they were bound a-treasure-hunting.

"No, by thunder!" he cried. "It's us must break the treaty when the time comes; and till then I'll gammon that doctor, if I have to ile his boots with brandy."

Then, hand on my shoulder, he stalked out upon his crutch. "Slow, lad, slow," he said. "They might round upon us in a twinkle of an eye if we was seen to hurry."

When we reached the stockade, Silver stopped. "You'll make a note of this, doctor. When a man's steering as near the wind as me, you wouldn't think it too much to give him one good word? It's not my life only now — it's that boy's into the bargain."

Silver was a changed man now his back was to his friends; his cheeks seemed fallen in, his voice trembled. Never was a soul more dead in earnest. He stepped back out of earshot and sat upon a tree-stump, whistling, spinning round to keep both me and his ruffians in view.

"So, Jim," said the doctor sadly, "here you are. As you have brewed, so shall you drink. When Captain Smollett was well, you dared not have gone off; and when he was ill and couldn't help it, by George, it was downright cowardly!"

I began to weep. "Doctor, I have blamed myself enough; my life's forfeit anyway, and I should have been dead but for Silver. What I fear is torture. If they come to torture me—"

"Jim," the doctor interrupted, his voice quite changed, "whip over, and we'll run for it."

"Doctor, I passed my word."

"I know, I know. I'll take it on my shoulders, holus bolus. Jump! One jump, and we'll run for it like antelopes."

"No," I replied; "you know right well you wouldn't do the thing yourself. Silver trusted me; I passed my word, and back I go. But, doctor — if they torture me, I might let slip where the ship is. I got the ship; she lies in North Inlet, on the southern beach, and just below high water."

"The ship!" exclaimed the doctor.

I described my adventures, and he heard me out in silence. "There is a kind of fate in this. Every step, it's you that saves our lives; do you suppose we are going to let you lose yours? You found the plot; you found Ben Gunn. Silver!" he cried as the cook drew near. "Don't be in any great hurry after that treasure."

"Why, sir, I can only save my life and the boy's by seeking for that treasure."

"Well, Silver — if that is so, I'll go one step further: look out for squalls when you find it."

"Sir, as between man and man, that's too much and too little. What you're after, why you left the block house, why you given me that there chart, I don't know. If you won't tell me plain out, just say so and I'll leave the helm."

"I've no right to say more; it's not my secret. But if we both get alive out of this wolf-trap, I'll do my best to save you, short of perjury. Keep the boy close beside you, and when you need help, halloo. I'm off to seek it for you. Good-bye, Jim."

Dr. Livesey shook hands with me through the stockade, nodded to Silver, and set off at a brisk pace into the wood.

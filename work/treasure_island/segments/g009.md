## Chapter XXVI

The wind hauled into the west, serving us well. The coxswain told me how to lay the ship to, and after many trials I succeeded; we both sat in silence over another meal.

"Cap'n," said he at length with that uncomfortable smile, "here's my old shipmate; s'pose you was to heave him overboard. I don't reckon him ornamental now, do you?"

"I'm not strong enough," said I, "and there he lies, for me."

"This here's an unlucky ship, this HISPANIOLA, Jim," he went on. "Well now, I'm no scholar, and you're a lad as can read and figure: do you take it as a dead man is dead for good, or do he come alive again?"

"You can kill the body, Mr. Hands, but not the spirit," I replied. "He is in another world, and may be watching us."

"Ah!" says he. "That's unfort'nate—appears killing parties was a waste of time. Howsomever, sperrits don't reckon for much. I'll chance it with the sperrits, Jim. Now, you've spoke up free, and I'll take it kind if you'd step down and get me—shiver my timbers!—a bottle of wine. This brandy's too strong for my head."

His hesitation seemed unnatural; the wine-versus-brandy story was a pretext. He wanted me off the deck. His eyes never met mine; he kept smiling and putting his tongue out in a guilty, embarrassed manner. I saw my advantage and could conceal my suspicions to the end.

"Some wine?" I said. "Will you have white or red?"

"It's about the blessed same to me, shipmate," he replied; "so it's strong, and plenty of it."

"All right," I answered. "I'll bring you port."

I scuttled down the companion noisily, slipped off my shoes, ran along the gallery, and popped my head out of the fore companion. My suspicions proved true. Hands had risen to his hands and knees, and though his leg hurt sharply, he trailed himself across the deck. He picked from a coil of rope a short dirk, discoloured to the hilt with blood, concealed it in his jacket, and trundled back.

This was all I required to know. Israel could move, was armed, and I was meant to be the victim. Yet I could trust him in one point: our interests jumped together in beaching the schooner. Until she was safe, my life would be spared.

I stole back, slipped on my shoes, took up a bottle of wine, and reappeared on deck. Hands lay all fallen together, eyelids lowered. He looked up, knocked the neck off the bottle like a man who had done the same often, and took a good swig with his favourite toast of "Here's luck!" Then he begged me to cut him a quid of tobacco.

"Ah, Jim, Jim," said he, "I reckon I've missed stays! Cut me a quid, as'll likely be the last, lad, for I'm for my long home."

"Well," said I, "if I was you, I'd go to my prayers like a Christian man."

"Why?" said he. "Now, you tell me why."

"Why?" I cried. "You've broken your trust; you've lived in sin and lies and blood; there's a man you killed lying at your feet this moment. For God's mercy, Mr. Hands, that's why."

He took a great draught of wine and spoke with unusual solemnity. "For thirty years I've sailed the seas and seen good and bad, fair weather and foul. Well, now I tell you, I never seen good come o' goodness yet. Him as strikes first is my fancy; dead men don't bite; them's my views—amen, so be it. And now," he added, suddenly changing his tone, "we've had enough of this foolery. The tide's made good. You just take my orders, Cap'n Hawkins, and we'll sail slap in and be done with it."

We had scarce two miles to run, but the navigation was delicate. Hands was an excellent pilot, and we dodged in with a neatness that was a pleasure to behold. Scarcely had we passed the heads before the land closed around us. Right before us we saw the wreck of a great ship in the last stages of dilapidation, hung with webs of dripping seaweed, and on its deck shore bushes had taken root and flourished thick with flowers.

"Now," said Hands, "there's a pet bit for to beach a ship in. Fine flat sand, never a cat's paw, trees all around. And now, boy, stand by. Starboard a little—so—steady—starboard—larboard a little—steady—steady!"

He issued his commands, which I breathlessly obeyed, till, all of a sudden, he cried, "Now, my hearty, luff!" I put the helm hard up, and the HISPANIOLA swung round and ran stem on for the wooded shore.

The excitement of these manoeuvres had interfered with my watch upon the coxswain. I might have fallen without a struggle had not a sudden disquietude made me turn my head. There was Hands, already half-way towards me, with the dirk in his right hand.

We must both have cried out when our eyes met. He threw himself forward and I leapt sideways towards the bows. As I did so, I let go of the tiller, which sprang sharp to leeward, struck Hands across the chest, and stopped him dead.

Before he could recover, I was safe out of the corner. Just forward of the main-mast I stopped, drew a pistol, took a cool aim, and drew the trigger. The hammer fell, but there followed neither flash nor sound—the priming was useless with sea-water. I cursed myself for my neglect.

I placed my palms against the main-mast and waited, every nerve on the stretch. Seeing I meant to dodge, he paused; and a moment passed in feints on his part and corresponding movements upon mine. It was such a game as I had often played at home about the rocks of Black Hill Cove, but never with such a wildly beating heart.

Then, suddenly, the HISPANIOLA struck, staggered, and canted over to the port side till the deck stood at forty-five degrees. We were both capsized, rolled into the scuppers, the dead body tumbling stiffly after us. I was first afoot. Quick as thought, I sprang into the mizzen shrouds, rattled up hand over hand, and was seated on the cross-trees before I drew breath. The dirk had struck not half a foot below me; and there stood Israel with his mouth open, a perfect statue of surprise and disappointment.

I lost no time in changing the priming of my pistol, and recharging the other afresh. My new employment struck Hands all of a heap. After an obvious hesitation, he hauled himself heavily into the shrouds, and with the dirk in his teeth, began slowly and painfully to mount. I had quietly finished my arrangements before he was a third of the way up.

"One more step, Mr. Hands," said I, "and I'll blow your brains out! Dead men don't bite, you know," I added with a chuckle.

He stopped instantly. At last, with a swallow or two, he spoke. "Jim," says he, "I reckon we're fouled, you and me, and we'll have to sign articles. I'd have had you but for that there lurch, but I don't have no luck, not I; and I reckon I'll have to strike, which comes hard for a master mariner to a ship's younker like you, Jim."

I was drinking in his words and smiling away, as conceited as a cock upon a wall, when, all in a breath, back went his right hand over his shoulder. Something sang like an arrow through the air; I felt a blow and then a sharp pang, and there I was pinned by the shoulder to the mast. In the horrid pain and surprise of the moment, both my pistols went off, and both escaped out of my hands. They did not fall alone; with a choked cry, the coxswain loosed his grasp upon the shrouds and plunged head first into the water.

## Chapter XXVII

Owing to the cant of the vessel, the masts hung far out over the water, and from my perch on the cross-trees I had nothing below me but the surface of the bay. Hands rose once to the surface in a lather of foam and blood and then sank again for good. As the water settled, I could see him lying huddled together on the clean, bright sand in the shadow of the vessel's sides. A fish or two whipped past his body. He was dead enough, being both shot and drowned, and was food for fish in the very place where he had designed my slaughter.

I felt sick, faint, and terrified. The hot blood was running over my back and chest. The dirk, where it had pinned my shoulder to the mast, seemed to burn like a hot iron; yet it was not so much these real sufferings that distressed me as the horror of falling from the cross-trees into that still green water, beside the body of the coxswain.

I clung with both hands till my nails ached, and shut my eyes as if to cover up the peril. Gradually my mind came back, my pulses quieted, and I was once more in possession of myself.

It was my first thought to pluck forth the dirk, but either it stuck too hard or my nerve failed, and I desisted with a violent shudder. Oddly enough, that very shudder did the business. The knife had come the nearest in the world to missing me altogether; it held me by a mere pinch of skin, which the shudder tore away. The blood ran down the faster, but I was my own master again, only tacked to the mast by my coat and shirt. These I broke through with a sudden jerk, and regained the deck by the starboard shrouds.

I went below and did what I could for my wound; it pained me but was neither deep nor dangerous. Then I began to think of clearing the ship from its last passenger. He lay against the bulwarks like some horrible, ungainly puppet, life-size indeed, but how different from life's colour or comeliness. The habit of tragical adventures had worn off almost all my terror for the dead, and I took him by the waist and tumbled him overboard. He went in with a sounding plunge; the red cap came off and remained floating on the surface; and I could see him and Israel lying side by side, both wavering with the tremulous movement of the water, the quick fishes steering to and fro over both.

I was now alone upon the ship; the tide had just turned. The evening breeze had sprung up, and the cordage had begun to sing softly. I doused the jibs and tumbled them to the deck; the main-sail was a harder matter, and at last I got my knife and cut the halyards. The peak dropped, a great belly of loose canvas floated broad upon the water. For the rest, the HISPANIOLA must trust to luck, like myself.

Holding the cut hawser in both hands for a last security, I let myself drop softly overboard. The water scarcely reached my waist, and I waded ashore in great spirits, leaving the schooner on her side, with her main-sail trailing wide upon the surface of the bay. The sun went fairly down and the breeze whistled low among the tossing pines.

At least, and at last, I was off the sea, nor had I returned empty-handed. There lay the schooner, clear of buccaneers and ready for our own men to board. The recapture of the HISPANIOLA was a clenching answer, and I hoped even Captain Smollett would confess I had not lost my time.

I set my face homeward, turned the corner of the two-peaked hill, and waded across the watercourse. As I opened out the cleft between the two peaks, I became aware of a wavering glow against the sky, where the maroon was cooking his supper before a roaring fire. I wondered, in my heart, that he should show himself so careless—if I could see this radiance, might it not reach the eyes of Silver?

Gradually the night fell blacker, but a pale glimmer of moonbeams alighted on the summit of the Spy-glass, and soon I saw the moon had risen. With this to help me, I drew near the stockade. As I began to thread the grove that lies before it, I slacked my pace and went a trifle warily. It would have been a poor end of my adventures to be shot down by my own party in mistake.

Right in front of me a glow of a different colour appeared among the trees—red and hot, like the embers of a bonfire smouldering. For the life of me I could not think what it might be.

At last I came down upon the borders of the clearing. The block house itself lay in black shadow chequered with silvery streaks of moonlight. On the other side of the house an immense fire had burned itself into clear embers and shed a steady red reverberation. There was not a soul stirring nor a sound beside the noises of the breeze.

I stopped, with much wonder in my heart, and perhaps a little terror also. It had not been our way to build great fires; and I began to fear something had gone wrong while I was absent.

I stole round by the eastern end and crossed the palisade. To make assurance surer, I crawled, without a sound, towards the corner of the house. As I drew nearer, my heart was suddenly lightened. It was like music to hear my friends snoring together so loud and peaceful in their sleep.

By this time I had got to the door and stood up. All was dark within. There was the steady drone of the snorers and a small occasional flickering or pecking I could in no way account for. With my arms before me I walked steadily in. I should lie down in my own place (I thought with a silent chuckle) and enjoy their faces when they found me in the morning.

My foot struck something yielding—a sleeper's leg; and he turned and groaned, but without awaking. And then, all of a sudden, a shrill voice broke forth out of the darkness:

"Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight!" and so forth, without pause or change, like the clacking of a tiny mill.

Silver's green parrot, Captain Flint! It was she, keeping better watch than any human being, who thus announced my arrival with her wearisome refrain. I had no time to recover. The sleepers awoke and sprang up; and with a mighty oath, the voice of Silver cried, "Who goes?"

I turned to run, struck violently against one person, recoiled, and ran full into the arms of a second, who closed upon and held me tight.

"Bring a torch, Dick," said Silver when my capture was thus assured.

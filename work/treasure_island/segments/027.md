XXII

How I Began My Sea Adventure


There was no return of the mutineers—not so much as another shot. They had "got their rations for that day," as the captain put it. Of the eight who had fallen, only three still breathed—one of the pirates and two of our own; and of these the first two were as good as dead. The mutineer died under the doctor's knife; one servant lingered all day and went to his Maker in the night.

The captain's wounds were grievous but not dangerous. A ball had broken his shoulder-blade and touched the lung; another had torn muscles in the calf. He would recover, the doctor said, but must not walk nor move his arm, nor so much as speak when he could help it. My own cut across the knuckles was a flea-bite; Doctor Livesey patched it with plaster and pulled my ears for me into the bargain.

After dinner the squire and doctor consulted by the captain's side; then the doctor took his hat and pistols, girt on a cutlass, pocketed the chart, and crossed the palisade with a musket over his shoulder, setting off briskly through the trees.

Gray took his pipe out of his mouth, fairly thunder-struck. "Why, in the name of Davy Jones, is Dr. Livesey mad?"

"He's about the last of this crew for that," said I. "I take it he's going to see Ben Gunn."

I was right; but meanwhile the house was stifling and the sand within the palisade ablaze, and I began to envy the doctor in the cool shadow of the woods. With so much blood about me and dead bodies all around, I took a disgust of the place almost as strong as fear. At last, with no one observing me, I took the first step of my escapade: I filled my pockets with biscuit, laid hold of a brace of pistols, and felt myself well supplied.

My scheme was to go down the sandy spit, find the white rock I had observed last evening, and see whether Ben Gunn had hidden his boat there. As I should not be allowed to leave, my only plan was to take French leave—so bad a way as made the thing itself wrong. But I was only a boy, and I had made up my mind. The squire and Gray were busy with the captain's bandages; the coast was clear; I made a bolt over the stockade and was out of cry before my absence was observed. This was my second folly, far worse than the first; but like the first, it was a help towards saving all of us.

I took my way for the east coast. Soon I came forth into the open, and saw the sea blue and sunny to the horizon, the surf tumbling along the beach. I have never seen the sea quiet round Treasure Island. The sun might blaze overhead, the air be without a breath, but still these great rollers ran along the external coast, thundering by day and night.

I walked along the surf till, thinking I was far enough south, I crept up to the ridge of the spit. The breeze had blown itself out, succeeded by light airs carrying great banks of fog; the anchorage lay still and leaden. The HISPANIOLA, in that unbroken mirror, was exactly portrayed from truck to waterline, the Jolly Roger hanging from her peak. Alongside lay a gig, Silver in the stern-sheets—him I could always recognize. All at once there began the most horrid, unearthly screaming, which startled me, though I soon remembered the voice of Captain Flint.

Soon the jolly-boat pulled for shore. The sun went down behind the Spy-glass, and as the fog collected rapidly, it began to grow dark. I saw I must lose no time.

The white rock was still an eighth of a mile down the spit. Night had almost come when I laid my hand on its rough sides. Below was a hollow of green turf hidden by banks and underwood; and in the centre, a little tent of goat-skins. I lifted the side, and there was Ben Gunn's boat—a rude, lop-sided framework stretched with goat-skin, the hair inside. There was one thwart set low, a stretcher in the bows, and a double paddle. It was like the first and worst coracle ever made by man—but exceedingly light and portable.

Now I had taken another notion, and become so obstinately fond of it that I would have carried it out in the teeth of Captain Smollett himself: to slip out under cover of night, cut the HISPANIOLA adrift, and let her go ashore where she fancied. The mutineers, after their repulse, had nothing nearer their hearts than to up anchor and away; and now I had seen they left their watchmen unprovided with a boat, I thought it might be done with little risk.

Down I sat to wait for darkness and made a hearty meal of biscuit. The fog had buried all heaven. When at last I shouldered the coracle and groped my way out of the hollow, there were but two points visible on the whole anchorage: the great fire on shore where the defeated pirates caroused; and a mere blur of light marking the anchored ship.

The ebb had run some time, and I had to wade through a belt of swampy sand before I came to the water; and wading a little way in, I set my coracle, keel downwards, on the surface.

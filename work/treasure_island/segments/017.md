What I Heard in the Apple-Barrel

"No, not I," said Silver. "Flint was cap'n; I was quartermaster, along of my timber leg. The same broadside I lost my leg, old Pew lost his deadlights. Now, what a ship was christened, so let her stay, I says. So it was with the old Walrus, Flint's old ship, as I've seen amuck with the red blood and fit to sink with gold."

"Ah!" cried another voice—the youngest hand on board, full of admiration. "He was the flower of the flock, was Flint!"

"I laid by nine hundred safe, from England, and two thousand after Flint. That ain't bad for a man before the mast—all safe in bank. 'Tain't earning now, it's saving does it, you may lay to that. Where's all England's men now? Where's Flint's? Why, most on 'em aboard here, and glad to get the duff. Old Pew, as had lost his sight, spent twelve hundred pound in a year like a lord; but for two year before he died, by the powers, the man was starving!"

"Well, it ain't much use, after all," said the young seaman.

"'Tain't much use for fools," cried Silver. "But you look here: you're young, but you're as smart as paint. I'll talk to you like a man."

You may imagine how I felt to hear this abominable old rogue addressing another in the very same words of flattery he had used to me. I think, if I had been able, I would have killed him through the barrel. Meantime he ran on, little supposing he was overheard.

"Here it is about gentlemen of fortune. They lives rough, and risk swinging, but they eat and drink like fighting-cocks, and when a cruise is done it's hundreds of pounds in their pockets. Now, the most goes for rum and a good fling, and to sea again in their shirts. But that's not the course I lay. I puts it all away, some here, some there, and none too much anywheres. I'm fifty, mark you; once back from this cruise, I set up gentleman in earnest."

"And can you trust your missis?" asked the other.

"Gentlemen of fortune," returned the cook, "usually trusts little among themselves, and right they are. But I have a way with me, I have. There was some that was feared of Pew, and some that was feared of Flint; but Flint his own self was feared of me. Ah, you may be sure of yourself in old John's ship."

"Well," replied the lad, "I didn't half a quarter like the job till I had this talk with you, John; but there's my hand on it."

"And a brave lad, and smart too," answered Silver, shaking hands so heartily that the barrel shook.

By this time I had begun to understand. By a "gentleman of fortune" they plainly meant neither more nor less than a common pirate, and the little scene I had overheard was the last act in the corruption of one of the honest hands—perhaps of the last left aboard. But on this point I was soon relieved, for Silver giving a little whistle, a third man strolled up.

"Dick's square," said Silver.

"Oh, I know'd Dick was square," returned the voice of Israel Hands. "He's no fool, is Dick." And he turned his quid and spat. "But look here: how long are we a-going to stand off and on like a blessed bumboat? I've had a'most enough o' Cap'n Smollett. I want to go into that cabin, I do. I want their pickles and wines."

"Israel," said Silver, "your head ain't much account, nor ever was. But you'll berth forward, and you'll live hard, and you'll speak soft, and you'll keep sober till I give the word; you may lay to that, my son."

"Well, I don't say no," growled the coxswain. "What I say is, when?"

"When! By the powers!" cried Silver. "The last moment I can manage. Here's a first-rate seaman, Cap'n Smollett, sails the blessed ship for us. Here's this squire and doctor with a map and such—I don't know where it is. Well then, I mean this squire and doctor shall find the stuff, and help us get it aboard. Then we'll see. If I was sure of you all, I'd have Cap'n Smollett navigate us half-way back again before I struck."

"Why, we're all seamen aboard here, I should think," said Dick.

"We're all forecastle hands, you mean," snapped Silver. "We can steer a course, but who's to set one? I'll finish with 'em at the island, as soon's the blunt's on board, and a pity it is."

"But," asked Dick, "when we do lay 'em athwart, what are we to do with 'em?"

"There's the man for me!" cried the cook admiringly. "Put 'em ashore like maroons? That would have been England's way. Or cut 'em down like that much pork? That would have been Flint's, or Billy Bones's."

"Billy was the man for that," said Israel. "'Dead men don't bite,' says he."

"Right you are," said Silver; "rough and ready. But mark you, this time it's serious. Dooty is dooty, mates. I give my vote—death. When I'm in Parlyment and riding in my coach, I don't want none of these sea-lawyers in the cabin a-coming home, unlooked for, like the devil at prayers. Wait is what I say; but when the time comes, why, let her rip!"

"John," cries the coxswain, "you're a man!"

"You'll say so, Israel, when you see," said Silver. "Only one thing I claim—I claim Trelawney. I'll wring his calf's head off his body with these hands. Dick," he added, "you just jump up and get me an apple, to wet my pipe like."

You may fancy the terror I was in! I should have leaped out and run if I had found the strength, but my limbs and heart alike misgave me. I heard Dick begin to rise, when Hands exclaimed, "Oh, stow that! Don't you get sucking of that bilge, John. Let's have a go of the rum."

"Dick," said Silver, "I trust you. There's the key; you fill a pannikin and bring it up."

Terrified as I was, I could not help thinking this must have been how the mate got the strong waters that destroyed him.

When Dick returned, one after another took the pannikin and drank—one "To luck," another "Here's to old Flint," and Silver himself, in a kind of song, "Here's to ourselves, and hold your luff, plenty of prizes and plenty of duff."

Just then a brightness fell upon me in the barrel; looking up, I found the moon had risen and was silvering the mizzen-top. Almost at the same moment the lookout shouted, "Land ho!"

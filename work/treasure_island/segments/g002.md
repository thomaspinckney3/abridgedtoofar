## Chapter 1

The Sea-chest

I lost no time in telling my mother all I knew, and we saw ourselves at once in a difficult and dangerous position. Some of the man's money was certainly due us, but his shipmates were not likely to yield their booty to pay a dead man's debts. To ride for Doctor Livesey would have left my mother unprotected; yet to remain was scarcely thinkable, with the captain dead on the parlour floor and the blind beggar perhaps returning. Bare-headed in the gathering evening and frosty fog, we ran out to seek help in the neighbouring hamlet.

It was already candle-light when we reached it, and I shall never forget how I was cheered to see the yellow shine in doors and windows. But no soul would consent to return with us to the Admiral Benbow. The name of Captain Flint, strange to me, carried a great weight of terror there; any comrade of the captain's was enough to frighten them to death. Several would ride to Dr. Livesey's, but not one would help defend the inn.

They say cowardice is infectious; but argument is a great emboldener, and my mother now made them a speech. She would not lose money that belonged to her fatherless boy. "If none of the rest of you dare," she said, "Jim and I dare. We'll have that chest open, if we die for it." They cried out at our foolhardiness, but gave me a loaded pistol and promised horses ready saddled, while a lad rode forward to the doctor's for armed assistance.

My heart was beating finely when we set forth in the cold night. A full moon rose redly through the upper fog; soon all would be as bright as day. We slipped along the hedges, noiseless and swift, and saw nothing till the door of the Admiral Benbow had closed behind us.

I slipped the bolt, and we stood and panted in the dark. Then my mother got a candle, and holding each other's hands we advanced into the parlour. He lay as we had left him, on his back, eyes open, one arm stretched out.

"Draw down the blind, Jim," whispered my mother; "they might come and watch outside. And now we have to get the key off _that_; and who's to touch it, I should like to know!" And she gave a kind of sob.

I went down on my knees. On the floor close to his hand was a little round of paper, blackened on one side. I could not doubt it was the _black spot_; and taking it up, I found written on the other side, in a very good, clear hand: "You have till ten tonight."

"He had till ten, Mother," said I; and just then our old clock began striking. The sudden noise startled us shockingly; but the news was good, for it was only six.

I felt in his pockets — small coins, a thimble, thread, pigtail tobacco, his gully, a pocket compass, a tinder box. I began to despair.

"Perhaps it's round his neck," suggested my mother.

Overcoming a strong repugnance, I tore open his shirt, and there, on a bit of tarry string which I cut with his own gully, we found the key. We hurried upstairs to the little room where his box had stood since his arrival.

It was like any seaman's chest, the initial "B" burned on the top with a hot iron, the corners smashed by rough usage. My mother had the lid thrown back in a twinkling. A strong smell of tobacco and tar rose from the interior; on top, a suit of very good clothes, never worn. Below lay the miscellany — a quadrant, sticks of tobacco, two brace of handsome pistols, an old Spanish watch and trinkets of foreign make, and five or six curious West Indian shells. I have often wondered since why he should have carried these shells with him in his wandering, guilty, and hunted life.

Underneath an old boat-cloak whitened with sea-salt lay, at last, a bundle tied up in oilcloth, and a canvas bag that gave forth, at a touch, the jingle of gold.

"I'll show these rogues that I'm an honest woman," said my mother. "I'll have my dues, and not a farthing over." And she began counting the captain's score from his bag into mine.

It was a long, difficult business, the coins of all countries — doubloons, louis d'ors, guineas, pieces of eight — shaken together at random. We were half-way through when I suddenly put my hand on her arm, for in the silent frosty air I had heard a sound that brought my heart into my mouth — the tap-tapping of the blind man's stick upon the frozen road. It drew nearer and nearer, while we sat holding our breath. Then it struck sharp on the inn door; the handle turned, the bolt rattled as the wretched being tried to enter; and then silence. At last the tapping recommenced, and to our indescribable joy, died slowly away.

"Mother," said I, "take the whole and let's be going."

But she would not take a fraction more nor less than her due, and was still arguing when a little low whistle sounded a good way off upon the hill. That was enough for both of us.

"I'll take what I have," she said, jumping to her feet.

"And I'll take this to square the count," said I, picking up the oilskin packet.

Next moment we were groping downstairs, leaving the candle by the empty chest, and out at the door in full retreat. The fog was rapidly dispersing; the moon shone clear on the high ground. The sound of running footsteps came to our ears, and a tossing lantern showed how rapidly they advanced.

"My dear," said my mother suddenly, "take the money and run on. I am going to faint."

We were just at the little bridge; I helped her, tottering, to the edge of the bank, where she gave a sigh and fell on my shoulder. Roughly done as it was, I managed to drag her down and a little way under the arch — farther I could not move her, for the bridge was too low. So there we had to stay, my mother almost entirely exposed and both of us within earshot of the inn.

## Chapter 2

The Last of the Blind Man

My curiosity was stronger than my fear, and I crept back to the bank, where, sheltering behind a bush of broom, I might command the road before our door. I was scarcely in position when my enemies began to arrive, seven or eight running hard, a lantern-bearer some paces in front. Three men ran hand in hand; the middle man of the trio was the blind beggar, and his voice the next moment confirmed it.

"Down with the door!" he cried.

A rush was made upon the Admiral Benbow. They paused, surprised to find the door open, but Pew's voice, louder and higher as if afire with rage, drove them on.

"In, in, in!" he shouted, and cursed them for their delay.

There was a pause, then a cry of surprise, and a voice shouting from the house, "Bill's dead."

"Search him, some of you shirking lubbers, and the rest aloft and get the chest!"

I could hear feet rattling up our old stairs. The window of the captain's room was thrown open with a slam and a jingle of broken glass, and a man leaned out into the moonlight.

"Pew," he cried, "they've been before us. Someone's turned the chest out alow and aloft."

"Is it there?" roared Pew.

"The money's there."

The blind man cursed the money. "Flint's fist, I mean!"

"We don't see it here nohow."

"It's these people of the inn — it's that boy. I wish I had put his eyes out!" cried Pew. "Scatter, lads, and find 'em!"

There followed a great to-do through the inn, heavy feet pounding, furniture thrown over, doors kicked in, until the very rocks re-echoed and the men came out again declaring we were nowhere to be found. Just then the same whistle that had alarmed us sounded twice through the night — a signal, I now perceived, from the hillside, warning of approaching danger.

"We'll have to budge, mates."

"Budge, you skulk!" cried Pew. "They must be close by; you have your hands on it. Scatter and look for them, dogs! Oh, shiver my soul, if I had eyes!"

Two fellows began to look half-heartedly among the lumber, while the rest stood irresolute.

"You have your hands on thousands, you fools, and you hang a leg!" Pew raged. "You'd be as rich as kings if you could find it. There wasn't one of you dared face Bill, and I did it — a blind man! And I'm to lose my chance for you!"

"Hang it, Pew, we've got the doubloons!" grumbled one.

Pew's passion took the upper hand, and he struck right and left in his blindness, his stick sounding heavily on more than one. They cursed back and tried in vain to wrest it from him.

This quarrel was the saving of us, for while it was still raging, another sound came from the hill — the tramp of horses galloping. A pistol-shot flashed from the hedge side. The buccaneers turned at once and ran, separating in every direction, so that in half a minute not a sign of them remained but Pew. Him they had deserted, whether in panic or revenge for his blows I know not; but there he remained, tapping up and down the road in a frenzy, groping and calling for his comrades. He took a wrong turn and ran past me, crying, "Johnny, Black Dog, Dirk — you won't leave old Pew, mates, not old Pew!"

Just then four or five riders came in sight in the moonlight and swept at full gallop down the slope. Pew saw his error, turned with a scream, and ran straight under the nearest of the coming horses. The rider tried to save him, but in vain. Down went Pew with a cry that rang high into the night; and the four hoofs trampled and spurned him and passed by. He fell on his side, then gently collapsed upon his face and moved no more.

I leaped to my feet and hailed the riders. They were revenue officers, summoned by the lad sent for the doctor; news of the lugger in Kitt's Hole had set them forth that night, and to that we owed our preservation. Pew was dead, stone dead. My mother, carried up to the hamlet and revived with cold water and salts, was none the worse for her terror, though she still continued to deplore the balance of the money. The officers rode on to Kitt's Hole, but the lugger was already standing out; a bullet whistled past their hail, and soon she doubled the point and disappeared.

Back at the Admiral Benbow, you cannot imagine such a state of smash; the very clock had been thrown down. Though nothing had been taken but the captain's money-bag and a little silver from the till, I could see at once that we were ruined.

"They got the money — what in fortune were they after? More money?"

"No, sir; not money, I think," I replied. "I believe I have the thing in my breast pocket; and I should like to get it put in safety."

Within the hour we were on the road to Dr. Livesey's house.

## Chapter 3

The Captain's Papers

We rode hard till we drew up before Dr. Livesey's door. The maid told us the doctor had gone up to the hall to dine and pass the evening with the squire.

The distance was short. We rode up the long, leafless, moonlit avenue, and were admitted at a word and led down a matted passage into a great library, all lined with bookcases, where the squire and Dr. Livesey sat, pipe in hand, on either side of a bright fire.

I had never seen the squire so near. A tall man, over six feet, broad in proportion, with a bluff face roughened and reddened by long travel; his black eyebrows moved readily, giving him a look of temper, not bad, you would say, but quick and high.

"Good evening, friend Jim," said the doctor with a nod. "What good wind brings you here?"

The story was told like a lesson; and you should have seen how the two gentlemen leaned forward and forgot to smoke in their interest. When they heard how my mother went back to the inn, Dr. Livesey slapped his thigh, and the squire cried "Bravo!" and broke his long pipe against the grate.

"As for riding down that black miscreant," said the squire, "I regard it as an act of virtue, sir, like stamping on a cockroach. This lad Hawkins is a trump."

"And so, Jim," said the doctor, "you have the thing they were after?"

"Here it is, sir," said I, and gave him the oilskin packet. The doctor put it quietly in the pocket of his coat.

"Squire, I mean to keep Jim Hawkins here to sleep at my house. With your permission, we'll have up the cold pie and let him sup."

I made a hearty supper, hungry as a hawk.

"And now, squire," said the doctor.

"And now, Livesey," said the squire in the same breath.

"One at a time," laughed Dr. Livesey. "You have heard of this Flint, I suppose?"

"Heard of him!" cried the squire. "He was the bloodthirstiest buccaneer that sailed. Blackbeard was a child to Flint. The Spaniards were so prodigiously afraid of him that, I tell you, sir, I was sometimes proud he was an Englishman."

"Well, I've heard of him myself. But the point is, had he money?"

"Money! What were these villains after but money? For what would they risk their rascal carcasses but money?"

"Supposing that I have here in my pocket some clue to where Flint buried his treasure, will that treasure amount to much?"

"Amount, sir! If we have the clue you talk about, I fit out a ship in Bristol dock, and take you and Hawkins along, and I'll have that treasure if I search a year."

"Very well. If Jim is agreeable, we'll open the packet."

The bundle was sewn together, and the doctor cut the stitches with his medical scissors. It contained two things — a book and a sealed paper.

The first page of the book bore only scraps of writing — "Billy Bones his fancy," "Mr. W. Bones, mate," "No more rum," "Off Palm Key he got itt." I could not help wondering who had "got itt," and what "itt" was. A knife in his back, as like as not.

The next pages were a curious series of entries: a date at one end of the line, a sum of money at the other, and varying numbers of crosses between. The record lasted nearly twenty years, and at the end stood a grand total: "Bones, his pile."

"I can't make head or tail of this," said Dr. Livesey.

"Clear as noonday," cried the squire. "This is the black-hearted hound's account-book. The crosses stand for ships or towns they sank or plundered; the sums are the scoundrel's share. And the amounts increase, you see, as he rose in rank."

"Right!" said the doctor. "And now for the other."

The paper had been sealed with a thimble — that very thimble, perhaps, I had found in the captain's pocket. The doctor cut the seals with great care, and out fell the map of an island, with latitude and longitude, soundings, names of hills and bays. It was about nine miles long and five across, shaped like a fat dragon standing up, with two fine land-locked harbours, and a hill in the centre marked "The Spy-glass." Three crosses of red ink — two on the north part of the island, one in the southwest — and beside this last, in a small, neat hand very different from the captain's tottery characters: "Bulk of treasure here."

On the back the same hand had added bearings — tall tree on Spy-glass shoulder, Skeleton Island east-southeast, ten feet — and signed, J.F.

That was all; but brief as it was, it filled the squire and Dr. Livesey with delight.

"Livesey," said the squire, "you will give up this wretched practice at once. Tomorrow I start for Bristol. In three weeks' time — three weeks! — two weeks — ten days — we'll have the best ship, sir, and the choicest crew in England. Hawkins shall come as cabin-boy. You'll make a famous cabin-boy, Hawkins. You, Livesey, are ship's doctor; I am admiral. We'll have favourable winds, a quick passage, and not the least difficulty in finding the spot, and money to eat, to roll in, to play duck and drake with ever after."

"Trelawney," said the doctor, "I'll go with you; and I'll go bail for it, so will Jim, and be a credit to the undertaking. There's only one man I'm afraid of."

"And who's that?"

"You; for you cannot hold your tongue. We are not the only men who know of this paper. These fellows who attacked the inn tonight, and the rest who stayed aboard that lugger, and more, I dare say, not far off, are, one and all, bound that they'll get that money. From first to last, not one of us must breathe a word of what we've found."

"Livesey," returned the squire, "you are always in the right of it. I'll be as silent as the grave."

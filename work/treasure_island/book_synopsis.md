Jim Hawkins, an innkeeper’s son on the English coast, watches his quiet life at the Admiral Benbow unravel when a scarred old sailor named Billy Bones takes up residence and pays him to watch for a one‑legged seaman. The inn fills with menace: Black Dog appears and fights Billy, the blind beggar Pew arrives to deliver the “black spot,” and Billy dies of apoplexy as revenue officers scatter the gang. From Billy’s sea chest Jim and his mother salvage a packet containing a map to Treasure Island, which Jim brings to the principled Dr. Livesey and the impulsive Squire Trelawney, who resolve to find the hoard of the notorious pirate Captain Flint. The squire outfits a schooner, the Hispaniola, in Bristol and blithely hires a one‑legged cook named Long John Silver, who charms everyone but quietly gathers a crew of Flint’s old hands. Captain Smollett senses danger, keeps discipline, and stows the arms aft, but the voyage rolls on under an uneasy truce. Hiding in an apple barrel, Jim overhears Silver’s plan to seize the ship and the treasure after the officers guide them to the island, a discovery that turns the adventure into a test of wits.

They anchor off a feverish, forested isle marked by three conical hills, and Silver’s men clamor ashore. Jim slips into the woods, witnesses Silver murder a loyal sailor, and flees in horror. He stumbles upon Ben Gunn, a marooned former pirate crazed by solitude and cheese cravings, who hints at knowing where the treasure lies. Meanwhile, Smollett, Livesey, and the loyal hands fortify an old stockade near a spring, fight off a relentless assault, and suffer losses among their servants, even as Silver sues for parley and jockeys between menace and charm. The siege grinds into a deadly stalemate, punctured by Jim’s impulsive solo foray in a tiny coracle to cut the Hispaniola free, a gamble that ends with him killing the treacherous coxswain Israel Hands in a desperate masthead struggle. Jim beaches the ship in North Inlet, only to return to the stockade and fall into Silver’s hands, where the pirate chieftain shields him to keep a bargaining chip alive.

Livesey visits under a flag of truce, leaves the pirates supplies and, bafflingly, the treasure map, and quietly counsels Jim to stay close to Silver. Silver’s men, feverish and fractious, force their captain to lead them inland by the bearings “Tall tree, Spy‑glass shoulder,” with Jim roped along like a captive bear. They find a skeleton laid as a compass pointer, Flint’s gruesome joke, and then an empty pit at the great pine: the treasure cache has been rifled, and the branding on broken chests—WALRUS—proves they are too late. Panic erupts, Silver shoots a mutineer at the moment Dr. Livesey, Gray, and Ben Gunn spring an ambush from the brush, scatter the survivors, and reveal the ruse. Months before, Ben had secretly dug up Flint’s hoard and stashed it in his cave; the doctor’s “gift” of the map was a decoy to move the pirates off and save the loyalists. Reunited, the party retreats to Ben’s cavern, where heaps of coin and gold bars gleam in the firelight and Captain Smollett, wounded but unbowed, receives Jim with gruff affection.

Over days of backbreaking labor they ferry fortunes to the Hispaniola, while the remnants of Silver’s crew drink themselves senseless in the marsh. The squire rebukes Silver as a “monstrous impostor,” yet the old sea‑cook remains useful, obsequious, and ever calculating, offering hands at every turn. At last they weigh anchor, refusing a final entreaty—and a musket ball—hurled by the three marooned mutineers on the point. In a friendly Spanish‑American port, Ben Gunn contrives Silver’s quiet escape with a sack of guineas, preferring a living rogue on the run to a gallows scene on the quay. The Hispaniola returns to Bristol with only five of her original company, “drink and the devil” having taken the rest.

In the aftermath, the treasure is divided and lives turn: Captain Smollett retires with honor, Gray becomes a sober ship’s officer and part‑owner, and Ben Gunn squanders his thousand pounds in nineteen days before settling into an odd local fame. Of Long John Silver there is no certain word—only the hope he found some small comfort with his old black woman and a parrot that still cries, “Pieces of eight!” As narrator, Jim marks this voyage as his coming of age, a schooling in courage, loyalty, and the corrosive lure of gold. He confesses that the surf and the Spy‑glass still haunt his sleep and that oxen and wain‑ropes would not drag him back to that accursed shore. Treasure Island endures in his memory as both a boy’s perilous playground and a dark ledger of greed, blood, and cunning. In the end, it is not maps and markers that steer men, he implies, but the compasses within them—and those are too easily shifted by the glitter of buried wealth.

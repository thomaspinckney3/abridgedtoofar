Squire Trelawney, Dr. Livesey, and the rest of these gentlemen having asked me to write down the whole particulars about Treasure Island, from the beginning to the end, keeping nothing back but the bearings of the island, and that only because there is still treasure not yet lifted, I take up my pen in the year of grace 17—, and go back to the time when my father kept the Admiral Benbow inn and the brown old seaman with the sabre cut first took up his lodging under our roof.

I remember him as if it were yesterday, as he came plodding to the inn door, his sea-chest following behind him in a hand-barrow—a tall, strong, heavy, nut-brown man, his tarry pigtail falling over the shoulder of his soiled blue coat, his hands ragged and scarred, with black, broken nails, and the sabre cut across one cheek, a dirty, livid white. I remember him looking round the cove and whistling to himself as he did so, and then breaking out in that old sea-song that he sang so often afterwards:

"Fifteen men on the dead man's chest— Yo-ho-ho, and a bottle of rum!"

in the high, old tottering voice that seemed to have been tuned and broken at the capstan bars. He rapped on the door with a bit of stick like a handspike, and called roughly for a glass of rum, which he drank slowly, like a connoisseur, still looking about him at the cliffs and up at our signboard.

"This is a handy cove," says he at length; "and a pleasant sittyated grog-shop. Much company, mate?"

My father told him no, very little company.

"Well, then," said he, "this is the berth for me. I'm a plain man; rum and bacon and eggs is what I want, and that head up there for to watch ships off. What you mought call me? You mought call me captain." He threw down three or four gold pieces. "You can tell me when I've worked through that," says he, looking as fierce as a commander.

Bad as his clothes were and coarsely as he spoke, he had none of the appearance of a man who sailed before the mast, but seemed like a mate or skipper accustomed to be obeyed.

He was a very silent man by custom. All day he hung round the cove or upon the cliffs with a brass telescope; all evening he sat in a corner of the parlour and drank rum and water very strong. Every day when he came back from his stroll he would ask if any seafaring men had gone by, and at last we began to see he was desirous to avoid them. He had taken me aside and promised me a silver fourpenny on the first of every month if I would only keep my "weather-eye open for a seafaring man with one leg" and let him know the moment he appeared.

How that personage haunted my dreams, I need scarcely tell you. On stormy nights, when the wind shook the four corners of the house, I would see him in a thousand forms—the leg cut off at the knee, or at the hip; or a monstrous creature who had never had but the one leg, in the middle of his body. I paid pretty dear for my monthly fourpenny piece.

There were nights when he took more rum than his head would carry, and would sing his wicked, old, wild sea-songs, or force the trembling company to bear a chorus, the house shaking with "Yo-ho-ho, and a bottle of rum." His stories were what frightened people worst of all—dreadful stories about hanging, and walking the plank, and storms at sea, and wild deeds on the Spanish Main. My father was always saying the inn would be ruined; but I really believe his presence did us good, for there was a fine excitement in it, and some of the younger men called him a "true sea-dog" and the sort that made England terrible at sea.

He kept on staying, week after week and month after month, till the money was long exhausted, and still my father never plucked up the heart to insist on more. If ever he mentioned it, the captain blew through his nose so loudly you might say he roared, and stared my poor father out of the room. The annoyance and the terror he lived in must have greatly hastened his early and unhappy death.

He was only once crossed, and that was towards the end. Dr. Livesey came late one afternoon to see my father, and went into the parlour to smoke a pipe. I remember the contrast the neat, bright doctor, with his powder as white as snow and his bright black eyes, made with that filthy, bleared scarecrow of a pirate of ours, sitting far gone in rum. Suddenly the captain began to pipe up his eternal song. The captain flapped his hand upon the table for silence. The voices stopped, all but Dr. Livesey's, who went on speaking clear and kind. The captain glared, and at last broke out with a villainous oath, "Silence, there, between decks!"

"Were you addressing me, sir?" says the doctor; and when the ruffian had told him so, "I have only one thing to say to you, sir," replies the doctor, "that if you keep on drinking rum, the world will soon be quit of a very dirty scoundrel!"

The old fellow's fury was awful. He sprang to his feet, drew and opened a sailor's clasp-knife, and threatened to pin the doctor to the wall.

The doctor never so much as moved. He spoke as before, over his shoulder, in the same tone, perfectly calm and steady: "If you do not put that knife this instant in your pocket, I promise, upon my honour, you shall hang at the next assizes."

Then followed a battle of looks, but the captain soon knuckled under, put up his weapon, and resumed his seat, grumbling like a beaten dog.

"And now, sir," continued the doctor, "since I now know there's such a fellow in my district, you may count I'll have an eye upon you day and night. I'm not a doctor only; I'm a magistrate; and if I catch a breath of complaint against you, I'll have you hunted down and routed out of this. Let that suffice."

Soon after, Dr. Livesey rode away; but the captain held his peace that evening, and for many evenings to come.

It was not long after this that there occurred the first of the mysterious events that rid us at last of the captain, though not of his affairs. It was a bitter cold winter, and my poor father was little likely to see the spring.

One January morning, very early—a pinching, frosty morning—the captain had risen earlier than usual and set out down the beach, his cutlass swinging under the broad skirts of his old blue coat, his brass telescope under his arm. I was laying the breakfast-table when the parlour door opened and a man stepped in on whom I had never set my eyes before. He was a pale, tallowy creature, wanting two fingers of the left hand, and though he wore a cutlass, he did not look much like a fighter.

I asked him what was for his service, and he said he would take rum; but as I was going out, he motioned me to draw near.

"Come here, sonny," says he. "Is this here table for my mate Bill?"

I told him I did not know his mate Bill, and this was for a person we called the captain.

"Well," said he, "my mate Bill would be called the captain, as like as not. He has a cut on one cheek and a mighty pleasant way with him, particularly in drink. Now, is my mate Bill in this here house?"

I told him he was out walking, and pointed out the way he was likely to return.

"Ah," said he, "this'll be as good as drink to my mate Bill."

The expression of his face was not pleasant. The stranger kept hanging about just inside the door, peering round the corner like a cat waiting for a mouse. Once I stepped out into the road, but he called me back with an oath that made me jump. Then he returned to his fawning manner, patted me on the shoulder, and told me I was a good boy. "And here, sure enough, is my mate Bill, with a spy-glass under his arm. You and me'll just go back into the parlour, sonny, and get behind the door, and we'll give Bill a little surprise."

So saying, he backed with me into the parlour and put me behind him in the corner so that we were both hidden by the open door. The stranger was certainly frightened himself; he loosened the blade in his sheath and kept swallowing.

At last in strode the captain, slammed the door behind him, and marched straight across the room to where his breakfast awaited him.

"Bill," said the stranger.

The captain spun round on his heel; all the brown had gone out of his face, and even his nose was blue; he had the look of a man who sees a ghost.

"Come, Bill, you know me; you know an old shipmate, Bill, surely," said the stranger.

The captain made a sort of gasp.

"Black Dog!" said he.

"And who else?" returned the other. "Black Dog as ever was, come for to see his old shipmate Billy, at the Admiral Benbow inn."

"Now, look here," said the captain; "you've run me down; here I am; well, then, speak up; what is it?"

"That's you, Bill," returned Black Dog. "I'll have a glass of rum, and we'll sit down and talk square, like old shipmates."

When I returned with the rum, they were already seated on either side of the table, Black Dog next the door so as to have an eye on his retreat. He bade me leave the door wide open and retire. For a long time I could hear nothing but a low gattling; but at last the voices began to grow higher.

"No, no, no, no; and an end of it!" cried the captain once. And again, "If it comes to swinging, swing all, say I."

Then all of a sudden there was a tremendous explosion of oaths—the chair and table went over, a clash of steel followed, and a cry of pain, and the next instant I saw Black Dog in full flight, and the captain pursuing, both with drawn cutlasses, the former streaming blood from the shoulder. Just at the door the captain aimed one last tremendous cut, which would certainly have split him to the chine had it not been intercepted by our big signboard of Admiral Benbow. You may see the notch on the lower side of the frame to this day.

That blow was the last of the battle. Black Dog showed a wonderful clean pair of heels and disappeared over the edge of the hill in half a minute. The captain stood staring at the signboard like a bewildered man, then passed his hand over his eyes and turned back into the house.

"Jim," says he, "rum"; and as he spoke, he reeled, and caught himself with one hand against the wall.

"Are you hurt?" cried I.

"Rum," he repeated. "I must get away from here. Rum! Rum!"

I ran to fetch it, but I was unsteadied, and while I was still getting in my own way, I heard a loud fall in the parlour, and running in, beheld the captain lying full length upon the floor. My mother came running downstairs. He was breathing very loud and hard, his face a horrible colour.

It was a happy relief when the door opened and Doctor Livesey came in, on his visit to my father.

"Oh, doctor," we cried, "where is he wounded?"

"Wounded? A fiddle-stick's end!" said the doctor. "No more wounded than you or I. The man has had a stroke, as I warned him. Now, Mrs. Hawkins, just you run upstairs to your husband and tell him, if possible, nothing about it. For my part, I must do my best to save this fellow's trebly worthless life; Jim, you get me a basin."

When I got back, the doctor had ripped up the captain's sleeve and exposed his great sinewy arm, tattooed in several places. "Here's luck," "A fair wind," and "Billy Bones his fancy," were neatly executed on the forearm; and up near the shoulder there was a sketch of a gallows and a man hanging from it.

"Prophetic," said the doctor. He took his lancet and opened a vein.

A great deal of blood was taken before the captain opened his eyes. He recognized the doctor with a frown, then looked at me with relief; but suddenly his colour changed, and he tried to raise himself, crying, "Where's Black Dog?"

"There is no Black Dog here," said the doctor, "except what you have on your own back. You have been drinking rum; you have had a stroke, and I have just dragged you headforemost out of the grave. Now, Mr. Bones—"

"That's not my name," he interrupted.

"Much I care," returned the doctor. "It's the name of a buccaneer of my acquaintance; and what I have to say is this: one glass of rum won't kill you, but if you take one you'll take another, and I stake my wig if you don't break off short, you'll die—do you understand that?—die, and go to your own place, like the man in the Bible."

Between us we hoisted him upstairs and laid him on his bed.

"Now, mind you," said the doctor, "I clear my conscience—the name of rum for you is death." And he took me aside. "I have drawn blood enough to keep him quiet awhile; he should lie for a week where he is; but another stroke would settle him."

About noon I stopped at the captain's door with some cooling drinks. He was lying weak and excited.

"Jim," he said, "you're the only one here that's worth anything, and I've been always good to you. Never a month but I've given you a silver fourpenny. And now, mate, I'm pretty low, and deserted by all; and Jim, you'll bring me one noggin of rum, won't you, matey?"

"The doctor—" I began.

But he broke in cursing the doctor in a feeble voice. "Doctors is all swabs; what do he know about seafaring men? I been in places hot as pitch, and mates dropping round with Yellow Jack—and I lived on rum. It's been meat and drink, and man and wife, to me. Look, Jim, how my fingers fidges. If I don't have a dram, I'll have the horrors; I seen old Flint in the corner there, behind you; as plain as print. I'll give you a golden guinea for a noggin, Jim."

He was growing more excited, and this alarmed me for my father, who needed quiet; besides, I was reassured by the doctor's words and offended by the bribe.

"I want none of your money," said I, "but what you owe my father. I'll get you one glass, and no more."

He seized it and drank it out.

"Aye, aye," said he, "that's some better. And now, did that doctor say how long I was to lie here?"

"A week at least," said I.

"Thunder!" he cried. "A week! I can't do that; they'd have the black spot on me by then. The lubbers is going about to get the wind of me this blessed moment. But I'm a saving soul. I'll trick 'em again. I'll shake out another reef, matey, and daddle 'em."

As he was speaking, he had risen from the bed with great difficulty, holding to my shoulder with a grip that almost made me cry out. He paused, sitting on the edge.

"That doctor's done me," he murmured. "My ears is singing. Lay me back." He fell back, and lay silent.

"Jim," he said at length, "you saw that seafaring man today? Black Dog—he's a bad 'un; but there's worse that put him on. Now, if I can't get away, and they tip me the black spot, mind you, it's my old sea-chest they're after. You get on a horse, and go to that eternal doctor swab, and tell him to pipe all hands—magistrates and sich—and he'll lay 'em aboard at the Admiral Benbow—all old Flint's crew that's left. I was first mate to old Flint, and I'm the on'y one as knows the place. He gave it me at Savannah, when he lay a-dying. But you won't peach unless they get the black spot on me, or unless you see that Black Dog again or a seafaring man with one leg, Jim—him above all."

"But what is the black spot, captain?" I asked.

"That's a summons, mate. But you keep your weather-eye open, Jim, and I'll share with you equals, upon my honour."

He wandered a little longer, then took his medicine like a child—"If ever a seaman wanted drugs, it's me"—and fell into a heavy, swoon-like sleep. What I should have done had all gone well I do not know. But as things fell out, my poor father died quite suddenly that evening, which put all other matters on one side. Our distress, the visits of the neighbours, and the work of the inn kept me too busy to think of the captain.

He got downstairs next morning, and had his meals, though he ate little and had more rum than usual, helping himself out of the bar; no one dared cross him. The doctor was suddenly taken up with a case many miles away. The captain seemed rather to grow weaker than regain his strength, clambering up and down stairs, holding on to the walls as he went, breathing hard like a man on a steep mountain. He had an alarming way now when drunk of drawing his cutlass and laying it bare before him on the table. Once, to our wonder, he piped up a different air, a kind of country love-song he must have learned in his youth.

So things passed until, the day after the funeral, about three o'clock of a bitter, foggy, frosty afternoon, I was standing at the door, full of sad thoughts about my father, when I saw someone drawing slowly near. He was plainly blind, for he tapped before him with a stick and wore a great green shade over his eyes; he was hunched and wore a huge old tattered sea-cloak with a hood that made him appear positively deformed. I never saw a more dreadful-looking figure. He stopped, and raising his voice in an odd sing-song, said, "Will any kind friend inform a poor blind man, who has lost the precious sight of his eyes in the gracious defence of his native country, England—and God bless King George!—where or in what part of this country he may now be?"

"You are at the Admiral Benbow, Black Hill Cove, my good man," said I.

"I hear a voice," said he, "a young voice. Will you give me your hand, my kind young friend, and lead me in?"

I held out my hand, and the horrible, soft-spoken, eyeless creature gripped it in a moment like a vise. I struggled to withdraw, but he pulled me close.

"Now, boy," he said, "take me in to the captain."

"Sir," said I, "upon my word I dare not."

"Oh," he sneered, "that's it! Take me in straight or I'll break your arm." And he gave it a wrench that made me cry out.

"Sir," said I, "it is for yourself I mean. The captain sits with a drawn cutlass—"

"Come, now, march," interrupted he; and I never heard a voice so cruel, and cold, and ugly. It cowed me more than the pain. I walked straight in toward the parlour, where our sick old buccaneer was sitting, dazed with rum. "Lead me straight up to him, and when I'm in view, cry out, 'Here's a friend for you, Bill.' If you don't, I'll do this," and with that he gave me a twitch that I thought would have made me faint.

As I opened the parlour door, I cried out the words in a trembling voice. The captain raised his eyes, and at one look the rum went out of him and left him staring sober. The expression of his face was not so much of terror as of mortal sickness. He made a movement to rise, but had not the force.

"Now, Bill, sit where you are," said the beggar. "If I can't see, I can hear a finger stirring. Business is business. Hold out your left hand. Boy, take his left hand by the wrist and bring it near to my right."

We both obeyed, and I saw him pass something from the hollow of his hand into the captain's palm, which closed upon it instantly.

"And now that's done," said the blind man; and at the words he suddenly left hold of me, and with incredible nimbleness skipped out into the road, where I could hear his stick go tap-tap-tapping into the distance.

It was some time before either of us gathered our senses, but at length the captain drew in his hand and looked sharply into the palm.

"Ten o'clock!" he cried. "Six hours. We'll do them yet," and he sprang to his feet.

Even as he did so, he reeled, put his hand to his throat, stood swaying for a moment, and then, with a peculiar sound, fell from his whole height face foremost to the floor.

I ran to him, calling to my mother. But haste was all in vain. The captain had been struck dead by thundering apoplexy. It is a curious thing: I had certainly never liked the man, though of late I had begun to pity him, but as soon as I saw he was dead, I burst into a flood of tears. It was the second death I had known, and the sorrow of the first was still fresh in my heart.

The Sea-chest

I lost no time in telling my mother all I knew, and we saw ourselves at once in a difficult and dangerous position. Some of the man's money was certainly due us, but his shipmates were not likely to yield their booty to pay a dead man's debts. To ride for Doctor Livesey would have left my mother unprotected; yet to remain was scarcely thinkable, with the captain dead on the parlour floor and the blind beggar perhaps returning. Bare-headed in the gathering evening and frosty fog, we ran out to seek help in the neighbouring hamlet.

It was already candle-light when we reached it, and I shall never forget how I was cheered to see the yellow shine in doors and windows. But no soul would consent to return with us to the Admiral Benbow. The name of Captain Flint, strange to me, carried a great weight of terror there; any comrade of the captain's was enough to frighten them to death. Several would ride to Dr. Livesey's, but not one would help defend the inn.

They say cowardice is infectious; but argument is a great emboldener, and my mother now made them a speech. She would not lose money that belonged to her fatherless boy. "If none of the rest of you dare," she said, "Jim and I dare. We'll have that chest open, if we die for it." They cried out at our foolhardiness, but gave me a loaded pistol and promised horses ready saddled, while a lad rode forward to the doctor's for armed assistance.

My heart was beating finely when we set forth in the cold night. A full moon rose redly through the upper fog; soon all would be as bright as day. We slipped along the hedges, noiseless and swift, and saw nothing till the door of the Admiral Benbow had closed behind us.

I slipped the bolt, and we stood and panted in the dark. Then my mother got a candle, and holding each other's hands we advanced into the parlour. He lay as we had left him, on his back, eyes open, one arm stretched out.

"Draw down the blind, Jim," whispered my mother; "they might come and watch outside. And now we have to get the key off _that_; and who's to touch it, I should like to know!" And she gave a kind of sob.

I went down on my knees. On the floor close to his hand was a little round of paper, blackened on one side. I could not doubt it was the _black spot_; and taking it up, I found written on the other side, in a very good, clear hand: "You have till ten tonight."

"He had till ten, Mother," said I; and just then our old clock began striking. The sudden noise startled us shockingly; but the news was good, for it was only six.

I felt in his pockets — small coins, a thimble, thread, pigtail tobacco, his gully, a pocket compass, a tinder box. I began to despair.

"Perhaps it's round his neck," suggested my mother.

Overcoming a strong repugnance, I tore open his shirt, and there, on a bit of tarry string which I cut with his own gully, we found the key. We hurried upstairs to the little room where his box had stood since his arrival.

It was like any seaman's chest, the initial "B" burned on the top with a hot iron, the corners smashed by rough usage. My mother had the lid thrown back in a twinkling. A strong smell of tobacco and tar rose from the interior; on top, a suit of very good clothes, never worn. Below lay the miscellany — a quadrant, sticks of tobacco, two brace of handsome pistols, an old Spanish watch and trinkets of foreign make, and five or six curious West Indian shells. I have often wondered since why he should have carried these shells with him in his wandering, guilty, and hunted life.

Underneath an old boat-cloak whitened with sea-salt lay, at last, a bundle tied up in oilcloth, and a canvas bag that gave forth, at a touch, the jingle of gold.

"I'll show these rogues that I'm an honest woman," said my mother. "I'll have my dues, and not a farthing over." And she began counting the captain's score from his bag into mine.

It was a long, difficult business, the coins of all countries — doubloons, louis d'ors, guineas, pieces of eight — shaken together at random. We were half-way through when I suddenly put my hand on her arm, for in the silent frosty air I had heard a sound that brought my heart into my mouth — the tap-tapping of the blind man's stick upon the frozen road. It drew nearer and nearer, while we sat holding our breath. Then it struck sharp on the inn door; the handle turned, the bolt rattled as the wretched being tried to enter; and then silence. At last the tapping recommenced, and to our indescribable joy, died slowly away.

"Mother," said I, "take the whole and let's be going."

But she would not take a fraction more nor less than her due, and was still arguing when a little low whistle sounded a good way off upon the hill. That was enough for both of us.

"I'll take what I have," she said, jumping to her feet.

"And I'll take this to square the count," said I, picking up the oilskin packet.

Next moment we were groping downstairs, leaving the candle by the empty chest, and out at the door in full retreat. The fog was rapidly dispersing; the moon shone clear on the high ground. The sound of running footsteps came to our ears, and a tossing lantern showed how rapidly they advanced.

"My dear," said my mother suddenly, "take the money and run on. I am going to faint."

We were just at the little bridge; I helped her, tottering, to the edge of the bank, where she gave a sigh and fell on my shoulder. Roughly done as it was, I managed to drag her down and a little way under the arch — farther I could not move her, for the bridge was too low. So there we had to stay, my mother almost entirely exposed and both of us within earshot of the inn.

The Last of the Blind Man

My curiosity was stronger than my fear, and I crept back to the bank, where, sheltering behind a bush of broom, I might command the road before our door. I was scarcely in position when my enemies began to arrive, seven or eight running hard, a lantern-bearer some paces in front. Three men ran hand in hand; the middle man of the trio was the blind beggar, and his voice the next moment confirmed it.

"Down with the door!" he cried.

A rush was made upon the Admiral Benbow. They paused, surprised to find the door open, but Pew's voice, louder and higher as if afire with rage, drove them on.

"In, in, in!" he shouted, and cursed them for their delay.

There was a pause, then a cry of surprise, and a voice shouting from the house, "Bill's dead."

"Search him, some of you shirking lubbers, and the rest aloft and get the chest!"

I could hear feet rattling up our old stairs. The window of the captain's room was thrown open with a slam and a jingle of broken glass, and a man leaned out into the moonlight.

"Pew," he cried, "they've been before us. Someone's turned the chest out alow and aloft."

"Is it there?" roared Pew.

"The money's there."

The blind man cursed the money. "Flint's fist, I mean!"

"We don't see it here nohow."

"It's these people of the inn — it's that boy. I wish I had put his eyes out!" cried Pew. "Scatter, lads, and find 'em!"

There followed a great to-do through the inn, heavy feet pounding, furniture thrown over, doors kicked in, until the very rocks re-echoed and the men came out again declaring we were nowhere to be found. Just then the same whistle that had alarmed us sounded twice through the night — a signal, I now perceived, from the hillside, warning of approaching danger.

"We'll have to budge, mates."

"Budge, you skulk!" cried Pew. "They must be close by; you have your hands on it. Scatter and look for them, dogs! Oh, shiver my soul, if I had eyes!"

Two fellows began to look half-heartedly among the lumber, while the rest stood irresolute.

"You have your hands on thousands, you fools, and you hang a leg!" Pew raged. "You'd be as rich as kings if you could find it. There wasn't one of you dared face Bill, and I did it — a blind man! And I'm to lose my chance for you!"

"Hang it, Pew, we've got the doubloons!" grumbled one.

Pew's passion took the upper hand, and he struck right and left in his blindness, his stick sounding heavily on more than one. They cursed back and tried in vain to wrest it from him.

This quarrel was the saving of us, for while it was still raging, another sound came from the hill — the tramp of horses galloping. A pistol-shot flashed from the hedge side. The buccaneers turned at once and ran, separating in every direction, so that in half a minute not a sign of them remained but Pew. Him they had deserted, whether in panic or revenge for his blows I know not; but there he remained, tapping up and down the road in a frenzy, groping and calling for his comrades. He took a wrong turn and ran past me, crying, "Johnny, Black Dog, Dirk — you won't leave old Pew, mates, not old Pew!"

Just then four or five riders came in sight in the moonlight and swept at full gallop down the slope. Pew saw his error, turned with a scream, and ran straight under the nearest of the coming horses. The rider tried to save him, but in vain. Down went Pew with a cry that rang high into the night; and the four hoofs trampled and spurned him and passed by. He fell on his side, then gently collapsed upon his face and moved no more.

I leaped to my feet and hailed the riders. They were revenue officers, summoned by the lad sent for the doctor; news of the lugger in Kitt's Hole had set them forth that night, and to that we owed our preservation. Pew was dead, stone dead. My mother, carried up to the hamlet and revived with cold water and salts, was none the worse for her terror, though she still continued to deplore the balance of the money. The officers rode on to Kitt's Hole, but the lugger was already standing out; a bullet whistled past their hail, and soon she doubled the point and disappeared.

Back at the Admiral Benbow, you cannot imagine such a state of smash; the very clock had been thrown down. Though nothing had been taken but the captain's money-bag and a little silver from the till, I could see at once that we were ruined.

"They got the money — what in fortune were they after? More money?"

"No, sir; not money, I think," I replied. "I believe I have the thing in my breast pocket; and I should like to get it put in safety."

Within the hour we were on the road to Dr. Livesey's house.

The Captain's Papers

We rode hard till we drew up before Dr. Livesey's door. The maid told us the doctor had gone up to the hall to dine and pass the evening with the squire.

The distance was short. We rode up the long, leafless, moonlit avenue, and were admitted at a word and led down a matted passage into a great library, all lined with bookcases, where the squire and Dr. Livesey sat, pipe in hand, on either side of a bright fire.

I had never seen the squire so near. A tall man, over six feet, broad in proportion, with a bluff face roughened and reddened by long travel; his black eyebrows moved readily, giving him a look of temper, not bad, you would say, but quick and high.

"Good evening, friend Jim," said the doctor with a nod. "What good wind brings you here?"

The story was told like a lesson; and you should have seen how the two gentlemen leaned forward and forgot to smoke in their interest. When they heard how my mother went back to the inn, Dr. Livesey slapped his thigh, and the squire cried "Bravo!" and broke his long pipe against the grate.

"As for riding down that black miscreant," said the squire, "I regard it as an act of virtue, sir, like stamping on a cockroach. This lad Hawkins is a trump."

"And so, Jim," said the doctor, "you have the thing they were after?"

"Here it is, sir," said I, and gave him the oilskin packet. The doctor put it quietly in the pocket of his coat.

"Squire, I mean to keep Jim Hawkins here to sleep at my house. With your permission, we'll have up the cold pie and let him sup."

I made a hearty supper, hungry as a hawk.

"And now, squire," said the doctor.

"And now, Livesey," said the squire in the same breath.

"One at a time," laughed Dr. Livesey. "You have heard of this Flint, I suppose?"

"Heard of him!" cried the squire. "He was the bloodthirstiest buccaneer that sailed. Blackbeard was a child to Flint. The Spaniards were so prodigiously afraid of him that, I tell you, sir, I was sometimes proud he was an Englishman."

"Well, I've heard of him myself. But the point is, had he money?"

"Money! What were these villains after but money? For what would they risk their rascal carcasses but money?"

"Supposing that I have here in my pocket some clue to where Flint buried his treasure, will that treasure amount to much?"

"Amount, sir! If we have the clue you talk about, I fit out a ship in Bristol dock, and take you and Hawkins along, and I'll have that treasure if I search a year."

"Very well. If Jim is agreeable, we'll open the packet."

The bundle was sewn together, and the doctor cut the stitches with his medical scissors. It contained two things — a book and a sealed paper.

The first page of the book bore only scraps of writing — "Billy Bones his fancy," "Mr. W. Bones, mate," "No more rum," "Off Palm Key he got itt." I could not help wondering who had "got itt," and what "itt" was. A knife in his back, as like as not.

The next pages were a curious series of entries: a date at one end of the line, a sum of money at the other, and varying numbers of crosses between. The record lasted nearly twenty years, and at the end stood a grand total: "Bones, his pile."

"I can't make head or tail of this," said Dr. Livesey.

"Clear as noonday," cried the squire. "This is the black-hearted hound's account-book. The crosses stand for ships or towns they sank or plundered; the sums are the scoundrel's share. And the amounts increase, you see, as he rose in rank."

"Right!" said the doctor. "And now for the other."

The paper had been sealed with a thimble — that very thimble, perhaps, I had found in the captain's pocket. The doctor cut the seals with great care, and out fell the map of an island, with latitude and longitude, soundings, names of hills and bays. It was about nine miles long and five across, shaped like a fat dragon standing up, with two fine land-locked harbours, and a hill in the centre marked "The Spy-glass." Three crosses of red ink — two on the north part of the island, one in the southwest — and beside this last, in a small, neat hand very different from the captain's tottery characters: "Bulk of treasure here."

On the back the same hand had added bearings — tall tree on Spy-glass shoulder, Skeleton Island east-southeast, ten feet — and signed, J.F.

That was all; but brief as it was, it filled the squire and Dr. Livesey with delight.

"Livesey," said the squire, "you will give up this wretched practice at once. Tomorrow I start for Bristol. In three weeks' time — three weeks! — two weeks — ten days — we'll have the best ship, sir, and the choicest crew in England. Hawkins shall come as cabin-boy. You'll make a famous cabin-boy, Hawkins. You, Livesey, are ship's doctor; I am admiral. We'll have favourable winds, a quick passage, and not the least difficulty in finding the spot, and money to eat, to roll in, to play duck and drake with ever after."

"Trelawney," said the doctor, "I'll go with you; and I'll go bail for it, so will Jim, and be a credit to the undertaking. There's only one man I'm afraid of."

"And who's that?"

"You; for you cannot hold your tongue. We are not the only men who know of this paper. These fellows who attacked the inn tonight, and the rest who stayed aboard that lugger, and more, I dare say, not far off, are, one and all, bound that they'll get that money. From first to last, not one of us must breathe a word of what we've found."

"Livesey," returned the squire, "you are always in the right of it. I'll be as silent as the grave."

It was longer than the squire imagined ere we were ready for the sea. The doctor had to go to London; the squire was hard at work at Bristol; and I lived on at the hall under the charge of old Redruth, the gamekeeper, almost a prisoner, but full of sea-dreams. I brooded by the hour together over the map. I climbed a thousand times to that tall hill they call the Spy-glass, and from the top enjoyed the most wonderful and changing prospects. Sometimes the isle was thick with savages, sometimes full of dangerous animals; but in all my fancies nothing occurred to me so strange and tragic as our actual adventures.

So the weeks passed, till one fine day a letter came addressed to Dr. Livesey, with this addition, "To be opened, in the case of his absence, by Tom Redruth or young Hawkins." I read:

*Old Anchor Inn, Bristol, March 1, 17—.*

*Dear Livesey—The ship is bought and fitted. She lies at anchor, ready for sea. You never imagined a sweeter schooner—a child might sail her—two hundred tons; name, HISPANIOLA.*

*I wished a round score of men—in case of natives, buccaneers, or the odious French—and I had the worry of the deuce itself to find so much as half a dozen, till the most remarkable stroke of fortune brought me the very man that I required. I was standing on the dock, when, by the merest accident, I fell in talk with him. I found he was an old sailor, kept a public-house, knew all the seafaring men in Bristol, had lost his health ashore, and wanted a good berth as cook to get to sea again. I was monstrously touched—and, out of pure pity, I engaged him on the spot to be ship's cook. Long John Silver, he is called, and has lost a leg; but that I regarded as a recommendation, since he lost it in his country's service, under the immortal Hawke.*

*Well, sir, I thought I had only found a cook, but it was a crew I had discovered. Between Silver and myself we got together in a few days a company of the toughest old salts imaginable—not pretty to look at, but fellows, by their faces, of the most indomitable spirit. I declare we could fight a frigate.*

*I am in the most magnificent health and spirits, eating like a bull, sleeping like a tree, yet I shall not enjoy a moment till I hear my old tarpaulins tramping round the capstan. Seaward, ho! Hang the treasure! It's the glory of the sea that has turned my head. So now, Livesey, come post; do not lose an hour, if you respect me.*

*Let young Hawkins go at once to see his mother, with Redruth for a guard; and then both come full speed to Bristol.*

*John Trelawney*

You can fancy the excitement into which that letter put me. The next morning Redruth and I set out on foot for the Admiral Benbow, where I found my mother in good health and spirits. The captain was gone where the wicked cease from troubling. The squire had had everything repaired and the sign repainted, and had found her a boy as an apprentice so that she should not want help while I was gone.

It was on seeing that boy that I understood, for the first time, my situation. I had thought up to that moment of the adventures before me, not at all of the home that I was leaving; and now, at sight of this clumsy stranger who was to stay here in my place beside my mother, I had my first attack of tears.

The next day, after dinner, Redruth and I were afoot again. I said good-bye to Mother and the cove where I had lived since I was born, and the dear old Admiral Benbow—since he was repainted, no longer quite so dear. One of my last thoughts was of the captain, who had so often strode along the beach with his cocked hat, his sabre-cut cheek, and his old brass telescope. Next moment we had turned the corner and my home was out of sight.

The mail picked us up about dusk, and I slept like a log up hill and down dale until I was awakened by a punch in the ribs and opened my eyes to find we were standing still before a large building in a city street and the day had broken.

"Where are we?" I asked.

"Bristol," said Tom. "Get down."

Mr. Trelawney had taken up his residence at an inn far down the docks, and our way lay along the quays beside the great multitude of ships of all sizes and rigs and nations. Though I had lived by the shore all my life, I seemed never to have been near the sea till then. The smell of tar and salt was something new. I saw the most wonderful figureheads, that had all been far over the ocean. I saw old sailors with rings in their ears and tarry pigtails; and if I had seen as many kings or archbishops I could not have been more delighted.

And I was going to sea myself, to sea in a schooner, with a piping boatswain and pig-tailed singing seamen, to sea, bound for an unknown island, and to seek for buried treasure!

While I was still in this delightful dream, we came suddenly in front of a large inn and met Squire Trelawney, all dressed out like a sea-officer, in stout blue cloth, coming out of the door with a smile on his face and a capital imitation of a sailor's walk.

"Here you are," he cried, "and the doctor came last night from London. Bravo! The ship's company complete!"

"Oh, sir," cried I, "when do we sail?"

"Sail!" says he. "We sail tomorrow!"

When I had done breakfasting the squire gave me a note addressed to John Silver, at the sign of the Spy-glass, and told me I should easily find the place by following the docks and looking out for a little tavern with a large brass telescope for sign. I set off, overjoyed at this opportunity to see more of the ships and seamen.

The Spy-glass was a bright enough little place. The sign was newly painted; the windows had neat red curtains; the floor was cleanly sanded. The customers were mostly seafaring men, and they talked so loudly that I hung at the door, almost afraid to enter.

As I was waiting, a man came out of a side room, and at a glance I was sure he must be Long John. His left leg was cut off close by the hip, and under the left shoulder he carried a crutch, which he managed with wonderful dexterity, hopping about upon it like a bird. He was very tall and strong, with a face as big as a ham—plain and pale, but intelligent and smiling.

Now, from the very first mention of Long John in Squire Trelawney's letter I had taken a fear in my mind that he might prove to be the very one-legged sailor whom I had watched for so long at the old Benbow. But one look at the man before me was enough. I had seen the captain, and Black Dog, and the blind man, Pew, and I thought I knew what a buccaneer was like—a very different creature, according to me, from this clean and pleasant-tempered landlord.

I plucked up courage at once, crossed the threshold, and walked right up to him.

"Mr. Silver, sir?" I asked, holding out the note.

"Yes, my lad," said he; "such is my name, to be sure. And who may you be?" And then as he saw the squire's letter, he seemed to give something almost like a start. "Oh!" said he, quite loud, and offering his hand. "I see. You are our new cabin-boy; pleased I am to see you."

Just then one of the customers at the far side rose suddenly and made for the door. He was out in the street in a moment. But his hurry had attracted my notice, and I recognized him at a glance. It was the tallow-faced man, wanting two fingers, who had come first to the Admiral Benbow.

"Oh," I cried, "stop him! It's Black Dog!"

"I don't care two coppers who he is," cried Silver. "But he hasn't paid his score. Harry, run and catch him."

One of the others leaped up and started in pursuit.

"If he were Admiral Hawke he shall pay his score," cried Silver. "Who did you say he was? Black what?"

"Dog, sir," said I. "Has Mr. Trelawney not told you of the buccaneers? He was one of them."

"So?" cried Silver. "In my house!" And as he ran on—stumping up and down the tavern on his crutch, slapping tables with his hand, and giving such a show of excitement as would have convinced an Old Bailey judge—my suspicions had been thoroughly reawakened. But he was too deep, and too ready, and too clever for me, and by the time the men returned out of breath and confessed they had lost the track in a crowd, I would have gone bail for the innocence of Long John Silver.

"See here, now, Hawkins," said he, "here's a blessed hard thing on a man like me. Here I have this confounded son of a Dutchman sitting in my own house drinking of my own rum! What could I do, with this old timber I hobble on? When I was an A B master mariner I'd have come up alongside of him, hand over hand, and broached him to in a brace of old shakes, I would; but now—"

And then suddenly he stopped, and his jaw dropped. "The score!" he burst out. "Three goes o' rum! Why, shiver my timbers, if I hadn't forgotten my score!"

And falling on a bench, he laughed until the tears ran down his cheeks. I could not help joining, and we laughed together until the tavern rang again.

On our walk along the quays, he made himself the most interesting companion, telling me about the different ships, their rig and tonnage, and every now and then telling me some little anecdote of ships or seamen or repeating a nautical phrase till I had learned it perfectly. I began to see that here was one of the best of possible shipmates.

When we got to the inn, the squire and Dr. Livesey were seated together, finishing a quart of ale. Long John told the story from first to last, with a great deal of spirit and the most perfect truth. "That was how it were, now, weren't it, Hawkins?" he would say, now and again, and I could always bear him entirely out.

The two gentlemen regretted that Black Dog had got away, but we all agreed there was nothing to be done; and after he had been complimented, Long John took up his crutch and departed.

"Well, squire," said Dr. Livesey, "I don't put much faith in your discoveries, as a general thing; but I will say this, John Silver suits me."

"The man's a perfect trump," declared the squire.

The Hispaniola lay some way out, and we went under the figureheads and round the sterns of many other ships. We were saluted as we stepped aboard by the mate, an old sailor who was thick and friendly with the squire. But things were not the same between Mr. Trelawney and the captain.

This last was a sharp-looking man who seemed angry with everything on board, and we had hardly got down into the cabin when a sailor announced him.

"Well, Captain Smollett, what have you to say? All shipshape and seaworthy?"

"Well, sir," said the captain, "better speak plain, even at the risk of offence. I don't like this cruise; I don't like the men; and I don't like my officer. That's short and sweet."

"Perhaps, sir, you don't like the ship?" inquired the squire, very angry.

"I can't speak as to that, sir, not having seen her tried. She seems a clever craft; more I can't say."

"Possibly, sir, you may not like your employer, either?"

But here Dr. Livesey cut in. "Stay a bit. No use of such questions but to produce ill feeling. The captain has said too much or he has said too little, and I require an explanation. You don't, you say, like this cruise. Now, why?"

"I was engaged, sir, on what we call sealed orders, to sail this ship for that gentleman where he should bid me. So far so good. But now I find that every man before the mast knows more than I do. I don't call that fair, now, do you?"

"No," said Dr. Livesey, "I don't."

"Next, I learn we are going after treasure—hear it from my own hands. Now, treasure is ticklish work; I don't like treasure voyages on any account, and I don't like them, above all, when they are secret and when (begging your pardon, Mr. Trelawney) the secret has been told to the parrot."

"Silver's parrot?"

"It's a way of speaking. Blabbed, I mean. It's my belief neither of you gentlemen know what you are about, but I'll tell you my way of it—life or death, and a close run."

"That is all clear, and, I dare say, true enough," replied Dr. Livesey. "Next, you say you don't like the crew. Are they not good seamen?"

"I don't like them, sir. And I think I should have had the choosing of my own hands. As for my mate, I believe him a good seaman, but he's too free with the crew to be a good officer."

"Well, now, the short and long of it, captain?" asked the doctor. "Tell us what you want."

"Are you determined to go on this cruise?"

"Like iron," answered the squire.

"Very good. Then hear me a few words more. They are putting the powder and the arms in the fore hold. You have a good place under the cabin; why not put them there? Then, you are bringing four of your own people, and they tell me some are to be berthed forward. Why not give them the berths here beside the cabin?"

"Any more?" asked Mr. Trelawney.

"One more. There's been too much blabbing already. I'll tell you what I've heard myself: that you have a map of an island, that there's crosses on the map to show where treasure is, and that the island lies—" And then he named the latitude and longitude exactly.

"I never told that," cried the squire, "to a soul!"

"The hands know it, sir."

"Livesey, that must have been you or Hawkins!"

"It doesn't much matter who it was," replied the doctor. And neither he nor the captain paid much regard to Mr. Trelawney's protestations.

"I make it a point," continued the captain, "the map shall be kept secret even from me. Otherwise I would ask you to let me resign."

"I see," said the doctor. "You wish us to keep this matter dark and to make a garrison of the stern part of the ship. In other words, you fear a mutiny."

"Sir," said Captain Smollett, "with no intention to take offence, I deny your right to put words into my mouth. No captain would be justified in going to sea at all if he had ground enough to say that. But I am responsible for the ship's safety and the life of every man Jack aboard of her. I see things going, as I think, not quite right. And I ask you to take certain precautions or let me resign my berth."

"Captain Smollett," began the doctor with a smile, "did ever you hear the fable of the mountain and the mouse? When you came in here, I'll stake my wig, you meant more than this."

"Doctor, you are smart. When I came in here I meant to get discharged. I had no thought that Mr. Trelawney would hear a word."

"No more I would," cried the squire. "Had Livesey not been here I should have seen you to the deuce. As it is, I will do as you desire, but I think the worse of you."

"That's as you please, sir. You'll find I do my duty."

And with that he took his leave.

"Trelawney," said the doctor, "contrary to all my notions, I believe you have managed to get two honest men on board with you—that man and John Silver."

"Silver, if you like," cried the squire; "but as for that intolerable humbug, I declare I think his conduct unmanly, unsailorly, and downright un-English."

"Well," says the doctor, "we shall see."

When we came on deck, the men had begun already to take out the arms and powder, yo-ho-ing at their work. The whole schooner had been overhauled; six berths had been made astern, and Redruth and I were to get two of them, while the captain and his mate were to sleep on deck in the companion.

We were all hard at work when Long John came off in a shore-boat. The cook came up the side like a monkey for cleverness. "So ho, mates! What's this?"

"We're a-changing of the powder, Jack."

"Why, by the powers," cried Long John, "if we do, we'll miss the morning tide!"

"My orders!" said the captain shortly. "You may go below, my man."

"Aye, aye, sir," answered the cook, and touching his forelock, he disappeared at once toward his galley.

"That's a good man, captain," said the doctor.

"Very likely, sir." And then suddenly observing me examining the swivel we carried amidships, "Here you, ship's boy," he cried, "out o' that! Off with you to the cook and get some work."

And then as I was hurrying off I heard him say, quite loudly, "I'll have no favourites on my ship."

I assure you I was quite of the squire's way of thinking, and hated the captain deeply.

The Voyage

That night we were in great bustle stowing things, with the squire's friends coming off to wish him good voyage. I was dog-tired when, before dawn, the boatswain sounded his pipe and the crew manned the capstan-bars. Yet I would not have left the deck for anything, all was so new—the brief commands, the shrill whistle, the men bustling to their places in the lantern-glimmer.

"Now, Barbecue, tip us a stave," cried one voice.

"The old one," cried another.

"Aye, aye, mates," said Long John, who stood by with his crutch, and broke out in the air I knew so well:

"Fifteen men on the dead man's chest—"

And the whole crew bore chorus:

"Yo-ho-ho, and a bottle of rum!"

At the third "Ho!" they drove the bars before them with a will. It carried me back to the old Admiral Benbow in a second; I seemed to hear the captain piping in the chorus. But soon the anchor was short up; the sails began to draw, the land flit by on either side, and before I could lie down to snatch an hour of sleep the Hispaniola had begun her voyage to the Isle of Treasure.

I shall not relate that voyage in detail. The ship proved good, the crew capable, the captain understood his business. But before we came the length of Treasure Island, certain things happened which require to be known.

The mate proved a worse drunkard than the captain had feared, until one dark night with a head sea he disappeared overboard and was seen no more. "Well, gentlemen," said Captain Smollett, "that saves the trouble of putting him in irons." The boatswain served thereafter as mate; Mr. Trelawney, who had followed the sea, took a watch in easy weather; and the coxswain, Israel Hands, a careful, wily, experienced seaman, could be trusted at a pinch with almost anything. He was a great confidant of Long John Silver, and so the mention of his name leads me to speak of our ship's cook, Barbecue, as the men called him.

Aboard ship Silver carried his crutch by a lanyard round his neck, to have both hands free. It was something to see him wedge the foot of the crutch against a bulkhead and, propped against it, get on with his cooking like someone safe ashore. He had lines rigged to help him across the widest spaces—Long John's earrings, they were called—and he handed himself from one place to another as quickly as another man could walk. All the crew respected and even obeyed him. He had a way with each, doing everybody some particular service. To me he was unweariedly kind, and always glad to see me in the galley, which he kept clean as a new pin, his parrot in a cage in the corner.

"Come away, Hawkins," he would say; "come and have a yarn with John. Here's Cap'n Flint—I calls my parrot Cap'n Flint, after the famous buccaneer—predicting success to our v'yage. Wasn't you, Cap'n?"

And the parrot would say, with great rapidity, "Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight!" till you wondered it was not out of breath, or till John threw his handkerchief over the cage.

"Now, that bird," he would say, "is maybe two hundred years old, Hawkins—they live forever mostly; and if anybody's seen more wickedness, it must be the devil himself. She's sailed with England, the great Cap'n England. But you can't touch pitch and not be mucked, lad. She would swear the same, in a manner of speaking, before chaplain." And John would touch his forelock with a solemn way that made me think him the best of men.

Meanwhile the squire and Captain Smollett kept on distant terms. The squire despised the captain; the captain spoke only when spoken to, sharp and short and dry. He owned, when driven into a corner, that he had been wrong about the crew. As for the ship, he had taken a downright fancy to her. "She'll lie a point nearer the wind than a man has a right to expect of his own married wife, sir. But all I say is, we're not home again, and I don't like the cruise."

The squire would turn away and march the deck, chin in air. "A trifle more of that man, and I shall explode."

There was always a barrel of apples broached in the waist for any man with a fancy. "Never knew good come of it yet," the captain said to Dr. Livesey. "Spoil forecastle hands, make devils." But good did come of it, for if it had not been for the apple barrel we should have had no note of warning, and might all have perished by the hand of treachery.

This was how it came about. We had run up the trades and now were running down for the island, with a bright lookout day and night; some time that night, or before noon of the morrow, we should sight Treasure Island. Just after sundown, when my work was over, it occurred to me I should like an apple. I ran on deck. The watch was forward, looking out for land; the man at the helm whistled gently; the only sound besides was the swish of the sea against the bows.

In I got bodily into the barrel, and found scarce an apple left; but sitting there in the dark, what with the sound of the waters and the rocking of the ship, I had either fallen asleep or was on the point of doing so when a heavy man sat down with a clash close by. The barrel shook as he leaned his shoulders against it, and I was just about to jump up when the man began to speak. It was Silver's voice; and before I had heard a dozen words I would not have shown myself for all the world, but lay there trembling and listening, in the extreme of fear and curiosity, for from those dozen words I understood that the lives of all the honest men aboard depended upon me alone.

What I Heard in the Apple-Barrel

"No, not I," said Silver. "Flint was cap'n; I was quartermaster, along of my timber leg. The same broadside I lost my leg, old Pew lost his deadlights. Now, what a ship was christened, so let her stay, I says. So it was with the old Walrus, Flint's old ship, as I've seen amuck with the red blood and fit to sink with gold."

"Ah!" cried another voice—the youngest hand on board, full of admiration. "He was the flower of the flock, was Flint!"

"I laid by nine hundred safe, from England, and two thousand after Flint. That ain't bad for a man before the mast—all safe in bank. 'Tain't earning now, it's saving does it, you may lay to that. Where's all England's men now? Where's Flint's? Why, most on 'em aboard here, and glad to get the duff. Old Pew, as had lost his sight, spent twelve hundred pound in a year like a lord; but for two year before he died, by the powers, the man was starving!"

"Well, it ain't much use, after all," said the young seaman.

"'Tain't much use for fools," cried Silver. "But you look here: you're young, but you're as smart as paint. I'll talk to you like a man."

You may imagine how I felt to hear this abominable old rogue addressing another in the very same words of flattery he had used to me. I think, if I had been able, I would have killed him through the barrel. Meantime he ran on, little supposing he was overheard.

"Here it is about gentlemen of fortune. They lives rough, and risk swinging, but they eat and drink like fighting-cocks, and when a cruise is done it's hundreds of pounds in their pockets. Now, the most goes for rum and a good fling, and to sea again in their shirts. But that's not the course I lay. I puts it all away, some here, some there, and none too much anywheres. I'm fifty, mark you; once back from this cruise, I set up gentleman in earnest."

"And can you trust your missis?" asked the other.

"Gentlemen of fortune," returned the cook, "usually trusts little among themselves, and right they are. But I have a way with me, I have. There was some that was feared of Pew, and some that was feared of Flint; but Flint his own self was feared of me. Ah, you may be sure of yourself in old John's ship."

"Well," replied the lad, "I didn't half a quarter like the job till I had this talk with you, John; but there's my hand on it."

"And a brave lad, and smart too," answered Silver, shaking hands so heartily that the barrel shook.

By this time I had begun to understand. By a "gentleman of fortune" they plainly meant neither more nor less than a common pirate, and the little scene I had overheard was the last act in the corruption of one of the honest hands—perhaps of the last left aboard. But on this point I was soon relieved, for Silver giving a little whistle, a third man strolled up.

"Dick's square," said Silver.

"Oh, I know'd Dick was square," returned the voice of Israel Hands. "He's no fool, is Dick." And he turned his quid and spat. "But look here: how long are we a-going to stand off and on like a blessed bumboat? I've had a'most enough o' Cap'n Smollett. I want to go into that cabin, I do. I want their pickles and wines."

"Israel," said Silver, "your head ain't much account, nor ever was. But you'll berth forward, and you'll live hard, and you'll speak soft, and you'll keep sober till I give the word; you may lay to that, my son."

"Well, I don't say no," growled the coxswain. "What I say is, when?"

"When! By the powers!" cried Silver. "The last moment I can manage. Here's a first-rate seaman, Cap'n Smollett, sails the blessed ship for us. Here's this squire and doctor with a map and such—I don't know where it is. Well then, I mean this squire and doctor shall find the stuff, and help us get it aboard. Then we'll see. If I was sure of you all, I'd have Cap'n Smollett navigate us half-way back again before I struck."

"Why, we're all seamen aboard here, I should think," said Dick.

"We're all forecastle hands, you mean," snapped Silver. "We can steer a course, but who's to set one? I'll finish with 'em at the island, as soon's the blunt's on board, and a pity it is."

"But," asked Dick, "when we do lay 'em athwart, what are we to do with 'em?"

"There's the man for me!" cried the cook admiringly. "Put 'em ashore like maroons? That would have been England's way. Or cut 'em down like that much pork? That would have been Flint's, or Billy Bones's."

"Billy was the man for that," said Israel. "'Dead men don't bite,' says he."

"Right you are," said Silver; "rough and ready. But mark you, this time it's serious. Dooty is dooty, mates. I give my vote—death. When I'm in Parlyment and riding in my coach, I don't want none of these sea-lawyers in the cabin a-coming home, unlooked for, like the devil at prayers. Wait is what I say; but when the time comes, why, let her rip!"

"John," cries the coxswain, "you're a man!"

"You'll say so, Israel, when you see," said Silver. "Only one thing I claim—I claim Trelawney. I'll wring his calf's head off his body with these hands. Dick," he added, "you just jump up and get me an apple, to wet my pipe like."

You may fancy the terror I was in! I should have leaped out and run if I had found the strength, but my limbs and heart alike misgave me. I heard Dick begin to rise, when Hands exclaimed, "Oh, stow that! Don't you get sucking of that bilge, John. Let's have a go of the rum."

"Dick," said Silver, "I trust you. There's the key; you fill a pannikin and bring it up."

Terrified as I was, I could not help thinking this must have been how the mate got the strong waters that destroyed him.

When Dick returned, one after another took the pannikin and drank—one "To luck," another "Here's to old Flint," and Silver himself, in a kind of song, "Here's to ourselves, and hold your luff, plenty of prizes and plenty of duff."

Just then a brightness fell upon me in the barrel; looking up, I found the moon had risen and was silvering the mizzen-top. Almost at the same moment the lookout shouted, "Land ho!"

Council of War

There was a great rush of feet across the deck. Slipping outside my barrel, I dived behind the fore-sail, doubled toward the stern, and came out upon the open deck in time to join the rush for the weather bow.

A belt of fog had lifted with the moon. Away to the south-west we saw two low hills about a couple of miles apart, and rising behind one of them a third and higher, its peak still buried in fog—all three sharp and conical. So much I saw, almost in a dream, for I had not yet recovered from my horrid fear. Then I heard Captain Smollett issuing orders. The Hispaniola was laid a couple of points nearer the wind, on a course to clear the island on the east.

"Now, men," said the captain, "has any one of you ever seen that land ahead?"

"I have, sir," said Silver. "I've watered there with a trader I was cook in. Skeleton Island they calls it. Three hills running south'ard—fore, main, and mizzen. The main, with the cloud on it, they usually calls the Spy-glass, by reason of a lookout they kept when they was in the anchorage cleaning."

"I have a chart here," says Captain Smollett. "See if that's the place."

Long John's eyes burned in his head as he took the chart, but by the fresh look of the paper I knew he was doomed to disappointment. This was not the map from Billy Bones's chest, but an accurate copy, complete in all things—except for the red crosses and the written notes. Sharp as must have been his annoyance, Silver had the strength of mind to hide it.

"Yes, sir," said he, "this is the spot, very prettily drawed out. Right you was, sir, to haul your wind and keep the weather of the island."

"Thank you, my man," says Captain Smollett. "You may go."

I was surprised at the coolness with which John avowed his knowledge, and half-frightened when I saw him drawing nearer to me. He did not know I had overheard from the apple barrel, yet I had taken such a horror of his cruelty and duplicity that I could scarce conceal a shudder when he laid his hand upon my arm.

"Ah," says he, "this here is a sweet spot for a lad to get ashore on. You'll bathe, and climb trees, and hunt goats. Why, it makes me young again. When you want to go a bit of exploring, you just ask old John, and he'll put up a snack for you to take along."

And clapping me in the friendliest way upon the shoulder, he hobbled off forward.

Captain Smollett, the squire, and Dr. Livesey were talking on the quarter-deck; anxious as I was to tell them, I durst not interrupt openly. While I was casting about for some excuse, Dr. Livesey called me to his side, as if to fetch his pipe; but as soon as I was near enough to speak unheard, I broke out: "Doctor, let me speak. Get the captain and squire down to the cabin, and then make some pretence to send for me. I have terrible news."

The doctor changed countenance a little, but next moment was master of himself. "Thank you, Jim," said he loudly, "that was all I wanted to know," as if he had asked a question.

He rejoined the others. Soon the captain piped all hands on deck.

"My lads," said Captain Smollett, "this land we have sighted is the place we have been sailing for. Mr. Trelawney, being a very open-handed gentleman, has just asked me a word or two; and as I was able to tell him every man on board has done his duty as I never ask to see better, why, he and I and the doctor are going below to drink your health and luck, and you'll have grog served out to drink ours. I think this handsome. And if you think as I do, you'll give a good sea-cheer for the gentleman that does it."

The cheer followed; but it rang out so full and hearty that I confess I could hardly believe these same men were plotting for our blood.

"One more cheer for Cap'n Smollett," cried Long John, and this also was given with a will.

Soon I was sent for to the cabin. The three sat round the table, a bottle of Spanish wine and some raisins before them, the doctor smoking with his wig on his lap—a sign, I knew, that he was agitated.

"Now, Hawkins," said the squire, "speak up."

I told the whole of Silver's conversation, as short as I could make it. Nobody interrupted, but kept their eyes on my face from first to last.

"Jim," said Dr. Livesey, "take a seat." They made me sit beside them, poured me wine, and all three, each with a bow, drank my health and their service to me, for my luck and courage.

"Now, captain," said the squire, "you were right, and I was wrong. I own myself an ass, and I await your orders."

"No more an ass than I, sir," returned the captain. "I never heard of a crew that meant to mutiny but what showed signs before. But this crew beats me."

"Captain," said the doctor, "that's Silver. A very remarkable man."

"He'd look remarkably well from a yard-arm, sir," returned the captain. "But this don't lead to anything. First point: we must go on, because we can't turn back. If I gave the word to go about, they would rise at once. Second, we have time—at least until this treasure's found. Third, there are faithful hands. It's got to come to blows sooner or later, and what I propose is to come to blows some fine day when they least expect it. We can count on your own home servants, Mr. Trelawney?"

"As upon myself," declared the squire.

"Three; ourselves make seven, counting Hawkins here."

"And to think they're all Englishmen!" broke out the squire. "Sir, I could find it in my heart to blow the ship up."

"Well, gentlemen," said the captain, "the best I can say is not much. We must lay to and keep a bright lookout. It would be pleasanter to come to blows. But there's no help for it till we know our men. Lay to, and whistle for a wind, that's my view."

"Jim here," said the doctor, "can help us more than anyone. The men are not shy with him, and Jim is a noticing lad."

"Hawkins, I put prodigious faith in you," added the squire.

I began to feel pretty desperate, for I felt altogether helpless; and yet, by an odd train of circumstances, it was indeed through me that safety came. Meantime, there were only seven out of the twenty-six on whom we knew we could rely; and out of these seven one was a boy, so the grown men on our side were six to their nineteen.

How I Began My Shore Adventure

The appearance of the island when I came on deck next morning was altogether changed. The breeze had ceased; we lay becalmed half a mile to the south-east of the low eastern coast. Grey-coloured woods covered most of the surface, broken by streaks of yellow sand-break and pines, but the colouring was uniform and sad. The hills ran up in spires of naked rock, and the Spy-glass, the tallest, was the strangest in configuration—running up sheer and then suddenly cut off at the top like a pedestal.

The HISPANIOLA was rolling scuppers under in the swell, creaking and jumping like a manufactory; I clung to the backstay, queasy.

Whether for that or for the look of the island—grey, melancholy woods, wild stone spires, surf thundering on the steep beach—my heart sank into my boots; and from that first look, I hated the very thought of Treasure Island.

There was a dreary morning's work before us. The boats had to be got out and the ship warped round the corner into the haven behind Skeleton Island. I volunteered for one of the boats, where I had no business. The heat was sweltering; the men grumbled fiercely. The boatswain in command of my boat grumbled as loud as the worst. "Well," he said with an oath, "it's not forever." Up to that day the men had gone briskly about their business; the very sight of the island had relaxed the cords of discipline.

Long John stood by the steersman and conned the ship, knowing the passage like the palm of his hand. We brought up just where the anchor was in the chart, about a third of a mile from each shore.

The place was entirely land-locked, buried in woods, the trees coming right down to high-water mark. Two little rivers emptied into this pond; the foliage had a kind of poisonous brightness. There was not a breath of air, nor a sound but the surf booming half a mile away. A peculiar stagnant smell hung over the anchorage—of sodden leaves and rotting tree trunks. The doctor sniffed and sniffed, like one tasting a bad egg.

"I don't know about treasure," he said, "but I'll stake my wig there's fever here."

The men's conduct, alarming in the boat, became truly threatening when they came aboard. They lay about the deck growling, every order received with a black look. Mutiny hung over us like a thunder-cloud.

Long John was hard at work going from group to group, all smiles, with the cheeriest "Aye, aye, sir!" in the world; he kept up one song after another, as if to conceal the discontent of the rest. Of all the gloomy features of that gloomy afternoon, this obvious anxiety on his part appeared the worst.

We held a council in the cabin.

"Sir," said the captain, "if I risk another order, the whole ship'll come about our ears. We've only one man to rely on—Silver. He's as anxious as we to smother things up. Let's allow the men an afternoon ashore. If they all go, we'll fight the ship. If none go, we hold the cabin. If some go, mark my words, Silver'll bring 'em aboard again as mild as lambs."

It was so decided. Loaded pistols were served to the sure men; the captain went on deck and offered the crew a turn ashore. They cheered like fellows who thought they'd break their shins over treasure as soon as landed.

The captain whipped out of sight, leaving Silver to arrange the party. Silver was the captain, and a mighty rebellious crew he had of it.

Six were to stay on board; the remaining thirteen, including Silver, began to embark. Then came the first of the mad notions that contributed so much to save our lives. With six left, our party could not take the ship; with only six left, the cabin had no present need of me. In a jiffy I had slipped over the side and curled up in the fore-sheets of the nearest boat, and almost at the same moment she shoved off.

No one took notice of me, only the bow oar saying, "Is that you, Jim? Keep your head down." But Silver, from the other boat, called sharply to know if that were me; and from that moment I began to regret what I had done.

The crews raced for the beach, but my boat shot far ahead. The bow struck among the shore-side trees; I caught a branch, swung myself out, and plunged into the nearest thicket while Silver and the rest were still a hundred yards behind.

"Jim, Jim!" I heard him shouting.

But I paid no heed; jumping, ducking, breaking through, I ran straight before my nose till I could run no longer.

The First Blow

I was so pleased at having given the slip to Long John that I began to enjoy myself. I had crossed a marshy tract and come out upon open sandy country dotted with pines and contorted oak-like trees. For the first time I felt the joy of exploration. Snakes hissed at me; little did I suppose one was a deadly enemy and the noise the famous rattle.

I came to a thicket of evergreen oaks that stretched down to a broad, reedy fen. All at once a wild duck flew up, and soon the whole surface was a cloud of birds screaming and circling. Some shipmates must be drawing near. Presently I heard the distant tones of a human voice; I crawled under cover of the nearest live-oak, silent as a mouse.

Another voice answered, and the first—Silver's—took up the story. The least I could do, having come ashore so foolhardy, was to overhear. Crawling on all fours, I made towards them, till raising my head to an aperture among the leaves I saw into a little green dell where Silver and another of the crew stood face to face.

Silver had thrown his hat beside him; his great, smooth, blond face, shining with heat, was lifted to the other man's in a kind of appeal.

"Mate," he was saying, "it's because I thinks gold dust of you—gold dust, and you may lay to that! If I hadn't took to you like pitch, would I be here a-warning of you? All's up—you can't make nor mend; it's to save your neck that I'm a-speaking."

"Silver," said the other—red in the face, hoarse as a crow—"you're old and honest, or has the name for it. Will you tell me you'll let yourself be led away with that mess of swabs? As sure as God sees me, I'd sooner lose my hand. If I turn agin my dooty—"

He was interrupted. Far away in the marsh arose a sound like the cry of anger, then another, and then one horrid, long-drawn scream. The rocks of the Spy-glass re-echoed it; the marsh-birds rose darkening heaven; and long after that death yell still rang in my brain.

Tom had leaped at the sound like a horse at the spur, but Silver had not winked an eye. He stood resting on his crutch, watching his companion like a snake about to spring.

"John!" said the sailor, stretching out his hand.

"Hands off!" cried Silver, leaping back a yard with the speed of a trained gymnast.

"Hands off, if you like, John Silver," said the other. "It's a black conscience that makes you feared of me. But in heaven's name, what was that?"

"That?" returned Silver, smiling away, but warier than ever, his eye a mere pin-point but gleaming like a crumb of glass. "That? Oh, I reckon that'll be Alan."

And at this Tom flashed out like a hero. "Alan!" he cried. "Then rest his soul for a true seaman! And as for you, John Silver, long you've been a mate of mine, but you're mate of mine no more. You've killed Alan, have you? Kill me too, if you can. But I defies you."

With that he turned his back on the cook and set off for the beach. But he was not destined to go far. With a cry John seized a tree branch, whipped his crutch from his armpit, and sent that uncouth missile hurtling through the air. It struck poor Tom point foremost between the shoulders; he gasped and fell. Silver, agile as a monkey even without his leg, was on top of him next moment and had twice buried his knife to the hilt in that defenceless body.

I do not know what it is to faint, but the whole world swam away before me in a whirling mist. When I came to myself the monster had pulled himself together, crutch under arm, hat on head; before him Tom lay motionless on the sward, while the murderer cleansed his blood-stained knife on a wisp of grass.

Then John drew a whistle and blew several modulated blasts that rang far across the heated air. More men would be coming. They had already slain two of the honest people; might not I come next?

I crawled back, then ran as I never ran before, fear growing into a kind of frenzy. When the gun fired, how should I dare go down among those fiends still smoking from their crime? Good-bye to the HISPANIOLA, the squire, the doctor, and the captain. Without noticing, I had drawn near the foot of the little hill with the two peaks; the air smelt fresher; the live-oaks grew more widely apart; scattered pines stood fifty or seventy feet high.

And here a fresh alarm brought me to a standstill with a thumping heart.

The Man of the Island

From the side of the hill, which was steep and stony, a spout of gravel was dislodged and fell rattling through the trees. I saw a figure leap behind the trunk of a pine—dark, shaggy, what I knew not, bear or man or monkey. The terror of this new apparition brought me to a stand. I was now cut off on both sides: behind me the murderers, before me this lurking nondescript. Silver himself appeared less terrible in contrast, and I turned to retrace my steps. Instantly the figure reappeared, circling to head me off; from trunk to trunk it flitted like a deer, manlike on two legs but stooping almost double.

I began to recall what I had heard of cannibals. But the mere fact that he was a man reassured me, and the recollection of my pistol flashed into my mind. With courage glowing again, I walked briskly towards him.

He was concealed behind another tree trunk; but as I moved he reappeared, hesitated, drew back, came forward again, and at last threw himself on his knees and held out his clasped hands in supplication.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"Ben Gunn," he answered, his voice hoarse and awkward like a rusty lock. "I'm poor Ben Gunn, I am; and I haven't spoke with a Christian these three years."

He was a white man like myself, with pleasing features but skin burnt almost black by the sun, his fair eyes startling in so dark a face. He was clothed in tatters of old ship's canvas and sea-cloth, held together by brass buttons, bits of stick, and loops of tarry gaskin; an old brass-buckled belt was the one solid thing in his accoutrement.

"Three years!" I cried. "Were you shipwrecked?"

"Nay, mate," said he; "marooned."

I knew the word for a horrible buccaneer punishment.

"Marooned three years agone," he continued, "and lived on goats since, berries and oysters. My heart is sore for Christian diet. You mightn't have a piece of cheese about you? No? Many's the long night I've dreamed of cheese—toasted, mostly—and woke and here I were."

"If ever I can get aboard again," said I, "you shall have cheese by the stone."

He had been feeling my jacket and smoothing my hands with childish pleasure; but at my last words he perked up slyly.

"If ever you can get aboard again, says you? Why, now, who's to hinder you?"

"Not you, I know."

"And right you was. What's your name, mate?"

"Jim."

"Jim, Jim," says he, pleased. "Well, Jim, I've lived rough as you'd be ashamed to hear. You wouldn't think I had a pious mother—to look at me?"

"Why, no, not in particular."

"Ah, well, but I had—_re_markable pious. I could rattle off my catechism so fast you couldn't tell one word from another. And here's what it come to, Jim—it begun with chuck-farthen on the blessed grave-stones! But it were Providence put me here. I've thought it all out in this lonely island, and I'm back on piety. You don't catch me tasting rum so much—just a thimbleful for luck. I'm bound I'll be good. And, Jim"—dropping his voice to a whisper—"I'm rich."

I felt sure the poor fellow had gone crazy, and must have shown it, for he repeated hotly: "Rich! Rich! I'll make a man of you, Jim. You'll bless your stars you was the first that found me!"

A shadow came over his face; he gripped my hand and raised a forefinger threateningly. "Now, Jim, tell me true: that ain't Flint's ship?"

At this I had a happy inspiration. "It's not Flint's ship, and Flint is dead; but there are some of Flint's hands aboard; worse luck for the rest of us."

"Not a man—with one—leg?" he gasped.

"Silver?"

"Ah, Silver! That were his name."

"He's the cook, and the ringleader too."

He was still holding my wrist, and gave it quite a wring. "If you was sent by Long John, I'm as good as pork. But where was you, do you suppose?"

I told him the whole story of our voyage and our predicament. When I had done he patted me on the head.

"You're a good lad, Jim, all in a clove hitch. Put your trust in Ben Gunn. Would your squire be liberal in case of help?"

I told him the squire was the most liberal of men.

"Aye," he returned. "Would he be likely to come down to the toon of, say one thousand pounds out of money that's as good as a man's own already?"

"I am sure he would," said I. "As it was, all hands were to share."

"AND a passage home?" he added shrewdly.

"Why, the squire's a gentleman. Besides, if we got rid of the others, we'd want you to help work the vessel home."

"Ah," said he, much relieved. "Now I'll tell you what. I were in Flint's ship when he buried the treasure; he and six strong seamen. They was ashore nigh a week. One day up went the signal, and here come Flint alone in a little boat, his head done up in a blue scarf, mortal white he looked. The six all dead. Battle, murder, and sudden death—him against six.

"Three years back, in another ship, we sighted this island. 'Here's Flint's treasure,' says I. Twelve days they looked, and every day had a worse word for me, till one morning they went aboard. 'Here's a musket, and a spade, and pick-axe. You can stay here and find Flint's money for yourself.'"

"Well," I said, "how am I to get on board?"

"Ah, that's the hitch. There's my boat under the white rock. If the worst come to the worst, we might try her after dark. Hi!" he broke out. "What's that?"

For just then, all the echoes of the island awoke and bellowed to the thunder of a cannon.

"They have begun to fight!" I cried. "Follow me."

I ran towards the anchorage, my terrors all forgotten, while at my side the marooned man in his goatskins trotted easily.

The cannon-shot was followed by a volley of small arms. Another pause; and then, not a quarter of a mile in front, I beheld the Union Jack flutter above a wood.

*Narrative Continued by the Doctor: How the Ship Was Abandoned*

It was about half past one—three bells in the sea phrase—that the two boats went ashore from the HISPANIOLA. The captain, the squire, and I were talking matters over in the cabin. Had there been wind, we should have fallen on the six mutineers left aboard and away to sea. But the wind was wanting; and worse, came word that Jim Hawkins had slipped ashore with the rest.

We were alarmed for his safety; with the men in the temper they were in, it seemed an even chance if we should see the lad again. We ran on deck. The pitch was bubbling in the seams; if ever a man smelt fever and dysentery, it was in that abominable anchorage. The six scoundrels grumbled under a sail in the forecastle; ashore we could see the gigs made fast, and a man in each. One of them was whistling "Lillibullero."

Waiting was a strain, and it was decided I should go ashore in the jolly-boat in quest of information.

I pulled straight in toward the stockade upon the chart. The two guarding the boats seemed in a bustle at our appearance; "Lillibullero" stopped off. Had they told Silver, all might have turned out differently; but they had their orders, and harked back again to "Lillibullero."

I jumped out and came as near running as I durst, a brace of pistols primed for safety. A hundred yards on I reached the stockade. A spring of clear water rose at the top of a knoll. On the knoll, enclosing the spring, they had clapped a stout loghouse, loopholed for musketry, and round it cleared a wide space, set with a paling six feet high without door or opening. Short of a complete surprise, the people in it might have held the place against a regiment.

What particularly took my fancy was the spring. For though we had a good place of it in the HISPANIOLA's cabin, with arms and wines, one thing was overlooked—we had no water. I was thinking this over when there came ringing over the island the cry of a man at the point of death. I was not new to violent death—I have served his Royal Highness the Duke of Cumberland, and got a wound myself at Fontenoy—but my pulse went dot and carry one. "Jim Hawkins is gone," was my first thought.

It is something to have been an old soldier, but more still to have been a doctor. There is no time to dilly-dally in our work. I returned to the shore and jumped on board the jolly-boat.

I found them all shaken. The squire sat white as a sheet, thinking of the harm he had led us to, the good soul.

"There's a man," says Captain Smollett, nodding to one of the hands, "new to this work. Another touch of the rudder and that man would join us."

We settled on the plan. We put a servant in the gallery between cabin and forecastle with loaded muskets and a mattress for protection, brought the boat round under the stern-port, and set to loading her with powder, muskets, biscuit, pork, cognac, and my invaluable medicine chest.

Meanwhile the captain hailed the coxswain on deck.

"Mr. Hands," he said, "here are two of us with a brace of pistols each. If any one of you six make a signal of any description, that man's dead."

They were taken aback, and after a consultation tumbled down the fore companion, no doubt to take us on the rear. But when they saw our man waiting with muskets in the galley, they went about ship at once, and a head popped out on deck.

"Down, dog!" cries the captain.

And the head popped back.

We touched land and tossed our stores over the palisade. Left a man to guard them; sculled back, loaded again. By the second trip the watchers had roused; "Lillibullero" was dropped, and one whipped off and disappeared. The final loading at the schooner was pork, powder, biscuit, and only a musket and cutlass apiece for the four of us. The rest of the arms we dropped overboard in two fathoms of water, where we could see the bright steel shining on the clean sandy bottom.

The tide was beginning to ebb. Voices halloaed from the direction of the gigs; it warned us to be off. Our servant retreated from his place and dropped into the boat, which we brought round to the ship's counter for Captain Smollett.

"Now, men," said he, "do you hear me?"

No answer from the forecastle.

"It's to you, Abraham Gray—it's to you I am speaking."

Still no reply.

"Gray," resumed Mr. Smollett, a little louder, "I am leaving this ship, and I order you to follow your captain. I know you are a good man at bottom. I have my watch here in my hand; I give you thirty seconds to join me in."

A pause.

"Come, my fine fellow," continued the captain; "don't hang so long in stays. I'm risking my life and the lives of these good gentlemen every second."

There was a sudden scuffle, a sound of blows, and out burst Abraham Gray with a knife cut on the cheek, and came running to the captain like a dog to the whistle.

"I'm with you, sir," said he.

The next moment he and the captain had dropped aboard, and we had shoved off and given way.

This fifth trip was different from the others. Our gallipot was gravely overloaded—five grown men and the cargo. The gunwale was lipping astern; my breeches were soaking before we had gone a hundred yards. The ebb was making, a strong current that swept us from our proper landing-place; if we let it have its way we should come ashore beside the gigs.

"I cannot keep her head for the stockade, sir," said I. "The tide keeps washing her down."

"Bear up, sir," said the captain, "until you see you're gaining."

I tried, and found I had to lay her head due east, square to our true course. "We'll never get ashore at this rate."

"If it's the only course we can lie, we must even lie it. The current must slacken, and then we can dodge back along the shore."

Suddenly the captain spoke up again, his voice a little changed.

"The gun!" said he.

"They could never get the gun ashore," said I.

"Look astern, doctor."

We had entirely forgotten the long nine; and there were the rogues busy about her, getting off her tarpaulin cover.

"Israel was Flint's gunner," said Gray hoarsely.

At any risk, we put the boat's head direct for the landing-place. But now we turned our broadside instead of our stern to the HISPANIOLA, and offered a target like a barn door. I could hear that brandy-faced rascal Hands plumping a round-shot on the deck.

"Who's the best shot?" asked the captain.

"Mr. Trelawney, out and away."

"Mr. Trelawney, will you please pick me off one of these men, sir? Hands, if possible."

Trelawney was as cool as steel. "Easy with that gun, sir, or you'll swamp the boat. All hands stand by to trim her when he aims."

The squire raised his gun, the rowing ceased, and we leaned to balance her. Hands at the muzzle was the most exposed; but just as Trelawney fired, down he stooped, and it was one of the others who fell. The cry was echoed from the shore; the rest of the pirates trooped from the trees and tumbled into their boats.

"Give way!" cried the captain. "We mustn't mind if we swamp her now."

We were close in. The one source of danger was the gun.

"Ready!" cried the squire.

"Hold!" cried the captain, quick as an echo.

He and the rowers backed with a great heave that sent her stern under water. The report fell at the same instant. The ball must have passed over our heads; the wind of it may have helped our disaster. The boat sank gently in three feet of water, leaving the captain and myself on our feet. The others came up drenched and bubbling.

No lives were lost; we could wade ashore. But our stores were at the bottom, and only two guns of five remained serviceable. Voices drew near in the woods. We waded ashore as fast as we could, leaving the poor jolly-boat and a good half of our powder and provisions.

*Narrative Continued by the Doctor: End of the First Day's Fighting*

We made our best speed across the wood that divided us from the stockade, the buccaneers' voices ringing nearer at every step.

"Captain," said I, "Trelawney is the dead shot. Give him your gun; his own is useless."

They exchanged guns; Trelawney, silent and cool, hung a moment to see all was fit. Observing Gray unarmed, I handed him my cutlass—our new hand was worth his salt.

Forty paces on we came to the edge of the wood. We struck the enclosure about the middle of the south side; almost at the same time seven mutineers appeared in full cry at the southwestern corner. They paused; before they recovered our four shots came in a scattering volley. One of the enemy fell, and the rest plunged into the trees.

We were rejoicing when a pistol cracked in the bush; a ball whistled past my ear, and poor Tom Redruth stumbled and fell his length on the ground. We returned the shot but had nothing to aim at.

The captain and Gray examined him; all was over. We carried him, groaning, into the log-house. He was the oldest of our party by a score of years, sullen and faithful, who had not uttered one word of complaint from the beginning of our troubles. The squire dropped on his knees, kissed his hand, and cried like a child.

"Be I going, doctor?" he asked.

"Tom, my man," said I, "you're going home."

"I wish I had had a lick at them with the gun first," he replied. After a silence he asked someone read a prayer—"It's the custom, sir," he added apologetically—and not long after passed away.

Meanwhile the captain had turned out stores from his swollen pockets—colours, Bible, rope, pen, ink, log-book, tobacco. He found a felled fir-tree, set it at the corner of the log-house, and ran up the colours with his own hand. Then he spread another flag on Tom's body.

"Don't you take on, sir," he said, shaking the squire's hand. "All's well with him; no fear for a hand shot down in his duty. It mayn't be good divinity, but it's a fact."

He pulled me aside. "Dr. Livesey, in how many weeks do you expect the consort?"

I told him months, not weeks—the end of August at earliest.

"Making large allowance for Providence, we're pretty close hauled. Powder and shot we'll do for; but rations are short—so short we're perhaps as well without that extra mouth." And he pointed to the body under the flag.

Just then, with a roar and a whistle, a round-shot passed high above the roof and plumped far beyond us in the wood.

"Oho!" said the captain. "Blaze away! You've little enough powder already, my lads."

The second trial was better; the ball descended inside the stockade, scattering sand but doing no damage.

"Captain," said the squire, "the house is invisible from the ship. It must be the flag they aim at. Would it not be wiser to take it in?"

"Strike my colours!" cried the captain. "No, sir, not I." And we all agreed with him. It was stout, seamanly good feeling, and good policy besides—it showed our enemies we despised their cannonade.

All evening they thundered away. The shot fell so high it buried itself in the sand; we had no ricochet to fear, and though one popped through the roof and out the floor, we soon minded it no more than cricket.

"There is one good thing," observed the captain; "the wood in front is likely clear. The ebb has made; our stores should be uncovered. Volunteers to bring in pork."

Two went, but the mission proved useless. The mutineers were bolder than we fancied: four or five were carrying our stores out to one of the gigs, Silver in the stern-sheets in command, every man now armed from some secret magazine.

The captain sat down to his log, and here is the beginning of the entry:

> Alexander Smollett, master; David Livesey, ship's doctor; Abraham Gray, carpenter's mate; John Trelawney, owner; and two servants—being all that is left faithful of the ship's company—with stores for ten days, came ashore this day and flew British colours on the log-house in Treasure Island. Thomas Redruth, owner's servant, shot by the mutineers; James Hawkins, cabin-boy—

And at the same time, I was wondering over poor Jim Hawkins' fate.

A hail on the land side.

"Doctor! Squire! Captain! Hullo!" came the cries.

I ran to the door in time to see Jim Hawkins, safe and sound, come climbing over the stockade.

As soon as Ben Gunn saw the colours he stopped me by the arm and sat down.

"Now," said he, "there's your friends, sure enough."

"Far more likely the mutineers," I answered.

"That!" he cried. "Where nobody puts in but gen'lemen of fortune, Silver would fly the Jolly Roger. No, that's your friends. There's been blows, and your friends has had the best of it; and here they are in the old stockade, as was made years ago by Flint."

"All the more reason I should hurry and join them."

"Nay, mate—not you. You're on'y a boy, all told. Now, Ben Gunn is fly. Rum wouldn't bring me there—not till I see your born gen'leman and gets it on his word of honour. And you'll say this: 'Ben Gunn,' says you, 'has reasons of his own.'"

He pinched me again with the same air of cleverness.

"And when Ben Gunn is wanted, you know where to find him, Jim. Just wheer you found him today. And him that comes is to have a white thing in his hand, and he's to come alone."

He was interrupted by a loud report; a cannonball tore through the trees and pitched in the sand not a hundred yards off. We took to our heels in different directions.

For a good hour reports shook the island, and balls kept crashing through the woods. I moved from hiding-place to hiding-place, pursued, or so it seemed, by these terrifying missiles. Towards the end of the bombardment, after a long detour east, I crept down among the shore-side trees.

The sun had just set; the sea breeze ruffled the grey surface of the anchorage. The HISPANIOLA still lay at anchor, but flying from her peak now was the Jolly Roger—the black flag of piracy. As I looked, the last red flash came, and the last round-shot whistled through the air.

Men were demolishing the poor jolly-boat with axes on the beach. Near the river-mouth a great fire glowed among the trees; one of the gigs went back and forth between that point and the ship, the men shouting at the oars like children. There was a sound in their voices that suggested rum.

I was on the low, sandy spit east of the anchorage. Further down I saw a peculiarly white isolated rock, and thought this might be the one Ben Gunn had spoken of: some day a boat might be wanted.

Then I skirted the woods to the rear of the stockade, and was warmly welcomed.

I had soon told my story and began to look about. The log-house was unsquared pine trunks—roof, walls, and floor. Under a porch at the door the spring welled up into a great ship's kettle of iron, sunk among the sand. A stone slab for a hearth, an old rusty iron basket for the fire.

The cold evening breeze whistled through every chink and sprinkled the floor with a continual rain of fine sand. There was sand in our eyes, sand in our teeth, sand in our suppers, sand dancing in the spring at the bottom of the kettle, for all the world like porridge beginning to boil. Our chimney was a hole in the roof; little smoke found its way out, the rest eddied about and kept us coughing and piping the eye.

Add to this Gray's face tied up in a bandage from his break with the mutineers, and poor old Tom Redruth, still unburied, lying along the wall under the Union Jack.

Captain Smollett was never the man to let us sit idle. He divided us into watches, sent men for firewood and to dig Redruth's grave, named the doctor cook, put me sentry, and went from one to another keeping up our spirits.

From time to time the doctor came to the door for air, and had a word for me.

"That man Smollett," he said once, "is a better man than I am. And when I say that it means a deal, Jim."

Another time he was silent, then looked at me.

"Is this Ben Gunn a man?"

"I am not sure he's sane."

"If there's any doubt about the matter, he is," returned the doctor. "A man who has been three years biting his nails on a desert island, Jim, can't expect to appear as sane as you or me. It doesn't lie in human nature."

Before supper we buried old Tom in the sand bare-headed in the breeze. Then, after pork and a stiff brandy grog, the three chiefs took counsel.

They were at their wits' end—the stores so low we must have been starved before help came. Our best hope was to kill off the buccaneers till they hauled down their flag or ran with the HISPANIOLA. From nineteen they were reduced to fifteen, two wounded, one severely. We had two allies besides—rum and the climate. We could hear them roaring and singing late into the night; the doctor staked his wig that half, camped in the marsh without remedies, would be on their backs in a week.

"So," he added, "if we are not all shot down they'll be glad to pack in the schooner. It's a ship; they can get to buccaneering again."

"First ship that ever I lost," said Captain Smollett.

I was dead tired and slept like a log. The rest were up and breakfasted when I was wakened by a bustle and voices.

"Flag of truce!" I heard someone say; and then, with a cry of surprise, "Silver himself!"

Up I jumped, rubbed my eyes, and ran to a loophole in the wall.

XX

Silver's Embassy


Sure enough, two men stood outside the stockade, one waving a white cloth, the other Silver himself, standing placidly by.

It was the coldest morning I was ever abroad in. The sky was bright overhead, but where Silver stood, all was still in shadow, and they waded knee-deep in a low white vapour that had crawled out of the morass. The chill and the vapour told a poor tale of the island: a damp, feverish, unhealthy spot.

"Keep indoors, men," said the captain. "Ten to one this is a trick." Then he hailed: "Who goes? Stand, or we fire."

"Flag of truce," cried Silver.

The captain set the watch and called the rest to load muskets. "What do you want with your flag of truce?"

"Cap'n Silver, sir, to come on board and make terms," shouted the other.

"Cap'n Silver! Don't know him. Who's he?" And we heard him add, "Cap'n, is it? My heart, and here's promotion!"

Long John answered for himself. "Me, sir. These poor lads have chosen me cap'n, after your desertion, sir"—laying a particular emphasis upon the word *desertion*. "All I ask is your word, Cap'n Smollett, to let me safe and sound out of this here stockade, and one minute to get out o' shot before a gun is fired."

"My man," said Captain Smollett, "I have not the slightest desire to talk to you. If you wish to talk to me, you can come, that's all. If there's any treachery, it'll be on your side, and the Lord help you."

"That's enough, Cap'n," shouted Long John cheerily. "A word from you's enough. I know a gentleman, and you may lay to that."

Silver threw over his crutch, surmounted the fence, and dropped to our side. He had terrible hard work up the knoll, but at last arrived before the captain—tricked out in his best, an immense blue coat thick with brass buttons, a fine laced hat on the back of his head. He saluted in the handsomest style.

"You had better sit down," said the captain.

"Why, Silver," he went on, when Silver complained of the cold sand, "if you had pleased to be an honest man, you might have been sitting in your galley. You're either my ship's cook—and then you were treated handsome—or Cap'n Silver, a common mutineer and pirate, and then you can go hang!"

"Well, well, Cap'n," returned the sea-cook, sitting on the sand.

"If you have anything to say, my man, better say it," said the captain.

"Dooty is dooty. That was a good lay of yours last night. Maybe my people was shook—maybe I was shook myself. But mark me, Cap'n, it won't do twice. He wasn't dead when I got round to him, not he."

Silver's words were a riddle to the captain, but I began to have an inkling—Ben Gunn had paid the buccaneers a visit while they lay drunk, and we had only fourteen enemies to deal with.

"Well, here it is," said Silver. "We want that treasure, and we'll have it. Give us the chart, and drop shooting poor seamen and stoving of their heads in while asleep, and we'll offer you a choice: come aboard once the treasure's shipped, and I'll give my affy-davy to clap you somewhere safe ashore; or stay here, divide stores man for man, and I'll speak the first ship I sight to send 'em to pick you up."

Captain Smollett rose and knocked out the ashes of his pipe. "Is that all?"

"Every last word, by thunder! Refuse that, and you've seen the last of me but musket-balls."

"Very good. Now you'll hear me. If you'll come up one by one, unarmed, I'll engage to clap you all in irons and take you home to a fair trial in England. If you won't, my name is Alexander Smollett, I've flown my sovereign's colours, and I'll see you all to Davy Jones. You can't find the treasure. You can't sail the ship. You can't fight us. Your ship's in irons, Master Silver; you're on a lee shore. And in the name of heaven, I'll put a bullet in your back when next I meet you. Bundle out of this, hand over hand, and double quick."

Silver's face was a picture; his eyes started in his head with wrath. "Give me a hand up!" he cried.

"Not I," returned the captain.

"Who'll give me a hand up?" he roared.

Not a man among us moved. Growling the foulest imprecations, he crawled along the sand till he got hold of the porch and could hoist himself again upon his crutch. Then he spat into the spring.

"There! That's what I think of ye. Before an hour's out, I'll stove in your old block house like a rum puncheon. Them that die'll be the lucky ones."

And with a dreadful oath he stumbled off and disappeared among the trees.

XXI

The Attack


As soon as Silver disappeared, the captain turned and found not a man at his post but Gray. It was the first time we had ever seen him angry.

"Quarters!" he roared; and as we slunk back, "Gray, I'll put your name in the log; you've stood by your duty like a seaman. Mr. Trelawney, I'm surprised at you, sir. Doctor, I thought you had worn the king's coat!"

"My lads," said he when all was clear, "I've given Silver a broadside. I pitched it in red-hot on purpose; and before the hour's out, we shall be boarded. We're outnumbered, but we fight in shelter; and I've no manner of doubt we can drub them."

He set the defence: doctor at the door, men at the loopholes east and west, Trelawney and Gray at the north side where the danger lay; Jim and the captain to load. The fire was tossed out, brandy served round; and as the sun climbed and drank up the vapours at a draught, we stood at our posts in a fever of heat and anxiety.

An hour passed.

"Hang them!" said the captain. "This is as dull as the doldrums. Gray, whistle for a wind."

And just at that moment came the first shot. The report was repeated from without in a scattering volley, like a string of geese, from every side of the enclosure. Several bullets struck the log-house, but not one entered; and as the smoke cleared, the woods looked as quiet and empty as before.

From the count of flashes, the attack would clearly come from the north. The captain made no change; if the mutineers crossed the stockade they could shoot us down like rats in our own stronghold.

Suddenly, with a loud huzza, a cloud of pirates leaped from the woods on the north side and ran on the stockade. A rifle ball sang through the doorway and knocked the doctor's musket into bits. The boarders swarmed over the fence like monkeys. Squire and Gray fired again and yet again; three men fell, one forwards into the enclosure, two back on the outside. One of these was up again in a crack and disappeared. Two had bit the dust, one had fled, four had made good their footing inside.

The four ran straight for the building. A pirate boatswain's head appeared at the middle loophole.

"At 'em, all hands—all hands!" he roared.

At the same moment another wrenched a musket from a servant's hands through the loophole and laid the poor fellow senseless on the floor; a third, running unharmed round the house, fell with his cutlass on the doctor.

Our position was utterly reversed. A moment since we were firing under cover at an exposed enemy; now it was we who lay uncovered.

"Out, lads, out, and fight 'em in the open! Cutlasses!" cried the captain.

I snatched a cutlass from the pile, and someone gave me a cut across the knuckles which I hardly felt. I dashed out into the sunlight. The doctor was pursuing his assailant down the hill, and as I looked, beat down his guard and sent him sprawling on his back with a great slash across the face.

"Round the house, lads! Round the house!" cried the captain.

I ran round the corner and was face to face with the boatswain. He roared, his hanger flashing above his head. I had not time to be afraid, but as the blow still hung impending, leaped to one side, missed my foot in the soft sand, and rolled headlong down the slope.

When I had first sallied from the door, the other mutineers had been swarming up the palisade. One man, in a red night-cap, with his cutlass in his mouth, had even thrown a leg across. So short had been the interval that when I found my feet again, all was in the same posture—and yet, in this breath of time, the fight was over and the victory was ours.

Gray, following close behind me, had cut down the boatswain ere he could recover. Another had been shot at a loophole in the act of firing into the house. A third the doctor had disposed of at a blow. Of the four who had scaled the palisade, only one remained, and he was now clambering out again with the fear of death upon him.

"Fire—fire from the house!" cried the doctor. "And you, lads, back into cover."

But his words were unheeded, and the last boarder made good his escape. In three seconds nothing remained of the attacking party but the five who had fallen, four on the inside and one on the outside.

The doctor and Gray and I ran for shelter. The house was cleared of smoke, and we saw at a glance the price of victory. One servant lay stunned beside his loophole, another shot through the head; while right in the centre, the squire was supporting the captain, one as pale as the other.

"The captain's wounded," said Mr. Trelawney.

"Have they run?"

"All that could," returned the doctor. "But there's five of them will never run again."

"Five!" cried the captain. "Come, that's better. Five against three leaves us four to nine. That's better odds than we had at starting."

XXII

How I Began My Sea Adventure


There was no return of the mutineers—not so much as another shot. They had "got their rations for that day," as the captain put it. Of the eight who had fallen, only three still breathed—one of the pirates and two of our own; and of these the first two were as good as dead. The mutineer died under the doctor's knife; one servant lingered all day and went to his Maker in the night.

The captain's wounds were grievous but not dangerous. A ball had broken his shoulder-blade and touched the lung; another had torn muscles in the calf. He would recover, the doctor said, but must not walk nor move his arm, nor so much as speak when he could help it. My own cut across the knuckles was a flea-bite; Doctor Livesey patched it with plaster and pulled my ears for me into the bargain.

After dinner the squire and doctor consulted by the captain's side; then the doctor took his hat and pistols, girt on a cutlass, pocketed the chart, and crossed the palisade with a musket over his shoulder, setting off briskly through the trees.

Gray took his pipe out of his mouth, fairly thunder-struck. "Why, in the name of Davy Jones, is Dr. Livesey mad?"

"He's about the last of this crew for that," said I. "I take it he's going to see Ben Gunn."

I was right; but meanwhile the house was stifling and the sand within the palisade ablaze, and I began to envy the doctor in the cool shadow of the woods. With so much blood about me and dead bodies all around, I took a disgust of the place almost as strong as fear. At last, with no one observing me, I took the first step of my escapade: I filled my pockets with biscuit, laid hold of a brace of pistols, and felt myself well supplied.

My scheme was to go down the sandy spit, find the white rock I had observed last evening, and see whether Ben Gunn had hidden his boat there. As I should not be allowed to leave, my only plan was to take French leave—so bad a way as made the thing itself wrong. But I was only a boy, and I had made up my mind. The squire and Gray were busy with the captain's bandages; the coast was clear; I made a bolt over the stockade and was out of cry before my absence was observed. This was my second folly, far worse than the first; but like the first, it was a help towards saving all of us.

I took my way for the east coast. Soon I came forth into the open, and saw the sea blue and sunny to the horizon, the surf tumbling along the beach. I have never seen the sea quiet round Treasure Island. The sun might blaze overhead, the air be without a breath, but still these great rollers ran along the external coast, thundering by day and night.

I walked along the surf till, thinking I was far enough south, I crept up to the ridge of the spit. The breeze had blown itself out, succeeded by light airs carrying great banks of fog; the anchorage lay still and leaden. The HISPANIOLA, in that unbroken mirror, was exactly portrayed from truck to waterline, the Jolly Roger hanging from her peak. Alongside lay a gig, Silver in the stern-sheets—him I could always recognize. All at once there began the most horrid, unearthly screaming, which startled me, though I soon remembered the voice of Captain Flint.

Soon the jolly-boat pulled for shore. The sun went down behind the Spy-glass, and as the fog collected rapidly, it began to grow dark. I saw I must lose no time.

The white rock was still an eighth of a mile down the spit. Night had almost come when I laid my hand on its rough sides. Below was a hollow of green turf hidden by banks and underwood; and in the centre, a little tent of goat-skins. I lifted the side, and there was Ben Gunn's boat—a rude, lop-sided framework stretched with goat-skin, the hair inside. There was one thwart set low, a stretcher in the bows, and a double paddle. It was like the first and worst coracle ever made by man—but exceedingly light and portable.

Now I had taken another notion, and become so obstinately fond of it that I would have carried it out in the teeth of Captain Smollett himself: to slip out under cover of night, cut the HISPANIOLA adrift, and let her go ashore where she fancied. The mutineers, after their repulse, had nothing nearer their hearts than to up anchor and away; and now I had seen they left their watchmen unprovided with a boat, I thought it might be done with little risk.

Down I sat to wait for darkness and made a hearty meal of biscuit. The fog had buried all heaven. When at last I shouldered the coracle and groped my way out of the hollow, there were but two points visible on the whole anchorage: the great fire on shore where the defeated pirates caroused; and a mere blur of light marking the anchored ship.

The ebb had run some time, and I had to wade through a belt of swampy sand before I came to the water; and wading a little way in, I set my coracle, keel downwards, on the surface.

The coracle was a safe enough boat for one of my size, both buoyant and clever in a seaway; but she was the most cross-grained, lop-sided craft to manage. Do as I pleased, she made more leeway than anything, and turning round and round was the manoeuvre she did best.

I never should have made the ship but for the tide. Paddle as I pleased, the ebb swept me down; and there lay the HISPANIOLA right in the fairway, hardly to be missed. First she loomed like a blot of something blacker than darkness, then her spars and hull took shape, and the next moment I was alongside her hawser and had laid hold.

The hawser was as taut as a bowstring, the current so strong she pulled upon her anchor. One cut with my sea-gully and the HISPANIOLA would go humming down the tide. But it next occurred to me that a taut hawser, suddenly cut, is a thing as dangerous as a kicking horse — ten to one, the coracle and I would be knocked clean out of the water.

This brought me to a full stop; but fortune again favoured me. The light airs had hauled round into the south-west, and a puff caught the HISPANIOLA, forced her up into the current, and to my great joy I felt the hawser slacken in my grasp. I took out my gully, opened it with my teeth, and cut one strand after another, till the vessel swung only by two. Then I lay quiet, waiting.

All this time loud voices had been coming from the cabin. One I knew for the coxswain's, Israel Hands, that had been Flint's gunner; the other was my friend of the red night-cap. Both were plainly drunk and still drinking; one threw out an empty bottle with a drunken cry. Oaths flew like hailstones, and now and then came such an explosion as I thought sure to end in blows — but each time the quarrel passed off and the voices grumbled lower for a while.

On shore, the glow of the great camp-fire burned through the trees. Someone was singing a dull, droning sailor's song, and I caught a verse:

  "But one man of her crew alive,
  What put to sea with seventy-five."

I thought it a ditty rather too dolefully appropriate for a company that had met such cruel losses that morning. But these buccaneers were as callous as the sea they sailed on.

At last the breeze came; the schooner sidled and drew nearer; the hawser slackened once more, and with a good tough effort I cut the last fibres through.

Almost instantly I was swept against the bows, and the schooner began to turn upon her heel. I wrought like a fiend, shoved straight astern, and as I gave the last impulsion, my hands came across a light cord trailing from the stern bulwarks. Curiosity took the upper hand: I pulled in hand over hand, rose at infinite risk to about half my height, and thus commanded a slice of the cabin's interior.

One glance was sufficient. It showed me Hands and his companion locked together in deadly wrestle, each with a hand upon the other's throat. I dropped upon the thwart again, none too soon.

From the camp-fire, the company had broken into the chorus I had heard so often: "Fifteen men on the dead man's chest — Yo-ho-ho, and a bottle of rum!"

Then a sudden lurch of the coracle; she yawed sharply; the speed had strangely increased. I glanced over my shoulder, and my heart jumped. The current had turned at right angles, sweeping schooner and coracle round, ever quickening, ever bubbling higher, spinning through the narrows for the open sea.

A violent yaw of the schooner; shouts from on board; feet pounding on the companion ladder — the two drunkards had awakened to their disaster.

I lay flat in the wretched skiff and recommended my spirit to its Maker. So I must have lain for hours, beaten to and fro upon the billows; weariness grew, and at last sleep supervened, and in my sea-tossed coracle I dreamed of home and the old Admiral Benbow.

It was broad day when I awoke, tossing at the south-west end of Treasure Island. Haulbowline Head was at my elbow, with cliffs forty or fifty feet high, and among the fallen rocks the breakers spouted and bellowed. Slimy monsters lay crawling on the rocks — sea lions, I learned later, and harmless; but the look of them disgusted me of that landing-place.

North of Haulbowline Head the land ran in, with another cape — Cape of the Woods — buried in tall green pines that descended to the sea. Remembering what Silver had said about the current that sets northward along the west coast, I preferred to leave Haulbowline Head behind and reserve my strength for the kindlier shore.

There was a great smooth swell. My little boat rode easily; often I would see a big blue summit heaving close above me, yet the coracle would but bounce a little, dance as if on springs, and subside as lightly as a bird. I grew bold and sat up to try my skill at paddling — but even a small change in the disposition of the weight produced violent changes. She ran straight down a slope of water so steep it made me giddy, and struck her nose deep into the next wave. Drenched, I fell back into my old position. It was plain she was not to be interfered with: I must lie still and let her thread the lower parts of each wave, only putting the paddle over now and then, in smooth places, to give her a shove towards land.

It was tiring work, yet I did visibly gain ground. As we drew near the Cape of the Woods I saw I must miss that point, but I had made some hundred yards of easting. I could see the cool green tree-tops swaying together in the breeze.

It was high time, for I now began to be tortured with thirst. The salt caking on my lips made my throat burn and my brain ache. The sight of the trees had almost made me sick with longing; but the current carried me past, and as the next reach opened out, I beheld a sight that changed the nature of my thoughts.

Right in front of me, not half a mile away, was the HISPANIOLA under sail. I made sure I should be taken; but surprise had taken entire possession of my mind, and I could do nothing but stare and wonder.

Her main-sail and two jibs shone in the sun like silver. When I first sighted her she was lying a course about north-west; I presumed they were going round the island back to the anchorage. Presently she fetched more westward, as if they had sighted me. At last she fell right into the wind's eye, was taken dead aback, and stood there helpless, with her sails shivering.

"Clumsy fellows," said I; "they must still be drunk as owls."

The schooner gradually fell off and filled again upon another tack, sailed swiftly a minute, and brought up once more dead in the wind's eye. Again and again was this repeated. To and fro she sailed by swoops and dashes, ending each time with idly flapping canvas. It became plain that nobody was steering. Either the men were dead drunk or had deserted her — and perhaps I might return the vessel to her captain.

If only I dared sit up and paddle, I made sure I could overhaul her. The scheme had an air of adventure that inspired me, and the thought of the water breaker beside the fore companion doubled my courage.

Up I got. Welcomed by another cloud of spray, I stuck to my purpose and set myself to paddle after the unsteered HISPANIOLA. Once I shipped a sea so heavy I had to stop and bail, with my heart fluttering like a bird; but gradually I got into the way of the thing.

I was gaining rapidly; the brass glistened on the tiller as it banged about, and still no soul appeared on her decks. If she was not deserted, the men must be lying drunk below.

Now, at last, I had my chance. The breeze fell, and the current turning her, the HISPANIOLA revolved slowly and presented me her stern, with the cabin window still gaping open and the lamp over the table still burning on into the day. The main-sail hung drooped like a banner.

I redoubled my efforts. I was not a hundred yards from her when the wind came again in a clap; she filled on the port tack and was off, stooping and skimming like a swallow. My first impulse was despair, my second towards joy. Round she came, broadside on to me, till she had covered three quarters of the distance. Immensely tall she looked from my low station in the coracle.

And then, of a sudden, I began to comprehend. I had scarce time to act. I was on the summit of one swell when the schooner came stooping over the next. The bowsprit was over my head. I sprang to my feet and leaped, stamping the coracle under water. With one hand I caught the jib-boom, my foot lodged between the stay and the brace; and as I clung there panting, a dull blow told me the schooner had struck the coracle, and I was left without retreat on the HISPANIOLA.

I had scarce gained the bowsprit when the flying jib flapped and filled on the other tack with a report like a gun. The schooner trembled; next moment the jib hung idle. I crawled back and tumbled head foremost on the deck.

Not a soul was to be seen. An empty bottle, broken by the neck, tumbled to and fro in the scuppers. Suddenly the HISPANIOLA came right into the wind; jibs cracked, rudder slammed, the ship gave a sickening heave, and the main-boom swung inboard, showing me the lee after-deck.

There were the two watchmen. Red-cap on his back, stiff as a handspike, arms stretched like a crucifix, his teeth-disclosing grin undisturbed by every jump of the ship; Israel Hands propped against the bulwarks, chin on chest, face white as a tallow candle. Around both were splashes of dark blood — they had killed each other in their drunken wrath.

In a calm moment, Hands writhed back to his first position with a low moan that went to my heart; but when I remembered the apple-barrel talk, all pity left me. I walked aft to the main-mast.

"Come aboard, Mr. Hands," I said ironically.

He rolled his eyes round heavily, too far gone for surprise. All he could utter was one word: "Brandy."

I slipped aft and down the companion stairs. The cabin was a scene of confusion — lockfast places broken open for the chart, floor thick with mud, empty bottles clinking in corners, one of the doctor's medical books gutted for pipelights. I found some brandy for Hands, biscuit, pickled fruits, raisins, and cheese for myself, drank deep at the water-breaker, and then, and not till then, gave Hands the brandy.

He must have drunk a gill before he took the bottle from his mouth.

"Aye," said he, "by thunder, but I wanted some o' that!"

I had sat down in my corner and begun to eat.

"Much hurt?" I asked.

He grunted. "If that doctor was aboard, I'd be right enough in a couple of turns; but I don't have no manner of luck. As for that swab, he's good and dead. Where mought you have come from?"

"I've come aboard to take possession of this ship, Mr. Hands; and you'll please regard me as your captain until further notice."

He looked at me sourly but said nothing.

"By the by," I continued, "I can't have these colours, Mr. Hands; and by your leave, I'll strike 'em. Better none than these."

And again dodging the boom, I ran to the colour lines, handed down their cursed black flag, and chucked it overboard.

"God save the king!" said I, waving my cap. "And there's an end to Captain Silver!"

He watched me keenly and slyly, his chin on his breast.

"I reckon," he said at last, "I reckon, Cap'n Hawkins, you'll kind of want to get ashore now. S'pose we talks."

"Why, yes," says I, "with all my heart, Mr. Hands. Say on." And I went back to my meal.

"This man and me got the canvas on her, meaning to sail her back. Well, he's dead now — and who's to sail this ship I don't see. You gives me food and drink and a scarf for my wound, and I'll tell you how to sail her, that's square all round."

"I'll tell you one thing," says I: "I'm not going back to Captain Kidd's anchorage. I mean to get into North Inlet and beach her quietly there."

"To be sure," he cried. "I've tried my fling and lost. North Inlet? I'd help you sail her up to Execution Dock, by thunder!"

There was sense in this. We struck our bargain on the spot, and in three minutes the HISPANIOLA was sailing easily before the wind, with good hopes of beaching her at North Inlet by high water.

I lashed the tiller and went below for a soft silk handkerchief of my mother's. With this Hands bound up the bleeding stab in his thigh; and after he had eaten and another swallow of brandy, he picked up visibly — sat straighter, spoke louder, looked another man.

The breeze served us admirably. We skimmed before it like a bird, the coast flashing by, and soon we had turned the corner of the rocky hill that ends the island on the north.

I was elated with my new command, plenty of water and food, my conscience quieted by the great conquest. I should have had nothing left to desire — but for the eyes of the coxswain following me derisively about the deck, and the odd smile that continually appeared on his face. A smile of pain and weakness — a haggard old man's smile; but with a grain of derision, a shadow of treachery, as he craftily watched, and watched, and watched me at my work.

The wind hauled into the west, serving us well. The coxswain told me how to lay the ship to, and after many trials I succeeded; we both sat in silence over another meal.

"Cap'n," said he at length with that uncomfortable smile, "here's my old shipmate; s'pose you was to heave him overboard. I don't reckon him ornamental now, do you?"

"I'm not strong enough," said I, "and there he lies, for me."

"This here's an unlucky ship, this HISPANIOLA, Jim," he went on. "Well now, I'm no scholar, and you're a lad as can read and figure: do you take it as a dead man is dead for good, or do he come alive again?"

"You can kill the body, Mr. Hands, but not the spirit," I replied. "He is in another world, and may be watching us."

"Ah!" says he. "That's unfort'nate—appears killing parties was a waste of time. Howsomever, sperrits don't reckon for much. I'll chance it with the sperrits, Jim. Now, you've spoke up free, and I'll take it kind if you'd step down and get me—shiver my timbers!—a bottle of wine. This brandy's too strong for my head."

His hesitation seemed unnatural; the wine-versus-brandy story was a pretext. He wanted me off the deck. His eyes never met mine; he kept smiling and putting his tongue out in a guilty, embarrassed manner. I saw my advantage and could conceal my suspicions to the end.

"Some wine?" I said. "Will you have white or red?"

"It's about the blessed same to me, shipmate," he replied; "so it's strong, and plenty of it."

"All right," I answered. "I'll bring you port."

I scuttled down the companion noisily, slipped off my shoes, ran along the gallery, and popped my head out of the fore companion. My suspicions proved true. Hands had risen to his hands and knees, and though his leg hurt sharply, he trailed himself across the deck. He picked from a coil of rope a short dirk, discoloured to the hilt with blood, concealed it in his jacket, and trundled back.

This was all I required to know. Israel could move, was armed, and I was meant to be the victim. Yet I could trust him in one point: our interests jumped together in beaching the schooner. Until she was safe, my life would be spared.

I stole back, slipped on my shoes, took up a bottle of wine, and reappeared on deck. Hands lay all fallen together, eyelids lowered. He looked up, knocked the neck off the bottle like a man who had done the same often, and took a good swig with his favourite toast of "Here's luck!" Then he begged me to cut him a quid of tobacco.

"Ah, Jim, Jim," said he, "I reckon I've missed stays! Cut me a quid, as'll likely be the last, lad, for I'm for my long home."

"Well," said I, "if I was you, I'd go to my prayers like a Christian man."

"Why?" said he. "Now, you tell me why."

"Why?" I cried. "You've broken your trust; you've lived in sin and lies and blood; there's a man you killed lying at your feet this moment. For God's mercy, Mr. Hands, that's why."

He took a great draught of wine and spoke with unusual solemnity. "For thirty years I've sailed the seas and seen good and bad, fair weather and foul. Well, now I tell you, I never seen good come o' goodness yet. Him as strikes first is my fancy; dead men don't bite; them's my views—amen, so be it. And now," he added, suddenly changing his tone, "we've had enough of this foolery. The tide's made good. You just take my orders, Cap'n Hawkins, and we'll sail slap in and be done with it."

We had scarce two miles to run, but the navigation was delicate. Hands was an excellent pilot, and we dodged in with a neatness that was a pleasure to behold. Scarcely had we passed the heads before the land closed around us. Right before us we saw the wreck of a great ship in the last stages of dilapidation, hung with webs of dripping seaweed, and on its deck shore bushes had taken root and flourished thick with flowers.

"Now," said Hands, "there's a pet bit for to beach a ship in. Fine flat sand, never a cat's paw, trees all around. And now, boy, stand by. Starboard a little—so—steady—starboard—larboard a little—steady—steady!"

He issued his commands, which I breathlessly obeyed, till, all of a sudden, he cried, "Now, my hearty, luff!" I put the helm hard up, and the HISPANIOLA swung round and ran stem on for the wooded shore.

The excitement of these manoeuvres had interfered with my watch upon the coxswain. I might have fallen without a struggle had not a sudden disquietude made me turn my head. There was Hands, already half-way towards me, with the dirk in his right hand.

We must both have cried out when our eyes met. He threw himself forward and I leapt sideways towards the bows. As I did so, I let go of the tiller, which sprang sharp to leeward, struck Hands across the chest, and stopped him dead.

Before he could recover, I was safe out of the corner. Just forward of the main-mast I stopped, drew a pistol, took a cool aim, and drew the trigger. The hammer fell, but there followed neither flash nor sound—the priming was useless with sea-water. I cursed myself for my neglect.

I placed my palms against the main-mast and waited, every nerve on the stretch. Seeing I meant to dodge, he paused; and a moment passed in feints on his part and corresponding movements upon mine. It was such a game as I had often played at home about the rocks of Black Hill Cove, but never with such a wildly beating heart.

Then, suddenly, the HISPANIOLA struck, staggered, and canted over to the port side till the deck stood at forty-five degrees. We were both capsized, rolled into the scuppers, the dead body tumbling stiffly after us. I was first afoot. Quick as thought, I sprang into the mizzen shrouds, rattled up hand over hand, and was seated on the cross-trees before I drew breath. The dirk had struck not half a foot below me; and there stood Israel with his mouth open, a perfect statue of surprise and disappointment.

I lost no time in changing the priming of my pistol, and recharging the other afresh. My new employment struck Hands all of a heap. After an obvious hesitation, he hauled himself heavily into the shrouds, and with the dirk in his teeth, began slowly and painfully to mount. I had quietly finished my arrangements before he was a third of the way up.

"One more step, Mr. Hands," said I, "and I'll blow your brains out! Dead men don't bite, you know," I added with a chuckle.

He stopped instantly. At last, with a swallow or two, he spoke. "Jim," says he, "I reckon we're fouled, you and me, and we'll have to sign articles. I'd have had you but for that there lurch, but I don't have no luck, not I; and I reckon I'll have to strike, which comes hard for a master mariner to a ship's younker like you, Jim."

I was drinking in his words and smiling away, as conceited as a cock upon a wall, when, all in a breath, back went his right hand over his shoulder. Something sang like an arrow through the air; I felt a blow and then a sharp pang, and there I was pinned by the shoulder to the mast. In the horrid pain and surprise of the moment, both my pistols went off, and both escaped out of my hands. They did not fall alone; with a choked cry, the coxswain loosed his grasp upon the shrouds and plunged head first into the water.

Owing to the cant of the vessel, the masts hung far out over the water, and from my perch on the cross-trees I had nothing below me but the surface of the bay. Hands rose once to the surface in a lather of foam and blood and then sank again for good. As the water settled, I could see him lying huddled together on the clean, bright sand in the shadow of the vessel's sides. A fish or two whipped past his body. He was dead enough, being both shot and drowned, and was food for fish in the very place where he had designed my slaughter.

I felt sick, faint, and terrified. The hot blood was running over my back and chest. The dirk, where it had pinned my shoulder to the mast, seemed to burn like a hot iron; yet it was not so much these real sufferings that distressed me as the horror of falling from the cross-trees into that still green water, beside the body of the coxswain.

I clung with both hands till my nails ached, and shut my eyes as if to cover up the peril. Gradually my mind came back, my pulses quieted, and I was once more in possession of myself.

It was my first thought to pluck forth the dirk, but either it stuck too hard or my nerve failed, and I desisted with a violent shudder. Oddly enough, that very shudder did the business. The knife had come the nearest in the world to missing me altogether; it held me by a mere pinch of skin, which the shudder tore away. The blood ran down the faster, but I was my own master again, only tacked to the mast by my coat and shirt. These I broke through with a sudden jerk, and regained the deck by the starboard shrouds.

I went below and did what I could for my wound; it pained me but was neither deep nor dangerous. Then I began to think of clearing the ship from its last passenger. He lay against the bulwarks like some horrible, ungainly puppet, life-size indeed, but how different from life's colour or comeliness. The habit of tragical adventures had worn off almost all my terror for the dead, and I took him by the waist and tumbled him overboard. He went in with a sounding plunge; the red cap came off and remained floating on the surface; and I could see him and Israel lying side by side, both wavering with the tremulous movement of the water, the quick fishes steering to and fro over both.

I was now alone upon the ship; the tide had just turned. The evening breeze had sprung up, and the cordage had begun to sing softly. I doused the jibs and tumbled them to the deck; the main-sail was a harder matter, and at last I got my knife and cut the halyards. The peak dropped, a great belly of loose canvas floated broad upon the water. For the rest, the HISPANIOLA must trust to luck, like myself.

Holding the cut hawser in both hands for a last security, I let myself drop softly overboard. The water scarcely reached my waist, and I waded ashore in great spirits, leaving the schooner on her side, with her main-sail trailing wide upon the surface of the bay. The sun went fairly down and the breeze whistled low among the tossing pines.

At least, and at last, I was off the sea, nor had I returned empty-handed. There lay the schooner, clear of buccaneers and ready for our own men to board. The recapture of the HISPANIOLA was a clenching answer, and I hoped even Captain Smollett would confess I had not lost my time.

I set my face homeward, turned the corner of the two-peaked hill, and waded across the watercourse. As I opened out the cleft between the two peaks, I became aware of a wavering glow against the sky, where the maroon was cooking his supper before a roaring fire. I wondered, in my heart, that he should show himself so careless—if I could see this radiance, might it not reach the eyes of Silver?

Gradually the night fell blacker, but a pale glimmer of moonbeams alighted on the summit of the Spy-glass, and soon I saw the moon had risen. With this to help me, I drew near the stockade. As I began to thread the grove that lies before it, I slacked my pace and went a trifle warily. It would have been a poor end of my adventures to be shot down by my own party in mistake.

Right in front of me a glow of a different colour appeared among the trees—red and hot, like the embers of a bonfire smouldering. For the life of me I could not think what it might be.

At last I came down upon the borders of the clearing. The block house itself lay in black shadow chequered with silvery streaks of moonlight. On the other side of the house an immense fire had burned itself into clear embers and shed a steady red reverberation. There was not a soul stirring nor a sound beside the noises of the breeze.

I stopped, with much wonder in my heart, and perhaps a little terror also. It had not been our way to build great fires; and I began to fear something had gone wrong while I was absent.

I stole round by the eastern end and crossed the palisade. To make assurance surer, I crawled, without a sound, towards the corner of the house. As I drew nearer, my heart was suddenly lightened. It was like music to hear my friends snoring together so loud and peaceful in their sleep.

By this time I had got to the door and stood up. All was dark within. There was the steady drone of the snorers and a small occasional flickering or pecking I could in no way account for. With my arms before me I walked steadily in. I should lie down in my own place (I thought with a silent chuckle) and enjoy their faces when they found me in the morning.

My foot struck something yielding—a sleeper's leg; and he turned and groaned, but without awaking. And then, all of a sudden, a shrill voice broke forth out of the darkness:

"Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight!" and so forth, without pause or change, like the clacking of a tiny mill.

Silver's green parrot, Captain Flint! It was she, keeping better watch than any human being, who thus announced my arrival with her wearisome refrain. I had no time to recover. The sleepers awoke and sprang up; and with a mighty oath, the voice of Silver cried, "Who goes?"

I turned to run, struck violently against one person, recoiled, and ran full into the arms of a second, who closed upon and held me tight.

"Bring a torch, Dick," said Silver when my capture was thus assured.

In the Enemy's Camp

The red glare of the torch showed me the worst of my apprehensions realized. The pirates were in possession of the block house and stores: the cask of cognac, the pork and bread, and—what tenfold increased my horror—not a sign of any prisoner. I could only judge that all had perished, and my heart smote me sorely that I had not been there to perish with them.

There were six buccaneers, all told. Five were on their feet, flushed and swollen with drink suddenly broken. The sixth had only risen upon his elbow, deadly pale, a blood-stained bandage round his head; I doubted not he was the man shot in the great attack. The parrot sat preening on Long John's shoulder. Silver himself looked paler and sterner than I was used to, his fine broadcloth bitterly the worse for wear, daubed with clay and torn with briers.

"So," said he, "here's Jim Hawkins, shiver my timbers! Dropped in, like, eh? Well, come, I take that friendly."

He sat down across the brandy cask and began to fill a pipe.

"Now, you see, Jim, so be as you ARE here," says he, "I'll give you a piece of my mind. I've always liked you, for a lad of spirit, and the picter of my own self when I was young and handsome. I always wanted you to jine and take your share, and die a gentleman, and now, my cock, you've got to. Cap'n Smollett's a fine seaman, but stiff on discipline. The doctor himself is gone dead again' you—'ungrateful scamp' was what he said. You can't go back to your own lot, for they won't have you; and without you start a third ship's company all by yourself, you'll have to jine with Cap'n Silver."

So far so good. My friends, then, were still alive; and though I half believed Silver's claim that they were incensed at my desertion, I was more relieved than distressed.

"I'm all for argyment," he continued; "I never seen good come o' threatening. If you like the service, you'll jine; and if you don't, Jim, why, you're free to answer no—free and welcome, shipmate."

"Am I to answer, then?" I asked, my voice tremulous. Through all this sneering talk I felt the threat of death overhung me.

"Lad, no one's a-pressing of you. Time goes so pleasant in your company, you see."

"Well," said I, growing bolder, "if I'm to choose, I have a right to know what's what, and where my friends are."

Silver told me how Doctor Livesey had come down with a flag of truce that morning to find the schooner gone from the bay; how a bargain had been struck for stores and block house; how the doctor's party had tramped off to where Silver did not know.

"And lest you take it into your head you was included in the treaty," he added, "here's the last word the doctor said: 'As for that boy, I don't know where he is, confound him, nor I don't much care. We're about sick of him.' These was his words."

"And now I am to choose?"

"And now you are to choose, and you may lay to that."

"Well," said I, by this time quite excited, "I am not such a fool but I know what I have to look for. Let the worst come to the worst, it's little I care. I've seen too many die since I fell in with you. But there's a thing or two I have to tell you. Here you are, in a bad way—ship lost, treasure lost, men lost; and if you want to know who did it—it was I! I was in the apple barrel the night we sighted land, and I heard you, John, and Dick, and Hands, who is now at the bottom of the sea, and told every word you said before the hour was out. As for the schooner, it was I who cut her cable, and I that killed the men you had aboard, and it was I who brought her where you'll never see her more, not one of you. The laugh's on my side; I no more fear you than I fear a fly. Kill me, if you please, or spare me. But if you spare me, when you fellows are in court for piracy, I'll save you all I can. It is for you to choose. Kill another and do yourselves no good, or spare me and keep a witness to save you from the gallows."

I stopped, out of breath, and to my wonder not a man of them moved, but all sat staring at me like as many sheep.

"And now, Mr. Silver," I said, "I believe you're the best man here, and if things go to the worst, I'll take it kind of you to let the doctor know the way I took it."

"I'll bear it in mind," said Silver, with an accent so curious I could not decide whether he were laughing at my request or favourably affected by my courage.

A mahogany-faced seaman called out that I was the boy who had known Black Dog; Silver added that I was the one who had faked the chart from Billy Bones. "First and last, we've split upon Jim Hawkins!"

"Then here goes!" the seaman cried, springing up with his knife.

"Avast, there!" roared Silver. "Cross me, and you'll go where many a good man's gone before you—some to the yard-arm, and some by the board, and all to feed the fishes. There's never a man looked me between the eyes and seen a good day a'terwards." A hoarse murmur rose from the others.

"Did any of you gentlemen want to have it out with ME?" he bellowed, bending forward from the keg, pipe still glowing. "Take a cutlass, him that dares, and I'll see the colour of his inside, crutch and all, before that pipe's empty."

Not a man stirred.

"I'm cap'n here by 'lection. I'm cap'n here because I'm the best man by a long sea-mile. You won't fight, as gentlemen o' fortune should; then, by thunder, you'll obey. I like that boy; he's more a man than any pair of rats of you in this here house. Let me see him that'll lay a hand on him."

There was a long pause. I stood straight against the wall, my heart going like a sledge-hammer, but with a ray of hope shining now. Silver leant back, arms crossed, calm as though he had been in church—yet his eye kept wandering, the tail of it on his unruly followers. They drew gradually together at the far end of the block house, the low hiss of their whispering sounding in my ear like a stream. Presently a long, ill-looking, yellow-eyed man stepped forward with an elaborate sea-salute and claimed his right to a council outside. One after another the rest followed, each making salute as he passed, and so all marched out and left Silver and me alone with the torch.

The sea-cook instantly removed his pipe.

"Now, look you here, Jim Hawkins," he said in a steady whisper, "you're within half a plank of death, and what's a long sight worse, of torture. They're going to throw me off. But you mark, I stand by you through thick and thin. I didn't mean to; not till you spoke up. But I see you was the right sort. Back to back, says I. You save your witness, and he'll save your neck!"

I began dimly to understand. "You mean all's lost?"

"Aye, by gum, I do! Ship gone, neck gone—that's the size of it. Once I looked into that bay, Jim Hawkins, and seen no schooner—well, I'm tough, but I gave out. I'll save your life, if so be as I can, from them. But, see here, Jim—tit for tat—you save Long John from swinging."

"What I can do, that I'll do," I said.

"It's a bargain!"

He hobbled to the torch and lit his pipe afresh. "I'm on squire's side now. I know you've got that ship safe somewheres. How you done it, I don't know, but safe it is. I ask no questions, nor I won't let others. Ah, you that's young—you and me might have done a power of good together!"

He drew some cognac into a tin cannikin. "And talking o' trouble, why did that doctor give me the chart, Jim?"

My face expressed a wonder so unaffected he saw the needlessness of further questions.

"Ah, well, he did, though. And there's something under that, no doubt—something, surely, under that, Jim—bad or good." He took another swallow, shaking his great fair head like a man who looks forward to the worst.

The Black Spot Again

The council had lasted some time when one of them re-entered, with a repetition of the same ironical salute, and begged a moment's loan of the torch. Silver agreed, and the man retired, leaving us in the dark.

"There's a breeze coming, Jim," said Silver, friendly and familiar now.

I turned to the loophole. The embers of the great fire had burned themselves so low that I understood why these conspirators desired a torch. About half-way down the slope they were collected in a group; one held the light, another knelt in their midst with the blade of an open knife shining in his hand, and a book as well. The kneeling figure rose, and the whole party began moving toward the house.

"Here they come," said I.

"Let 'em come, lad—let 'em come," said Silver cheerily. "I've still a shot in my locker."

The five pushed one of their number forward, who held his closed right hand before him.

"Step up, lad," cried Silver. "I won't eat you. Hand it over, lubber. I know the rules; I won't hurt a depytation."

The buccaneer stepped up, passed something to Silver, and slipped back to his companions.

"The black spot! I thought so," Silver observed. "Why, hillo! You've gone and cut this out of a Bible. What fool's cut a Bible?"

"It was Dick," said one.

"Dick, was it? Then Dick can get to prayers. He's seen his slice of luck, has Dick, and you may lay to that."

But here the yellow-eyed man cut in. "Belay that talk, John Silver. This crew has tipped you the black spot in full council, as in dooty bound; just you turn it over and see what's wrote there. Then you can talk."

"Thanky, George. Ah! 'Deposed'—that's it, is it? Very pretty wrote, like print, I swear. Your hand o' write, George? Why, you'll be cap'n next, I shouldn't wonder."

"Come, now," said George, "you don't fool this crew no more. You're over now, and you'll maybe step down off that barrel and help vote."

"I thought you said you knowed the rules," returned Silver contemptuously. "I'm still your cap'n, mind, till you outs with your grievances and I reply. In the meantime, your black spot ain't worth a biscuit."

"Oh," replied George, "WE'RE all square. First, you've made a hash of this cruise. Second, you let the enemy out o' this trap for nothing. Third, you wouldn't let us go at them upon the march. And fourth, there's this here boy."

"Is that all?" asked Silver quietly.

"Enough, too. We'll all swing for your bungling."

"Well now, I'll answer these four p'ints. I made a hash o' this cruise? You all know what I wanted, and you all know if that had been done we'd 'a been aboard the HISPANIOLA this night as ever was, every man alive, and the treasure in the hold of her, by thunder! Who crossed me? Why, it was Anderson, and Hands, and you, George Merry! And you're the last above board of that meddling crew, and you have the Davy Jones's insolence to up and stand for cap'n over me—you, that sank the lot of us!"

George wiped the sweat from his brow.

"Why, I give you my word, I'm sick to speak to you. We're that near the gibbet that my neck's stiff with thinking on it. You've seen 'em, hanged in chains, birds about 'em—'Who's that?' says one. 'That's John Silver. I knowed him well,' says another. Now, that's where we are, every mother's son of us, thanks to him, and Hands, and other ruination fools of you. And if you want to know about number four, and that boy—isn't he a hostage? Are we to waste a hostage? He might be our last chance. As for why I made a bargain—well, you came crawling on your knees to me to make it; you'd have starved too if I hadn't. But that's a trifle. You look there—that's why!"

And he cast down upon the floor a paper that I instantly recognized—the chart on yellow paper, with the three red crosses, that I had found in the oilcloth at the bottom of the captain's chest. Why the doctor had given it to him was more than I could fancy.

The surviving mutineers leaped on it like cats on a mouse. It went from hand to hand, with oaths and cries and childish laughter; you would have thought, not only they fingered the very gold, but were at sea with it, besides, in safety.

"Mighty pretty," said George. "But how are we to get away with it, and us no ship?"

Silver sprang up, supporting himself with a hand against the wall. "Now I give you warning, George! One more word of your sauce, and I'll call you down and fight you. You and the rest, that lost me my schooner with your interference, burn you. Civil you can speak, and shall."

"That's fair enow," said the old man Morgan.

"Fair! I reckon so. You lost the ship; I found the treasure. Who's the better man? And now I resign, by thunder! Elect whom you please to be your cap'n now; I'm done with it."

"Silver!" they cried. "Barbecue forever! Barbecue for cap'n!"

"So that's the toon, is it? George, I reckon you'll have to wait another turn, friend; lucky for you I'm not a revengeful man. And now, shipmates, this black spot? 'Tain't much good now, is it? Dick's crossed his luck and spoiled his Bible, and that's about all."

"It'll do to kiss the book on still, won't it?" growled Dick, evidently uneasy at the curse he had brought upon himself.

"A Bible with a bit cut out!" returned Silver derisively. "Not it. It don't bind no more'n a ballad-book."

"Here, Jim—here's a cur'osity for you," said Silver, and he tossed me the paper.

It was about the size of a crown piece. One side was blank; the other contained a verse or two of Revelation—these words among the rest, which struck sharply home upon my mind: "Without are dogs and murderers." The printed side had been blackened with wood ash, which already began to come off and soil my fingers; on the blank side had been written with the same material the one word "Depposed." I have that curiosity beside me at this moment, but not a trace of writing now remains beyond a single scratch, such as a man might make with his thumb-nail.

That was the end of the night's business. Soon after, with a drink all round, we lay down to sleep, and the outside of Silver's vengeance was to put George Merry up for sentinel and threaten him with death if he should prove unfaithful.

It was long ere I could close an eye, and heaven knows I had matter enough for thought in the man whom I had slain that afternoon, in my own most perilous position, and above all, in the remarkable game that I saw Silver now engaged upon—keeping the mutineers together with one hand and grasping with the other after every means, possible and impossible, to make his peace and save his miserable life. He himself slept peacefully and snored aloud, yet my heart was sore for him, wicked as he was, to think on the dark perils that environed him and the shameful gibbet that awaited him.

I was wakened by a clear, hearty voice hailing us from the margin of the wood: "Block house, ahoy! Here's the doctor."

And the doctor it was. Glad as I was, my gladness was not without admixture. I remembered my insubordinate and stealthy conduct, and felt ashamed to look him in the face.

"You, doctor! Top o' the morning to you, sir!" cried Silver, broad awake and beaming with good nature in a moment. "We've quite a surprise for you, sir — a noo boarder and lodger, looking fit and taut as a fiddle; slep' like a supercargo, right alongside of John."

I could hear the alteration in the doctor's voice as he said, "Not Jim?"

"The very same Jim as ever was," says Silver.

The doctor stopped outright. "Well, well," he said at last, "duty first and pleasure afterwards, as you might have said yourself, Silver. Let us overhaul these patients of yours."

He entered with one grim nod to me and proceeded among the sick, rattling on as if paying an ordinary professional visit in a quiet English family. His manner reacted on the men, who behaved as if he were still ship's doctor and they faithful hands before the mast.

"Because, you see, since I am mutineers' doctor, or prison doctor as I prefer to call it," says he, "I make it a point of honour not to lose a man for King George (God bless him!) and the gallows."

The rogues swallowed the home-thrust in silence. He dosed them round, and they took his prescriptions with really laughable humility, more like charity schoolchildren than blood-guilty mutineers.

"Well, that's done for today. And now I should wish to have a talk with that boy, please."

A mutineer at the door swung round with a deep flush and cried "No!" and swore.

Silver struck the barrel with his open hand. "Si-lence!" he roared, looking about him positively like a lion. "Hawkins, will you give me your word of honour as a young gentleman not to slip your cable?"

I readily gave the pledge.

"Then, doctor, you just step outside o' that stockade, and I'll bring the boy down on the inside, and I reckon you can yarn through the spars."

The explosion of disapproval that broke out as the doctor left accused Silver of playing double — the identical thing he was doing. But he called them all the fools and dolts you can imagine, fluttered the chart in their faces, asked if they could afford to break the treaty the very day they were bound a-treasure-hunting.

"No, by thunder!" he cried. "It's us must break the treaty when the time comes; and till then I'll gammon that doctor, if I have to ile his boots with brandy."

Then, hand on my shoulder, he stalked out upon his crutch. "Slow, lad, slow," he said. "They might round upon us in a twinkle of an eye if we was seen to hurry."

When we reached the stockade, Silver stopped. "You'll make a note of this, doctor. When a man's steering as near the wind as me, you wouldn't think it too much to give him one good word? It's not my life only now — it's that boy's into the bargain."

Silver was a changed man now his back was to his friends; his cheeks seemed fallen in, his voice trembled. Never was a soul more dead in earnest. He stepped back out of earshot and sat upon a tree-stump, whistling, spinning round to keep both me and his ruffians in view.

"So, Jim," said the doctor sadly, "here you are. As you have brewed, so shall you drink. When Captain Smollett was well, you dared not have gone off; and when he was ill and couldn't help it, by George, it was downright cowardly!"

I began to weep. "Doctor, I have blamed myself enough; my life's forfeit anyway, and I should have been dead but for Silver. What I fear is torture. If they come to torture me—"

"Jim," the doctor interrupted, his voice quite changed, "whip over, and we'll run for it."

"Doctor, I passed my word."

"I know, I know. I'll take it on my shoulders, holus bolus. Jump! One jump, and we'll run for it like antelopes."

"No," I replied; "you know right well you wouldn't do the thing yourself. Silver trusted me; I passed my word, and back I go. But, doctor — if they torture me, I might let slip where the ship is. I got the ship; she lies in North Inlet, on the southern beach, and just below high water."

"The ship!" exclaimed the doctor.

I described my adventures, and he heard me out in silence. "There is a kind of fate in this. Every step, it's you that saves our lives; do you suppose we are going to let you lose yours? You found the plot; you found Ben Gunn. Silver!" he cried as the cook drew near. "Don't be in any great hurry after that treasure."

"Why, sir, I can only save my life and the boy's by seeking for that treasure."

"Well, Silver — if that is so, I'll go one step further: look out for squalls when you find it."

"Sir, as between man and man, that's too much and too little. What you're after, why you left the block house, why you given me that there chart, I don't know. If you won't tell me plain out, just say so and I'll leave the helm."

"I've no right to say more; it's not my secret. But if we both get alive out of this wolf-trap, I'll do my best to save you, short of perjury. Keep the boy close beside you, and when you need help, halloo. I'm off to seek it for you. Good-bye, Jim."

Dr. Livesey shook hands with me through the stockade, nodded to Silver, and set off at a brisk pace into the wood.

"Jim," said Silver, "if I saved your life, you saved mine; and I'll not forget it. I seen the doctor waving you to run for it, and seen you say no, as plain as hearing. Jim, that's one to you. This is the first glint of hope I had since the attack failed. Now, you and me must stick close, back to back like, and we'll save our necks in spite o' fate and fortune."

A man hailed us that breakfast was ready. They had lit a fire fit to roast an ox, cooked three times more than we could eat, and one of them, with an empty laugh, threw what was left into the flames. I never saw men so careless of the morrow; bold enough for a brush, I could see their entire unfitness for prolonged campaign.

Even Silver, eating away with Captain Flint upon his shoulder, had not a word of blame, though I thought he had never shown himself so cunning.

"Aye, mates," said he, "it's lucky you have Barbecue to think for you with this here head. Sure enough, they have the ship. Once we hit the treasure, we'll find out where; and then, mates, us that has the boats has the upper hand. As for hostage, we'll keep him like so much gold, in case of accidents. Once we got the ship and treasure both, and off to sea like jolly companions, then we'll talk Mr. Hawkins over, and give him his share for all his kindness."

For my part, I was horribly cast down. Silver, already doubly a traitor, would not hesitate; he had still a foot in either camp, and would prefer wealth with the pirates to a bare escape from hanging. Even if he kept faith with the doctor, what a moment when his followers' suspicion turned to certainty and he and I — a cripple and a boy — must fight for our lives against five seamen!

We made a curious figure, all in soiled sailor clothes and all but me armed to the teeth — Silver with two guns, the great cutlass, and a pistol in each pocket, Captain Flint gabbling on his shoulder. I had a line about my waist and followed after the sea-cook like a dancing bear. The others carried picks and shovels, or pork and bread and brandy from our stock; for had Silver not bargained with the doctor, his mutineers, deserted by the ship, must have been driven to clear water and the proceeds of their hunting.

We pulled over the anchorage in two gigs, and as we went there was some discussion of the chart. The note ran thus:

> Tall tree, Spy-glass shoulder, bearing a point to the N. of N.N.E.
> Skeleton Island E.S.E. and by E.
> Ten feet.

A tall tree was the principal mark. The anchorage was bounded by a plateau dotted thickly with pines, here and there one rising clear above its neighbours. Which was Flint's tree could only be decided on the spot. Every man had picked a favourite ere we were half-way over, Long John alone shrugging his shoulders and bidding them wait.

Landing at the mouth of a wooded river, we began to ascend. Heavy, miry ground delayed us; but by little and little the hill steepened, and the wood changed character — heavy-scented broom and flowering shrubs taking the place of grass, thickets of green nutmeg dotted with the red columns and broad shadow of pines. The air was fresh and stirring.

The party spread fan-shape, shouting and leaping. Silver and I followed in the centre, well behind — I tethered by my rope, he ploughing among the sliding gravel, sometimes needing my hand.

We had proceeded half a mile when a man on the farthest left began to cry aloud, as in terror. The others ran in his direction.

It was not the treasure. At the foot of a pretty big pine, involved in a creeper, lay a human skeleton with a few shreds of clothing. A chill struck to every heart.

"He was a seaman," said one, examining the rags. "This is good sea-cloth."

"Aye, like enough," said Silver. "But what sort of a way is that for bones to lie? 'Tain't in natur'."

On a second glance, it seemed impossible to fancy the body in a natural position. The man lay perfectly straight — his feet pointing one way, his hands raised above his head like a diver's, the opposite.

"I've taken a notion into my old numbskull," observed Silver. "Here's the compass; there's the tip-top p'int o' Skeleton Island, stickin' out like a tooth. Just take a bearing along the line of them bones."

It was done. The body pointed straight in the direction of the island, and the compass read duly E.S.E. and by E.

"I thought so," cried the cook. "This here is a p'inter. But, by thunder! If it don't make me cold inside to think of Flint. This is one of HIS jokes, and no mistake. Him and six was alone here; he killed 'em every man, and laid this one down by compass, shiver my timbers!"

"There ain't a thing left here," said another, feeling round; "not a copper doit nor a baccy box. It don't look nat'ral."

"No, by gum, it don't," agreed Silver. "Messmates, but if Flint was living, this would be a hot spot for you and me. Six they were, and six are we; and bones is what they are now."

"Dead and gone below," said the fellow with the bandage; "but if ever sperrit walked, it would be Flint's. Dear heart, but he died bad, did Flint!"

"Come, come," said Silver; "stow this talk. He's dead, and he don't walk. Care killed a cat. Fetch ahead for the doubloons."

We started; but in spite of the staring daylight, the pirates no longer ran separate and shouting, but kept side by side and spoke with bated breath. The terror of the dead buccaneer had fallen on their spirits.

Partly from the alarm, partly to rest Silver and the sick, we sat down on the brow. The plateau commanded a wide prospect: the Cape of the Woods fringed with surf before us; behind, the anchorage and Skeleton Island, and open sea on the east. Sheer above rose the Spy-glass.

Silver took bearings with his compass. "There are three 'tall trees,'" said he, "about in the right line from Skeleton Island. It's child's play to find the stuff now."

The men muttered of Flint, of his blue face, of the rum that had taken him. All of a sudden, out of the trees, a thin, high, trembling voice struck up the well-known air:

> "Fifteen men on the dead man's chest—
> Yo-ho-ho, and a bottle of rum!"

I never have seen men more dreadfully affected. The colour went from their faces like enchantment; some leaped to their feet, some clawed hold of others; one grovelled on the ground.

"It's Flint, by ——!" cried a mutineer.

The song stopped as suddenly as it began, broken off as if a hand had been laid upon the singer's mouth. It had sounded airily and sweetly to me; the effect on my companions was the stranger.

"Come," said Silver, struggling with his ashen lips. "This won't do. Stand by to go about. It's someone skylarking — someone that's flesh and blood, and you may lay to that."

His courage came back as he spoke. The others had begun to lend an ear when the same voice broke out again — a faint distant hail that echoed fainter among the clefts of the Spy-glass.

"Darby M'Graw!" it wailed. "Darby M'Graw! Darby M'Graw!" again and again; and then, with an oath I leave out: "Fetch aft the rum, Darby!"

The buccaneers remained rooted, eyes starting from their heads.

"That fixes it!" gasped one. "Let's go."

"They was his last words," moaned another, "his last words above board."

Still Silver was unconquered. "Nobody in this here island ever heard of Darby," he muttered. "Shipmates, I'll not be beat by man or devil. I never was feared of Flint alive, and, by the powers, I'll face him dead. There's seven hundred thousand pound not a quarter of a mile from here. When did ever a gentleman o' fortune show his stern to that much dollars for a boozy old seaman with a blue mug — and him dead too?"

But there was no sign of reawakening courage; rather, growing terror at the irreverence of his words.

"Belay there, John!" said one. "Don't you cross a sperrit."

The rest were too terrified to reply; fear kept them close by John, as if his daring helped them. He had pretty well fought his weakness down.

"Sperrit? Well, maybe," he said. "But there's one thing not clear to me. There was an echo. Now, no man ever seen a sperrit with a shadow; what's he doing with an echo? That ain't in natur', surely?"

This argument seemed weak enough to me. But to my wonder, the men were greatly relieved.

"You've a head upon your shoulders, John, and no mistake. 'Bout ship, mates! It was like Flint's voice, but not just so clear-away. It was liker somebody else's voice—"

"By the powers, Ben Gunn!" roared Silver.

"Aye, and so it were!" cried another. "Ben Gunn it were!"

"Why, nobody minds Ben Gunn," cried a third; "dead or alive, nobody minds him."

It was extraordinary how their spirits returned. They shouldered the tools and set forth, walking by Silver's compass. He had said the truth: dead or alive, nobody minded Ben Gunn.

Striking near north-west, the first tall tree by the bearings proved wrong. So with the second. The third rose nearly two hundred feet above a clump of underwood — a giant of a vegetable, with a red column as big as a cottage, and a wide shadow around.

But it was not its size that now impressed my companions; it was the knowledge that seven hundred thousand pounds in gold lay buried below its shadow. The thought swallowed up their terrors. Their eyes burned; their feet grew speedier; their whole soul was bound up in that fortune.

Silver hobbled, grunting, on his crutch; his nostrils quivered; he plucked furiously at the line and turned his eyes upon me with a deadly look. I read his thoughts like print. His promise and the doctor's warning were things of the past; he hoped to seize the treasure, board the Hispaniola by night, cut every honest throat on the island, and sail away laden with crimes and riches.

Shaken with these alarms, it was hard to keep up. Now and again I stumbled, and Silver plucked roughly at the rope. I was haunted by the thought of the tragedy once acted on that plateau, when the buccaneer with the blue face had cut down his six accomplices with his own hand.

We were now at the margin of the thicket.

"Huzza, mates, all together!" shouted one in the lead; and the foremost broke into a run.

And suddenly, not ten yards further, we beheld them stop. A low cry arose. Silver doubled his pace, digging away like one possessed; next moment he and I had come also to a dead halt.

Before us was a great excavation, the sides fallen in and grass sprouted on the bottom. In it were the shaft of a broken pick and the boards of several packing-cases strewn around. On one I saw, branded with a hot iron, the name WALRUS — the name of Flint's ship.

All was clear to probation. The cache had been found and rifled; the seven hundred thousand pounds were gone!

The Fall of a Chieftain.

There never was such an overturn in this world. Each of the six men was as though he had been struck; but with Silver the blow passed almost instantly. He kept his head, found his temper, and changed his plan before the others had had time to realize the disappointment.

"Jim," he whispered, "take that, and stand by for trouble."

And he passed me a double-barrelled pistol. He began quietly moving northward, putting the hollow between us two and the other five. His looks were not quite friendly, and I was so revolted at these constant changes that I could not forbear whispering, "So you've changed sides again."

The buccaneers, with oaths and cries, leapt one after another into the pit and dug with their fingers. Morgan found a two-guinea piece, which went from hand to hand among them.

"Two guineas!" roared Merry, shaking it at Silver. "That's your seven hundred thousand pounds, is it? You're the man for bargains, ain't you?"

"Dig away, boys," said Silver with the coolest insolence; "you'll find some pig-nuts and I shouldn't wonder."

"Pig-nuts!" repeated Merry in a scream. "Mates, that man there knew it all along. Look in the face of him and you'll see it wrote there."

"Ah, Merry," remarked Silver, "standing for cap'n again? You're a pushing lad, to be sure."

This time everyone was entirely in Merry's favour. They scrambled out of the excavation, darting furious glances behind them—all upon the opposite side from Silver. There we stood, two on one side, five on the other, the pit between us, and nobody screwed up to offer the first blow. Silver never moved; he watched them, very upright on his crutch, and looked as cool as ever I saw him. He was brave, and no mistake.

At last Merry seemed to think a speech might help matters. "Mates," says he, "there's two of them alone there; one's the old cripple that brought us all here and blundered us down to this; the other's that cub that I mean to have the heart of. Now, mates—"

He was raising his arm and his voice, and plainly meant to lead a charge. But just then—crack! crack! crack!—three musket-shots flashed out of the thicket. Merry tumbled head foremost into the excavation; the man with the bandage spun round like a teetotum and fell dead; and the other three turned and ran for it with all their might.

Before you could wink, Long John had fired two barrels of his pistol into the struggling Merry. "George," said he, "I reckon I settled you."

At the same moment the doctor, Gray, and Ben Gunn joined us with smoking muskets from among the nutmeg-trees.

"Forward!" cried the doctor. "Double quick, my lads. We must head 'em off the boats."

We set off at a great pace, and the work Silver went through, leaping on his crutch till the muscles of his chest were fit to burst, was work no sound man ever equalled. The three survivors were already running for Mizzenmast Hill, and we were between them and the boats. We four sat down to breathe while Long John came slowly up, mopping his face.

As we proceeded leisurely downhill, the doctor related what had taken place. Ben Gunn, in his long lonely wanderings, had found the skeleton and the treasure; he had dug it up—the haft of his pick-axe lay broken in the excavation—and had carried it, in many weary journeys, to a cave on the two-pointed hill, where it had lain stored since two months before our arrival. When the doctor had wormed this secret from him on the afternoon of the attack, he had gone next morning to Silver and given him the now-useless chart—given him the stores—given anything, to get our party in safety from the malarial stockade to the cave.

"As for you, Jim," he said, "it went against my heart, but I did what I thought best for those who had stood by their duty; and if you were not one of these, whose fault was it?"

"Ah," said Silver, "it were fortunate for me that I had Hawkins here. You would have let old John be cut to bits, and never given it a thought, doctor."

"Not a thought," replied Dr. Livesey cheerily.

The doctor demolished one gig with the pick-axe, and we took the other round by sea for North Inlet. Silver, almost killed with fatigue, was set to an oar like the rest. Passing the two-pointed hill we saw the squire standing by the black mouth of Ben Gunn's cave, and gave him three cheers, in which Silver joined as heartily as any. Three miles farther, we met the Hispaniola cruising by herself; the last flood had lifted her, with little amiss beyond the wreck of the main-sail. We dropped a fresh anchor, and Gray returned single-handed to pass the night on guard.

At the cave the squire met us. To me he was cordial and kind, saying nothing of my escapade either in blame or praise. At Silver's polite salute he flushed.

"John Silver," he said, "you're a prodigious villain and imposter—a monstrous imposter, sir. I am told I am not to prosecute you. Well, then, I will not. But the dead men, sir, hang about your neck like mill-stones."

"Thank you kindly, sir," replied Long John, again saluting.

"I dare you to thank me!" cried the squire. "Stand back."

We entered the cave. It was a large, airy place, with a little spring and a pool of clear water, overhung with ferns. Before a big fire lay Captain Smollett; and in a far corner, only duskily flickered over by the blaze, I beheld great heaps of coin and quadrilaterals built of bars of gold. That was Flint's treasure that we had come so far to seek and that had cost already the lives of seventeen men from the Hispaniola. How many it had cost in the amassing, what blood and sorrow, what good ships scuttled on the deep, what brave men walking the plank blindfold, what shot of cannon, what shame and lies and cruelty, perhaps no man alive could tell.

"Come in, Jim," said the captain. "You're a good boy in your line, Jim, but I don't think you and me'll go to sea again. You're too much of the born favourite for me. Is that you, John Silver? What brings you here, man?"

"Come back to my dooty, sir," returned Silver.

"Ah!" said the captain, and that was all he said.

What a supper I had of it that night, with all my friends around me; and what a meal it was, with Ben Gunn's salted goat and a bottle of old wine from the Hispaniola. Never were people gayer or happier. And there was Silver, sitting back almost out of the firelight, but eating heartily, prompt to spring forward when anything was wanted, even joining quietly in our laughter—the same bland, polite, obsequious seaman of the voyage out.

And Last.

The next morning we fell early to work, transporting the gold near a mile by land to the beach, and three miles by boat to the Hispaniola. The three fellows still abroad on the island did not greatly trouble us; a single sentry on the shoulder of the hill was sufficient against any sudden onslaught.

Gray and Ben Gunn came and went with the boat, while the rest of us piled treasure on the beach. Two of the bars, slung in a rope's end, made a good load for a grown man. As I was not much use at carrying, I was kept busy all day in the cave packing the minted money into bread-bags.

It was a strange collection, like Billy Bones's hoard for the diversity of coinage, but so much larger and so much more varied that I think I never had more pleasure than in sorting them. English, French, Spanish, Portuguese, Georges, and Louises, doubloons and double guineas and moidores and sequins, the pictures of all the kings of Europe for the last hundred years, strange Oriental pieces stamped with what looked like wisps of string or bits of spider's web, round pieces and square pieces, and pieces bored through the middle, as if to wear them round your neck—nearly every variety of money in the world must have found a place in that collection; and for number, I am sure they were like autumn leaves, so that my back ached with stooping and my fingers with sorting them out.

At last—I think it was on the third night—the doctor and I were strolling on the shoulder of the hill when, from out the thick darkness below, the wind brought us a noise between shrieking and singing.

"Heaven forgive them," said the doctor; "'tis the mutineers!"

"All drunk, sir," struck in the voice of Silver from behind us.

Silver was allowed his entire liberty, and in spite of daily rebuffs seemed to regard himself once more as quite a privileged and friendly dependent. None treated him better than a dog, unless it was Ben Gunn, who was still terribly afraid of his old quartermaster, or myself, who had really something to thank him for.

"Drunk or raving," said the doctor.

"Right you were, sir," replied Silver; "and precious little odds which, to you and me."

"I suppose you would hardly ask me to call you a humane man," returned the doctor with a sneer. "But if I were sure they were raving—as I am morally certain one, at least, of them is down with fever—I should leave this camp, and at whatever risk to my own carcass, take them the assistance of my skill."

"Ask your pardon, sir, you would be very wrong," quoth Silver. "You would lose your precious life, and you may lay to that. These men down there, they couldn't keep their word—no, not supposing they wished to."

"No," said the doctor. "You're the man to keep your word, we know that."

That was about the last news we had of the three pirates. A council was held, and it was decided that we must desert them on the island. We left a good stock of powder and shot, the bulk of the salt goat, a few medicines, tools, clothing, a spare sail, and by the particular desire of the doctor, a handsome present of tobacco.

At last, one fine morning, we weighed anchor and stood out of North Inlet, the same colours flying that the captain had flown and fought under at the palisade. Coming through the narrows, we had to lie very near the southern point, and there we saw all three of them kneeling together on a spit of sand, with their arms raised in supplication. It went to all our hearts to leave them in that wretched state; but we could not risk another mutiny, and to take them home for the gibbet would have been a cruel sort of kindness. The doctor hailed them and told them where to find the stores. But they continued to call us by name and appeal to us, for God's sake, to be merciful.

At last, seeing the ship still bore on her course, one of them leapt to his feet with a hoarse cry, whipped his musket to his shoulder, and sent a shot whistling over Silver's head and through the main-sail. After that we kept under cover of the bulwarks; and when next I looked out the spit had melted out of sight in the growing distance. Before noon, to my inexpressible joy, the highest rock of Treasure Island had sunk into the blue round of sea.

We laid her head for the nearest port in Spanish America, for we could not risk the voyage home without fresh hands. It was just at sundown when we cast anchor in a most beautiful land-locked gulf, and were immediately surrounded by shore boats selling fruits and offering to dive for bits of money. The sight of so many good-humoured faces, the taste of the tropical fruits, and above all the lights that began to shine in the town made a most charming contrast to our dark and bloody sojourn on the island.

Ben Gunn was on deck alone when we came aboard at daybreak, and with wonderful contortions began to make us a confession. Silver was gone. The maroon had connived at his escape in a shore boat some hours before, assuring us he had only done so to preserve our lives. The sea-cook had not gone empty-handed: he had cut through a bulkhead unobserved and removed one of the sacks of coin, worth perhaps three or four hundred guineas. I think we were all pleased to be so cheaply quit of him.

To make a long story short, we got a few hands on board, made a good cruise home, and the Hispaniola reached Bristol. Five men only of those who had sailed returned with her. "Drink and the devil had done for the rest," with a vengeance, although we were not quite in so bad a case as that other ship they sang about:

     With one man of her crew alive,
     What put to sea with seventy-five.

All of us had an ample share of the treasure and used it wisely or foolishly, according to our natures. Captain Smollett is now retired from the sea. Gray studied his profession, and is now mate and part owner of a fine full-rigged ship, married besides, and the father of a family. As for Ben Gunn, he got a thousand pounds, which he spent or lost in three weeks, or to be more exact, in nineteen days, for he was back begging on the twentieth. Then he was given a lodge to keep, exactly as he had feared upon the island; and he still lives, a great favourite, though something of a butt, with the country boys, and a notable singer in church on Sundays and saints' days.

Of Silver we have heard no more. That formidable seafaring man with one leg has at last gone clean out of my life; but I dare say he met his old Negress, and perhaps still lives in comfort with her and Captain Flint. It is to be hoped so, I suppose, for his chances of comfort in another world are very small.

The bar silver and the arms still lie, for all that I know, where Flint buried them; and certainly they shall lie there for me. Oxen and wain-ropes would not bring me back again to that accursed island; and the worst dreams that ever I have are when I hear the surf booming about its coasts or start upright in bed with the sharp voice of Captain Flint still ringing in my ears: "Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight!"

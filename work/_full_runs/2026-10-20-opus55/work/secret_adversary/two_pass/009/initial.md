TAKEN aback though he was by the man’s words, Tommy did not hesitate. If audacity had successfully carried him so far, it was to be hoped it would carry him yet farther. He quietly passed into the house and mounted the ramshackle staircase. Everything in the house was filthy beyond words, and in every angle was a grey mass of cobweb.

Tommy proceeded leisurely. By the time he reached the bend of the staircase, he had heard the man below disappear into a back room. Clearly no suspicion attached to him as yet.

At the top of the stairs Tommy halted. In front of him ran a narrow passage, with doors opening on either side of it. From the one nearest him on the left came a low murmur of voices. It was this room which he had been directed to enter. But what held his glance was a small recess immediately on his right, half concealed by a torn velvet curtain. It was directly opposite the left-hand door and also commanded a good view of the upper part of the staircase. He thought things over in his usual slow and steady way, deciding that the mention of “Mr. Brown” was not a request for an individual, but in all probability a password used by the gang. His lucky use of it had gained him admission. But inside that room a further password might be required, or some proof of identity. Luck had served him very well so far, but there was such a thing as trusting it too far. To enter that room was a colossal risk.

A repetition of the signal knock sounded on the door below, and Tommy, his mind made up, slipped quickly into the recess and drew the curtain across so that it shielded him completely. Several rents in the ancient material afforded him a good view.

The man who came up the staircase with a furtive, soft-footed tread was quite unknown to Tommy. He was obviously of the very dregs of society. He stopped at the door opposite and gave the signal knock. A voice inside called out something, and the man opened the door and passed in, affording Tommy a momentary glimpse of the room. Four or five people sat round a long table, but his attention was caught by a tall man with close-cropped hair and a short, pointed, naval-looking beard, who sat at the head of the table with papers in front of him. As the new-comer entered he glanced up, and with a correct, but curiously precise enunciation, he asked:

“Your number, comrade?”

“Fourteen, gov’nor,” replied the other hoarsely.

“Correct.”

The door shut again.

“If that isn’t a Hun, I’m a Dutchman!” said Tommy to himself. “And running the show darned systematically too--as they always do. Lucky I didn’t roll in. I’d have given the wrong number, and there would have been the deuce to pay.”

The next visitor proved to be of an entirely different type. Tommy recognized in him an Irish Sinn Feiner. Certainly Mr. Brown’s organization was a far-reaching concern. The common criminal, the well-bred Irish gentleman, the pale Russian, and the efficient German master of the ceremonies! Truly a strange and sinister gathering! Who was this man who held in his finger these curiously variegated links of an unknown chain?

The procedure was exactly the same. The signal knock, the demand for a number, and the reply “Correct.”

Two knocks followed in quick succession. The first man Tommy put down as a city clerk, quiet and rather shabbily dressed. The second was of the working classes, and his face was vaguely familiar to the young man.

Three minutes later came another, a man of commanding appearance, exquisitely dressed, and evidently well born. His face, again, was not unknown to the watcher, though he could not for the moment put a name to it.

The last-comer came up the stairs so quietly that he was almost abreast of Tommy before the young man had realized his presence. He was a small man, very pale, with a gentle almost womanish air. The angle of the cheek-bones hinted at his Slavonic ancestry. As he passed the recess, he turned his head slowly. The strange light eyes seemed to burn through the curtain; Tommy could hardly believe that the man did not know he was there, and in spite of himself he shivered. The creature reminded him of a venomous snake.

The new-comer knocked as all had done, but his reception was very different. The bearded man rose to his feet, and all the others followed suit. The German came forward and shook hands. His heels clicked together.

“We are honoured,” he said. “We are greatly honoured. I much feared that it would be impossible.”

The other answered in a low voice that had a kind of hiss in it:

“There were difficulties. It will not be possible again, I fear. But one meeting is essential--to define my policy. I can do nothing without--Mr. Brown. He is here?”

The German replied with slight hesitation:

“We have received a message. It is impossible for him to be present in person.” He stopped, giving a curious impression of having left the sentence unfinished.

A very slow smile overspread the face of the other. He looked round at a circle of uneasy faces.

“Ah! I understand. I have read of his methods. He works in the dark and trusts no one. But, all the same, it is possible that he is among us now....” He looked round him again, and again that expression of fear swept over the group. Each man seemed eyeing his neighbour doubtfully.

The German indicated the place he had been occupying at the head of the table. The Russian demurred, but the other insisted.

“It is the only possible place,” he said, “for--Number One. Perhaps Number Fourteen will shut the door?”

The voices within sank once more to a mere undistinguishable murmur. Tommy felt that, by hook or by crook, he must hear more. The passage was deserted. He removed his shoes, left them behind the curtain, and walked gingerly out on his stockinged feet. Kneeling by the closed door, he laid his ear to the crack, but could distinguish little more than a chance word here and there.

Very slowly, a fraction of an inch at a time, he turned the handle, holding his breath. Then he pressed ever so slightly inward. The door did not budge. Finally, in desperation, he pushed with all his might, and at last the truth dawned upon him. It was locked or bolted on the inside.

For a moment or two Tommy’s indignation got the better of him.

“Well, I’m damned!” he said. “What a dirty trick!”

With the same infinite pains he restored the handle to its original position. There was a certain bulldog tenacity about Tommy that made him slow to admit defeat. A little farther along the passage on the left was a second door. He listened for a moment, then tried the handle. It yielded, and he slipped inside.

The room, which was untenanted, was furnished as a bedroom, and the dirt was, if anything, more abundant. But what interested Tommy was the thing he had hoped to find, a communicating door between the two rooms. The bolt was shot across it, very rusty. By gently wriggling it to and fro, Tommy managed to draw it back without making too much noise, then repeated his manœuvres with the handle--this time with complete success. The door swung open a crack, enough for Tommy to hear. A velvet _portière_ on the inside prevented him from seeing, but he was able to recognize the voices with a reasonable amount of accuracy.

The Sinn Feiner was speaking:

“That’s all very well. But more money is essential. No money--no results!”

Another voice which Tommy rather thought was that of Boris replied:

“Will you guarantee that there _are_ results?”

“In a month from now--sooner or later as you wish--I will guarantee you such a reign of terror in Ireland as shall shake the British Empire to its foundations.”

Then came the soft, sibilant accents of Number One:

“Good! You shall have the money. Boris, you will see to that.”

“I guess that’ll be all right!” said a new voice, with a transatlantic intonation, “though things are getting a mite difficult. There’s not the sympathy there was.”

Tommy felt that Boris had shrugged his shoulders as he answered:

“Does that matter, since the money only nominally comes from the States?”

“The chief difficulty is the landing of the ammunition,” said the Sinn Feiner. “The money is conveyed in easily enough--thanks to our colleague here.”

Another voice, which Tommy fancied was that of the tall, commanding-looking man whose face had seemed familiar to him, said:

“Think of the feelings of Belfast if they could hear you!”

“That is settled, then,” said the sibilant tones. “Now, in the matter of the loan to an English newspaper, you have arranged the details satisfactorily, Boris?”

“I think so.”

Then the clear voice of the German broke the silence:

“I am directed by--Mr. Brown, to place the summaries of the reports from the different unions before you. That of the miners is most satisfactory. We must hold back the railways.”

There was a long rustle of papers. Then Tommy heard the light tap-tap of fingers, drumming on the table.

“And--the date, my friend?” said Number One.

“The 29th.”

The Russian seemed to consider:

“That is rather soon.”

“I know. But it was settled by the principal Labour leaders, and we cannot seem to interfere too much. They must believe it to be entirely their own show.”

The Russian laughed softly.

“Yes, yes,” he said. “They must have no inkling that we are using them for our own ends. They are honest men--and that is their value to us.” He paused, and then repeated, as though the phrase pleased him: “Every revolution has had its honest men. They are soon disposed of afterwards.”

The German resumed:

“Clymes must go. He is too far-seeing. Number Fourteen will see to that.”

There was a hoarse murmur.

“That’s all right, gov’nor. Suppose I’m nabbed.”

“You will have the best legal talent to defend you,” replied the German quietly. “But in any case you will wear gloves fitted with the finger-prints of a notorious housebreaker.”

“Oh, I ain’t afraid, gov’nor. The streets is going to run with blood, so they say.” He spoke with a grim relish. “Dreams of it, sometimes, I does.”

Then Number One spoke:

“Then all is arranged. We are assured of success?”

“I--think so.” But the German spoke with less than his usual confidence.

Number One’s voice held suddenly a dangerous quality:

“What has gone wrong?”

“The Labour leaders,” said the German. “If they do not declare a general strike on the 29th---- As you’ve said, they’re honest. I’m not sure that they haven’t got a sneaking faith and belief in the Government. They will not go against it.”

Again the Russian’s fingers drummed on the table.

“To the point, my friend. I was given to understand that there was a certain document in existence which assured success.”

“That is so. If that document were placed before the leaders, they would declare for the revolution without a moment’s hesitation.”

“Then what more do you want?”

“The document itself,” said the German bluntly.

“Ah! It is not in your possession? But you know where it is?”

“No.”

“Does anyone know where it is?”

“One person--perhaps. And we are not sure of that even.”

“Who is this person?”

“A girl.”

Tommy held his breath.

“A girl?” The Russian’s voice rose contemptuously. “And you have not made her speak? In Russia we have ways of making a girl talk.”

“This case is different,” said the German sullenly.

“How--different? Where is the girl now?”

“She is----”

But Tommy heard no more. A crashing blow descended on his head, and all was darkness.

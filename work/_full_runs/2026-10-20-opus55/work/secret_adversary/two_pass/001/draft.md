“I beg your pardon.” A man’s voice beside her made her turn. She had noticed him amongst the first-class passengers: a mysterious man who spoke to no one and glanced suspiciously over his shoulder. Now he was greatly agitated, in overmastering fear. And yet he did not strike her as the kind of man who would be afraid to meet death!

He looked at her in desperate irresolution. “It must be!” he muttered to himself. “Yes--it is the only way.” Then aloud he said abruptly: “You are an American?”

“Yes.”

“A patriotic one?”

The girl flushed. “I guess you’ve no right to ask such a thing! Of course I am!”

“I’ve got to trust some one--and it must be a woman.”

“Why?”

“Because of ‘women and children first.’” He lowered his voice. “I’m carrying papers--vitally important papers. They may make all the difference to the Allies. They’ve more chance with you. Will you take them?”

The girl held out her hand.

“Wait--I must warn you. There may be a risk--if I’ve been followed. I don’t think I have.”

“I’m real proud to be chosen! What am I to do with them afterwards?”

“I’ll advertise in the _Times_, beginning ‘Shipmate.’ At the end of three days if there’s nothing, deliver the packet into the American Ambassador’s own hands.”

He took her hand. “Good-bye. Good luck to you,” he said loudly.

Her hand closed on the oilskin packet that had lain in his palm.

As the _Lusitania_ listed, the girl went forward to the boat.

## PROLOGUE

IT was 2 p.m. on May 7, 1915. The _Lusitania_, struck by two torpedoes, was sinking rapidly, and the women and children were being lined up for the boats. One girl, not more than eighteen, stood alone. She did not seem afraid, and her grave, steadfast eyes looked straight ahead.

“I beg your pardon.” She had noticed the speaker amongst the first-class passengers: a mysterious man who spoke to no one and glanced suspiciously over his shoulder. Now he was greatly agitated, in overmastering fear. And yet he did not strike her as the kind of man who would be afraid to meet death!

“It must be!” he muttered to himself. “Yes--it is the only way.” Then aloud he said abruptly: “You are an American?”

“Yes.”

“A patriotic one?”

The girl flushed. “I guess you’ve no right to ask such a thing! Of course I am!”

“I’ve got to trust some one--and it must be a woman.”

“Why?”

“Because of ‘women and children first.’” “I’m carrying papers--vitally important papers. They may make all the difference to the Allies. They’ve more chance with you. Will you take them?”

The girl held out her hand.

“Wait--I must warn you. There may be a risk--if I’ve been followed. I don’t think I have, but one never knows. Have you the nerve to go through with it?”

The girl smiled. “I’m real proud to be chosen! What am I to do with them afterwards?”

“I’ll advertise in the _Times_, beginning ‘Shipmate.’ At the end of three days if there’s nothing, deliver the packet into the American Ambassador’s own hands.”

He took her hand. “Good-bye. Good luck to you,” he said loudly.

Her hand closed on the oilskin packet that had lain in his palm.

As the _Lusitania_ listed, the girl went forward to the boat.

## CHAPTER I. THE YOUNG ADVENTURERS, LTD.

“TOMMY, old thing!”

“Tuppence, old bean!”

The two young people greeted each other affectionately, and momentarily blocked the Dover Street Tube exit in doing so. The adjective “old” was misleading. Their united ages would certainly not have totalled forty-five.

“Not seen you for simply centuries,” continued the young man. “Come and chew a bun with me.”

“Now then,” said Tommy, “where shall we go?”

The very faint anxiety which underlay his tone did not escape the astute ears of Miss Prudence Cowley, known to her intimate friends for some mysterious reason as “Tuppence.”

“Tommy, you’re stony!”

“Not a bit of it,” declared Tommy unconvincingly. “Rolling in cash.”

“You always were a shocking liar,” said Tuppence severely.

Tommy chuckled. “Good old hospital--demobbed like everything else, I suppose?”

Tuppence sighed. “Yes. You too?”

Tommy nodded. “Two months ago.”

“Gratuity?” hinted Tuppence.

“Spent.”

“Oh, Tommy!”

“No, old thing, not in riotous dissipation. The cost of living----”

“My dear child,” interrupted Tuppence, “there is nothing I do _not_ know about the cost of living. Here we are at Lyons’, and we will each of us pay for our own. That’s it!” And Tuppence led the way upstairs.

The place was full, and they wandered about looking for a table.

“Funny scraps one does overhear,” murmured Tommy. “I passed two Johnnies in the street to-day talking about some one called Jane Finn. Did you ever hear such a name?”

But at that moment two elderly ladies rose and collected parcels, and Tuppence deftly ensconced herself in one of the vacant seats.

Tommy sat down opposite her. His bared head revealed a shock of exquisitely slicked-back red hair. His face was pleasantly ugly--nondescript, yet unmistakably the face of a gentleman and a sportsman.

Tuppence had no claim to beauty, but there was character and charm in the elfin lines of her little face, with its determined chin and large, wide-apart grey eyes that looked mistily out from under straight, black brows. She wore a small bright green toque over her black bobbed hair. Her appearance presented a valiant attempt at smartness.

“Now then,” said Tommy, when the tea came, “let’s get up-to-date. Remember, I haven’t seen you since that time in hospital in 1916.”

“Very well.” Tuppence helped herself liberally to buttered toast. “Abridged biography of Miss Prudence Cowley, fifth daughter of Archdeacon Cowley of Little Missendell, Suffolk. First month: Washed up six hundred and forty-eight plates every day. Second month: Promoted to drying aforesaid plates. And so on, upwards and downwards, until the ninth month: Promoted to sweeping out wards, where I found a friend of my childhood in Lieutenant Thomas Beresford (bow, Tommy!). The meeting was affecting! After that, the talented Miss Cowley drove successively a trade delivery van, a motor-lorry and a general! The last was the pleasantest. He was quite a young general!”

“What blighter was that?” inquired Tommy.

“I’ve forgotten his name now,” confessed Tuppence. “Since then I’ve been looking for a job. Now then--your turn.”

“There’s not so much promotion in mine,” said Tommy regretfully. “I went out to France again, as you know. Then they sent me to Mesopotamia, and I got wounded for the second time. Then I got stuck in Egypt till the Armistice happened, and finally got demobbed. And, for ten long, weary months I’ve been job hunting! There aren’t any jobs!”

Tuppence nodded gloomily.

“Rich relations?” she suggested.

“I’ve got an old uncle who’s more or less rolling, but he’s no good.”

“Why not?”

“Wanted to adopt me once. I refused.”

“I think I remember hearing about it,” said Tuppence slowly. “You refused because of your mother----”

Tommy flushed.

“Yes, it would have been a bit rough on the mater. As you know, I was all she had.”

“Your mother’s dead, isn’t she?” said Tuppence gently.

Tommy nodded.

“You’re a good sort, Tommy. I always knew it.”

“Rot!” said Tommy hastily. “I’m just about desperate.”

“So am I! But it’s no good. I shall have to go home!”

“Don’t you want to?”

“Of course I don’t want to! What’s the good of being sentimental? Father’s a dear--I’m awfully fond of him--but you’ve no idea how I worry him! He has that delightful early Victorian view that short skirts and smoking are immoral. You see, there are seven of us at home. I don’t want to go back, but--oh, Tommy, what else is there to do?”

Tommy shook his head sadly. There was a silence, and then Tuppence burst out:

“Money, money, money! I think about money morning, noon and night! I dare say it’s mercenary of me, but there it is!”

“Same here,” agreed Tommy with feeling.

“I’ve thought over every imaginable way of getting it too,” continued Tuppence. “There are only three! To be left it, to marry it, or to make it. First is ruled out. I haven’t got any rich elderly relatives. Of course, marriage is my best chance. I made up my mind to marry money when I was quite young. Any thinking girl would! I’m not sentimental, you know.” She paused. “Come now, you can’t say I’m sentimental,” she added sharply.

“Certainly not,” agreed Tommy hastily. “No one would ever think of sentiment in connection with you.”

“That’s not very polite,” replied Tuppence. “But I dare say you mean it all right. Well, there it is! I’m ready and willing--but I never meet any rich men!”

“What about the general?” inquired Tommy.

“I fancy he keeps a bicycle shop in time of peace,” explained Tuppence. “No--marriage is fraught with difficulties. Remains--to _make_ money!”

“We’ve tried that, and failed,” Tommy reminded her.

“We’ve tried all the orthodox ways, yes. But suppose we try the unorthodox. Tommy, let’s be adventurers!”

“Certainly,” replied Tommy cheerfully. “How do we begin?”

“That’s the difficulty. If we could make ourselves known, people might hire us to commit crimes for them.”

“Delightful,” commented Tommy. “Especially coming from a clergyman’s daughter!”

“The moral guilt,” Tuppence pointed out, “would be theirs--not mine.”

“There wouldn’t be the least difference if you were caught!”

“Perhaps not. But I shouldn’t be caught. I’m so clever.”

“Modesty always was your besetting sin,” remarked Tommy.

“Don’t rag. Shall we form a business partnership? Let’s have a--what do you call it in book-keeping? Oh, I know--a joint venture! It struck me as such a romantic phrase to come across in the middle of musty old figures.”

“Trading under the name of the Young Adventurers, Ltd.? How do you propose to get in touch with your would-be employers?”

“Advertisement,” replied Tuppence promptly. “Have you got a bit of paper and a pencil?”

Tommy handed over a rather shabby green notebook, and Tuppence began writing busily.

“Now listen--how’s this? ‘Two young adventurers for hire. Willing to do anything, go anywhere. Pay must be good.’ Then we might add: ‘No reasonable offer refused’--like flats and furniture.”

“I should think any offer we get in answer to that would be a pretty _un_reasonable one!”

“Tommy! You’re a genius! ‘No unreasonable offer refused--if pay is good.’ How’s that?”

“I shouldn’t mention pay again. It looks rather eager.”

“It couldn’t look as eager as I feel! But perhaps you are right. ‘Two young adventurers for hire. Willing to do anything, go anywhere. Pay must be good. No unreasonable offer refused.’ How would that strike you if you read it?”

“It would strike me as either being a hoax, or else written by a lunatic.”

She tore out the leaf and handed it to Tommy. “There you are. _Times_, I think. Reply to Box so-and-so.”

Tommy was holding the paper thoughtfully. “Shall we really try it?” he said at last. “Just for the fun of the thing?”

“Tommy, you’re a sport! Let’s drink to success.” She poured some cold dregs of tea into the two cups.

“Here’s to our joint venture, and may it prosper!”

“The Young Adventurers, Ltd.!” responded Tommy.

They put down the cups and laughed rather uncertainly. Tuppence rose.

“Where shall we meet?” asked Tommy. “And when?”

“Twelve o’clock to-morrow. Piccadilly Tube station. Will that suit you?”

“My time is my own,” replied Mr. Beresford magnificently.

“So long, then.”

“Good-bye, old thing.”

The two young people went off in opposite directions. For reasons of economy Tuppence did not take a bus.

She was half-way across St. James’s Park, when a man’s voice behind her made her start.

“Excuse me,” it said. “But may I speak to you for a moment?”

## CHAPTER II. MR. WHITTINGTON’S OFFER

TUPPENCE turned sharply, but the words hovering on the tip of her tongue remained unspoken, for the man's appearance and manner did not bear out her first and most natural assumption. He said quickly:

"I can assure you I mean no disrespect."

Tuppence believed him. Although she disliked and distrusted him instinctively, she was inclined to acquit him of the particular motive which she had at first attributed to him. He was a big man, clean shaven, with a heavy jowl. His eyes were small and cunning, and shifted their glance under her direct gaze.

"Well, what is it?" she asked.

The man smiled. "I happened to overhear part of your conversation with the young gentleman in Lyons'."

"You followed me here?"

"I took that liberty."

He handed her a card with a bow. It bore the inscription, "Mr. Edward Whittington." Below the name were the words "Esthonia Glassware Co.," and the address of a city office.

"If you will call upon me to-morrow morning at eleven o'clock," Mr. Whittington said, "I will lay the details of my proposition before you."

"Very well. I'll be there."

He raised his hat with a flourish, and walked away. Then she gave a curious movement of her shoulders, rather as a terrier shakes himself.

"The adventures have begun," she murmured to herself. "And as I've said before, and shall doubtless say again, little Tuppence can look after herself, thank you!"

She presently turned aside and entered a post office. The thought of a possible five shillings spent unnecessarily spurred her to action, and she decided to risk the waste of ninepence. Tuppence drew out Tommy's pencil which she had retained and wrote rapidly: "Don't put in advertisement. Will explain to-morrow." She addressed it to Tommy at his club.

On the stroke of eleven Tuppence came to a halt outside the ground glass door. She knocked, and walked into a small rather dirty outer office.

A middle-aged clerk came towards her inquiringly.

"I have an appointment with Mr. Whittington," said Tuppence.

The clerk crossed to a partition door with "Private" on it, opened the door and stood aside to let her pass in.

Mr. Whittington was seated behind a large desk. The combination of his sleek prosperity and his shifty eye was not attractive.

"So you've turned up all right? That's good. Sit down, will you?"

Tuppence sat down facing him, looking particularly small and demure.

"You want work? Well, I have work to offer you. What should you say now to £100 down, and all expenses paid?"

Tuppence eyed him warily. "And the nature of the work?"

"Nominal--purely nominal. A pleasant trip, that is all."

"Where to?"

"Paris," said Mr. Whittington, smiling again. "To put the clock back a few years and re-enter one of those charming _pensionnats de jeunes filles_ with which Paris abounds----"

"A _pensionnat?_"

"Exactly. Madame Colombier's in the Avenue de Neuilly."

"For how long?" she asked.

"Possibly three months. You would go in the character of my ward, and you would hold no communication with your friends. I should have to request absolute secrecy for the time being. By the way, you are English, are you not? Yet you speak with a slight American accent?"

"My great pal in hospital was a little American girl," said Tuppence. "I dare say I picked it up from her. I can soon get out of it again."

"On the contrary, it might be simpler for you to pass as an American. Then----"

"One moment, Mr. Whittington! The terms are almost too liberal. I cannot see any way in which I can be worth that amount of money to you."

"No?" said Whittington softly. "What I am willing to pay for is a young lady with sufficient intelligence and presence of mind to sustain her part well, and also one who will have sufficient discretion not to ask too many questions."

"There's another thing," she said. "So far there has been no mention of Mr. Beresford. Where does he come in?"

"Mr. Beresford?"

"My partner," said Tuppence with dignity. "You saw us together yesterday."

"Ah, yes. But I'm afraid we shan't require his services."

"Then it's off!" Tuppence rose. "It's both or neither. Good morning, Mr. Whittington."

"Wait a minute. Sit down again, Miss----" He paused interrogatively.

Tuppence's conscience gave her a passing twinge as she remembered the archdeacon.

"Jane Finn," she said hastily; and then paused open-mouthed at the effect of those two simple words.

All the geniality had faded out of Whittington's face. It was purple with rage. And behind it all there lurked a sort of incredulous dismay. He leaned forward and hissed savagely:

"So that's your little game, is it?"

She had not the faintest comprehension of his meaning, but she felt it imperative to "keep her end up."

"Been playing with me, have you, all the time, like a cat and mouse?" Whittington went on. He eyed her keenly. "Who's been blabbing? Rita?"

Tuppence shook her head, realizing the importance of not dragging an unknown Rita into it.

"No," she replied with perfect truth. "Rita knows nothing about me."

"How much do you know?" he shot out.

"Very little indeed," answered Tuppence, and was pleased to note that Whittington's uneasiness was augmented instead of allayed.

"Anyway," snarled Whittington, "you knew enough to come in here and plump out that name."

"It might be my own name," Tuppence pointed out.

"It's likely, isn't it, then there would be two girls with a name like that?"

"Or I might just have hit upon it by chance," continued Tuppence, intoxicated with the success of truthfulness.

Mr. Whittington brought his fist down upon the desk with a bang.

"Quit fooling! How much do you know? And how much do you want?"

The last five words took Tuppence's fancy mightily, especially after a meagre breakfast and a supper of buns the night before.

"My dear Mr. Whittington," she said, "you heard me say yesterday that I proposed to live by my wits. I admit I have knowledge of a certain name, but perhaps my knowledge ends there."

"Yes--and perhaps it doesn't," snarled Whittington.

"I shouldn't like to contradict you, Mr. Whittington," Tuppence said softly.

"So we come to the usual question--how much?"

"Suppose we say a little something down, and a fuller discussion of the matter later?"

Whittington gave her an ugly glance. "Blackmail, eh?"

Tuppence smiled sweetly. "Oh no! Shall we say payment of services in advance?"

Whittington grunted.

"You see," explained Tuppence still sweetly, "I'm so very fond of money!"

"You're about the limit, that's what you are," growled Whittington, with a sort of unwilling admiration. "All the same, some one's been talking. You say it isn't Rita. Was it----? Oh, come in."

The clerk followed his discreet knock into the room, and laid a paper at his master's elbow.

"Telephone message just come for you, sir."

Whittington snatched it up and read it. A frown gathered on his brow.

"That'll do, Brown. You can go."

The clerk withdrew.

"Come to-morrow at the same time. I'm busy now. Here's fifty to go on with."

He rapidly sorted out some notes, and pushed them across the table. The girl counted the notes in a businesslike manner, secured them in her handbag, and rose.

"Good morning, Mr. Whittington," she said politely. "At least, au revoir, I should say."

"Exactly. Au revoir!" Whittington looked almost genial again, a reversion that aroused in Tuppence a faint misgiving. "Au revoir, my clever and charming young lady."

A neighbouring clock showed the time to be five minutes to twelve.

"Let's give Tommy a surprise!" murmured Tuppence, and hailed a taxi.

The cab drew up outside the tube station. Tommy was just within the entrance. She smiled at him affectionately, and remarked in a slightly affected voice:

"Pay the thing, will you, old bean? I've got nothing smaller than a five-pound note!"

## CHAPTER III. A SET BACK

THE moment was not quite so triumphant as it ought to have been. To begin with, the resources of Tommy’s pockets were somewhat limited. In the end the fare was managed, the lady recollecting a plebeian twopence, and the driver was prevailed upon to move on, which he did after one last hoarse demand as to what the gentleman thought he was giving him?

“And really and truly,” said Tuppence, opening her eyes very wide, “I haven’t got anything smaller than a five-pound note.”

“You did that part of it very well, old bean, but all the same the fellow wasn’t taken in--not for a moment!”

“No,” said Tuppence thoughtfully, “he didn’t believe it. That’s the curious part about speaking the truth. No one does believe it. Now let’s go to lunch. How about the _Savoy?_”

Tommy grinned. “How about the _Ritz?_”

“On second thoughts, I prefer the _Piccadilly_. Come along.”

“Is this a new brand of humour? Or is your brain really unhinged?” inquired Tommy.

“Your last supposition is the correct one. I have come into money, and the shock has been too much for me! For that particular form of mental trouble an eminent physician recommends unlimited _Hors d’œuvre_, Lobster _à l’américane_, Chicken Newberg, and Pêche Melba!” Tuppence wrenched open her bag. “Look here, and here, and here!”

Tommy groaned.

“And now tell me,” said Tommy, as they sat in state surrounded by the many _hors d’œuvre_ of Tuppence’s dreams.

Miss Cowley told him.

“And the curious part of it is,” she ended, “that I really did invent the name of Jane Finn!”

“Perhaps that’s so,” said Tommy slowly. “But you didn’t invent it.”

“What?”

“No. _I_ told it to you. Don’t you remember, I said yesterday I’d overheard two people talking about a female called Jane Finn?”

“So you did. How extraordinary----” Tuppence tailed off into silence. “Tommy! What were they like, the two men you passed?”

“One was a big fat sort of chap. Clean shaven, I think--and dark.”

“That’s him,” cried Tuppence, in an ungrammatical squeal. “That’s Whittington! What was the other man like?”

“I can’t remember. I didn’t notice him particularly.”

“And people say that coincidences don’t happen!”

But Tommy had become serious.

“Look here, Tuppence, old girl, you can’t bluff him forever. And, anyway, I’m not at all sure that it isn’t actionable--blackmail, you know.”

“Nonsense. Blackmail is saying you’ll tell unless you are given money. Now, there’s nothing I could tell, because I don’t really know anything.”

“Hm,” said Tommy doubtfully. “Well, anyway, what _are_ we going to do?”

Tuppence frowned severely. “We must think.”

“There!” said Tuppence at last. “I’ve got a plan. We can only find out through Whittington. We must discover where he lives, what he does--sleuth him, in fact! Now I can’t do it, because he knows me, but he only saw you for a minute or two in Lyons’. After all, one young man is much like another.”

“I repudiate that remark utterly. I’m sure my pleasing features and distinguished appearance would single me out from any crowd.”

“My plan is this,” Tuppence went on calmly, “I’ll go alone to-morrow. I’ll put him off again like I did to-day. You’ll hang about outside. When he comes out of the building I’ll drop a handkerchief or something, and off you go!”

“I somehow feel that in real life one will feel a bit of an ass standing in the street for hours with nothing to do.”

“Not in the city. Probably no one will even notice you at all.”

“That’s the second time you’ve made that sort of remark. What are you doing this afternoon?”

“Well,” said Tuppence meditatively. “I _had_ thought of hats!”

“Hold hard,” admonished Tommy. “There’s a limit to fifty pounds! But let’s do dinner and a show to-night at all events.”

The day passed pleasantly. The evening even more so. Two of the five-pound notes were now irretrievably dead.

They met by arrangement the following morning and proceeded citywards. Tommy remained on the opposite side of the road while Tuppence plunged into the building. Presently she darted back across the road.

“Tommy! The place is shut. Come up with me, and let’s try again.”

As they passed the third floor landing a young clerk came out of an office.

“Were you wanting the Esthonia Glassware? It’s closed down. Since yesterday afternoon. Company being wound up, they say. But anyway the office is to let.”

“Th--thank you,” faltered Tuppence. “I suppose you don’t know Mr. Whittington’s address?”

“Afraid I don’t. They left rather suddenly.”

“That’s torn it,” said Tommy at length.

“And I never suspected it,” wailed Tuppence.

“Cheer up, old thing, it can’t be helped.”

“Can’t it, though!” Tuppence’s little chin shot out defiantly. “Do you think this is the end? If so, you’re wrong. It’s just the beginning!”

“The beginning of what?”

“Of our adventure! Tommy, don’t you see, if they are scared enough to run away like this, it shows that there must be a lot in this Jane Finn business! We’ll be sleuths in earnest!”

“Yes, but there’s no one left to sleuth.”

“No, that’s why we’ll have to start all over again. Lend me that bit of pencil. Thanks.” Tuppence handed back the pencil, and handed him a slip of paper.

“Advertisement,” she said.

Tommy read the words on it aloud:

“WANTED, any information respecting Jane Finn. Apply Y. A.”

## CHAPTER IV. WHO IS JANE FINN?

THE next day passed slowly. Carefully husbanded, forty pounds will last a long time.

On Thursday the advertisement had duly appeared. On Friday letters might be expected to arrive at Tommy's rooms.

He had been bound by an honourable promise not to open any such letters if they did arrive, but to repair to the National Gallery, where his colleague would meet him at ten o'clock.

Tuppence was first at the rendezvous. She gazed at the Turners with unseeing eyes until she saw the familiar figure enter the room.

"Well?"

"Well," returned Mr. Beresford provokingly. "Which is your favourite picture?"

"Don't be a wretch. Aren't there _any_ answers?"

Tommy shook his head with a deep and somewhat overacted melancholy.

"I didn't want to disappoint you, old thing, by telling you right off. Good money wasted." He sighed. "Still, there it is. The advertisement has appeared, and--there are only two answers!"

"Tommy, you devil!" almost screamed Tuppence. "Give them to me. How could you be so mean!"

"Your language, Tuppence, your language! And do remember that as a clergyman's daughter----"

"I ought to be on the stage!" finished Tuppence with a snap.

Tuppence snatched the two precious envelopes from him unceremoniously. Her little thumb ripped open the first.

"DEAR SIR,

"Referring to your advertisement in this morning's paper, I may be able to be of some use to you. Perhaps you could call and see me at the above address at eleven o'clock to-morrow morning.

"Yours truly,

"A. CARTER."

"27 Carshalton Gardens," said Tuppence.

"The following," said Tommy, "is the plan of campaign. Mr. Carter says: 'Please take a seat, Mr.--er?' To which I reply promptly and significantly: 'Edward Whittington!' whereupon Mr. Carter turns purple in the face and gasps out: 'How much?'"

"Don't be absurd, Tommy. Now for the other letter. Oh, this is from the _Ritz!_ I'll read it:

"DEAR SIR,

"Re your advertisement, I should be glad if you would call round somewhere about lunch-time.

"Yours truly,

"JULIUS P. HERSHEIMMER."

"Ha!" said Tommy. "Do I smell a Boche? Or only an American millionaire of unfortunate ancestry? At all events we'll call at lunch-time. It's a good time--frequently leads to free food for two."

Tuppence nodded assent.

"Now for Carter. We'll have to hurry."

At No. 27 a neat maid showed them into a small study. Hardly a minute elapsed before a tall man with a lean hawklike face and a tired manner entered the room.

"Do sit down, both of you," he said, and smiled. His smile was distinctly attractive, and something in its quality made Tuppence's usual readiness desert her. She was forced to begin.

"We wanted to know--that is, would you be so kind as to tell us anything you know about Jane Finn?"

"Jane Finn? Ah!" Mr. Carter appeared to reflect. "Well, the question is, what do _you_ know about her?"

Tuppence drew herself up.

"I don't see that that's got anything to do with it."

"No? But it has, you know, really it has. Suppose you tell me...."

There was something very magnetic about Mr. Carter's personality. Tuppence seemed to shake herself free of it with an effort.

"We couldn't do that, could we, Tommy?"

But to her surprise, her companion did not back her up.

"I dare say the little we know won't be any good to you, sir. But such as it is, you're welcome to it."

"Tommy!" cried out Tuppence in surprise.

Mr. Carter's eyes asked a question. Tommy nodded.

"Yes, sir, I recognized you at once. Saw you in France when I was with the Intelligence. As soon as you came into the room, I knew----"

Mr. Carter held up his hand.

"No names, please. I'm known as Mr. Carter here. It's my cousin's house, by the way. She's willing to lend it to me sometimes when it's a case of working on strictly unofficial lines. Well, now, who's going to tell me the story?"

"Fire ahead, Tuppence," directed Tommy. "It's your yarn."

And obediently Tuppence told the whole story from the forming of the Young Adventurers, Ltd., downwards, through Mr. Whittington and his office.

When she had finished Mr. Carter nodded gravely.

"Not much. But suggestive. You might succeed where others have failed ... I believe in luck, you know--always have.... How would you like to work for me? All quite unofficial, you know. Expenses paid, and a moderate screw?"

"What should we have to do?" she breathed.

Mr. Carter smiled.

"Just go on with what you're doing now. _Find Jane Finn_."

"Yes, but--who _is_ Jane Finn?"

"Yes, you're entitled to know that, I think." He leaned back and began in a low monotone:

"In the early days of 1915 a certain document came into being--the draft of a secret agreement, drawn up in America, at that time a neutral country. It was dispatched to England by a special messenger, a young fellow called Danvers.

"Danvers sailed on the _Lusitania_. He carried the precious papers in an oilskin packet which he wore next his skin. On that voyage the _Lusitania_ was torpedoed and sunk. Eventually his body was washed ashore. But the oilskin packet was missing!

"After the torpedo struck, Danvers was seen speaking to a young American girl. No one actually saw him pass anything to her, but he might have done so. By later advice from America it seemed likely that Danvers had been closely shadowed.

"Her name was Jane Finn, and it duly appeared among the list of the survivors. She was an orphan, a pupil teacher in a small school out West, going to join the staff of a hospital in Paris. She never arrived. We tracked her across Ireland, but nothing could be heard of her after she set foot in England. No use was made of the draft treaty, and we therefore concluded that Danvers had destroyed it."

Tuppence broke in impatiently:

"But why has it all cropped up again? The war's over."

"Because it seems that the papers were not destroyed after all. Five years ago, that draft treaty was a weapon in our hands; to-day it is a weapon against us. It might possibly bring about another war--an extreme possibility, and I do not believe in its likelihood myself--but it undoubtedly implicates a number of our statesmen. As a party cry for Labour it would be irresistible--in my opinion a grave disability for British trade--but that is a mere nothing to the _real_ danger. Bolshevist gold is pouring into this country to procure a Revolution. And there is a certain man, whose real name is unknown to us, working in the dark for his own ends. The Bolshevists are behind the Labour unrest--but this man is _behind the Bolshevists_. He is always spoken of by the unassuming title of 'Mr. Brown.'"

"A naturalized German?" asked Tommy.

"On the contrary, I have every reason to believe he is an Englishman. What he seeks we do not know--probably supreme power for himself. Where we have come across his tracks, he has always played a secondary part. Somebody else assumes the chief rôle. But afterwards we always find that there has been some nonentity, a servant or a clerk, who has remained in the background unnoticed, and that the elusive Mr. Brown has escaped us once more."

"Oh!" Tuppence jumped. "I remember in Mr. Whittington's office. The clerk--he called him Brown. You don't think----"

Carter nodded thoughtfully.

"Very likely. Can you describe him at all?"

"I really didn't notice. He was quite ordinary--just like anyone else."

Mr. Carter sighed in his tired manner.

"That is the invariable description of Mr. Brown! Brought a telephone message to the man Whittington, did he? Notice a telephone in the outer office?"

"No, I don't think I did."

"Exactly. That 'message' was Mr. Brown's way of giving an order to his subordinate. He overheard the whole conversation of course. Possibly the finest criminal brain of the age. I don't quite like it, you know. You're such young things, both of you."

"I'll look after her, sir," said Tommy.

"And _I_'ll look after _you_," retorted Tuppence, resenting the manly assertion.

"Well, then, look after each other," said Mr. Carter, smiling. "Now there's something mysterious about this draft treaty. The Revolutionary element as good as declare that it's in their hands, yet they are clearly at fault about many of its provisions. It is as though they couldn't read it because it was in cipher--but we know the draft treaty wasn't in cipher--so that won't wash. The curious thing is that _they're trying to get information about the girl from us_."

"What?"

"Yes. And your story, little lady, confirms my idea. They know we're looking for Jane Finn. Well, they'll produce a Jane Finn of their own--say at a _pensionnat_ in Paris." Tuppence gasped. "No one knows in the least what she looks like. She's primed with a trumped-up tale, and her real business is to get information out of us."

"Then you think"--Tuppence paused--"that it _was_ as Jane Finn that they wanted me to go to Paris?"

Mr. Carter smiled more wearily than ever.

"I believe in coincidences, you know," he said.

## CHAPTER V. MR. JULIUS P. HERSHEIMMER

"Well," said Tuppence, recovering herself, "it really seems as though it were meant to be."

Carter nodded.

"I know what you mean. I'm superstitious myself. Luck, and all that sort of thing. Fate seems to have chosen you out to be mixed up in this. My experts have failed. There is a likelihood of the pace being forced."

Tuppence frowned uncomprehendingly.

"I have information that the big _coup_ was planned for early in the new year. But the Government is contemplating legislative action which will deal effectually with the strike menace. They'll get wind of it soon, and it's possible that that may bring things to a head. I'm just warning you that you haven't much time before you."

Tuppence rose.

"I think we ought to be businesslike. What exactly can we count upon you for, Mr. Carter?"

"Funds within reason, detailed information on any point, and _no official recognition_. I mean that if you get yourselves into trouble with the police, I can't officially help you out of it. You're on your own."

Tuppence nodded sagely.

"I quite understand that. I'll write out a list of the things I want to know when I've had time to think. Now--about money----"

"Yes, Miss Tuppence. Do you want to say how much?"

"Not exactly. But if we have to fill up a blue form and send it in, and then, after three months, they send us a green one--well, that won't be much use, will it?"

Mr. Carter laughed outright.

"You will send a personal demand to me here, and the money, in notes, shall be sent by return of post. As to salary, shall we say at the rate of three hundred a year? And an equal sum for Mr. Beresford, of course."

Tuppence beamed upon him.

"How lovely. You are kind. I do love money! I'll keep beautiful accounts of our expenses."

In another minute they were descending the steps with their heads in a whirl.

"Tommy! Tell me at once, who is 'Mr. Carter'?"

Tommy murmured a name in her ear.

"Oh!" said Tuppence, impressed.

"And I can tell you, old bean, he's IT!"

"Oh!" said Tuppence again. "He looks so awfully tired and bored, and yet you feel that underneath he's just like steel, all keen and flashing. Oh!" She gave a skip. "Pinch me, Tommy, do pinch me. I can't believe it's real!"

Mr. Beresford obliged.

"Ow! That's enough! We've got a job!"

"And what a job!" said Tommy. "What time is it? Let's have lunch--oh! Julius P. Hersheimmer! Come on, we'd better take a taxi."

"Now who's being extravagant?"

"All expenses paid, remember. Hop in."

"I'm sure blackmailers never arrive in buses!" said Tuppence, leaning back luxuriously.

"We've ceased being blackmailers," Tommy pointed out.

"I'm not sure I have," said Tuppence darkly.

On inquiring for Mr. Hersheimmer, they were at once taken up to his suite.

Mr. Julius P. Hersheimmer was a great deal younger than either Tommy or Tuppence had pictured him. The girl put him down as thirty-five. He was squarely built to match his jaw. His face was pugnacious but pleasant. No one could have mistaken him for anything but an American, though he spoke with very little accent.

"Get my note? Sit down and tell me right away all you know about my cousin."

"Your cousin?"

"Sure thing. Jane Finn. My father and her mother were brother and sister," explained Mr. Hersheimmer meticulously.

"Oh!" cried Tuppence. "Then you know where she is?"

"No!" Mr. Hersheimmer brought down his fist with a bang on the table. "I'm darned if I do! Don't you?"

"We advertised to receive information, not to give it," said Tuppence severely.

But Mr. Hersheimmer seemed to grow suddenly suspicious.

"See here," he declared. "This isn't Sicily! No demanding ransom or threatening to crop her ears if I refuse."

Tommy hastened to explain.

"We haven't kidnapped your cousin. On the contrary, we're trying to find her."

"Put me wise," said Mr. Hersheimmer succinctly.

Tommy gave him a guarded version of the disappearance of Jane Finn, and alluded to Tuppence and himself as "private inquiry agents."

"I guess that's all right. Just trot out your questions and I'll answer."

"When did you last see your cousin?"

"Never seen her," responded Mr. Hersheimmer.

"What?" demanded Tommy, astonished.

"No, sir. My father and her mother didn't always get on together. And when my aunt made up her mind to marry Amos Finn, my father was just mad! Said if he made his pile she'd never see a cent of it. Aunt Jane went out West and we never heard from her again.

"The old man _did_ pile it up. Then he died--last fall--and I got the dollars. Well, would you believe it, my conscience got busy! End of it was, I hired a man to hunt her down. Result, she was dead, and Amos Finn was dead, but they'd left a daughter--Jane--who'd been torpedoed in the _Lusitania_ on her way to Paris. She was saved all right. I phoned Scotland Yard and the Admiralty first thing. Scotland Yard were very civil--even sent a man round this morning to get her photograph. I'm off to Paris to-morrow."

"But say now," he ended, "you're not after her for anything? If there's such a thing as graft in this country, I'll buy her off."

Tuppence reassured him.

"That's good. Then we can work together. What about some lunch?"

Lunch was under way when a card was brought to Hersheimmer.

"Inspector Japp, C.I.D. Scotland Yard again. Another man this time. I hope they haven't lost that photograph. That Western photographer's place was burned down and all his negatives destroyed--this is the only copy in existence. I got it from the principal of the college there."

An unformulated dread swept over Tuppence.

"You--you don't know the name of the man who came this morning?"

"Yes, I do. No, I don't. Half a second. It was on his card. Oh, I know! Inspector Brown. Quiet, unassuming sort of chap."

## CHAPTER VI. A PLAN OF CAMPAIGN

A veil might with profit be drawn over the events of the next half-hour. Suffice it to say that no such person as “Inspector Brown” was known to Scotland Yard. The photograph of Jane Finn was lost beyond recovery. Once again “Mr. Brown” had triumphed.

The immediate result of this set-back was to effect a _rapprochement_ between Julius Hersheimmer and the Young Adventurers. All barriers went down with a crash. They abandoned the discreet reticence of “private inquiry agents,” and revealed to him the whole history of the joint venture, whereat the young man declared himself “tickled to death.”

The upshot was that Tommy and Tuppence took up their abode forthwith at the _Ritz_, in order, as Tuppence put it, to keep in touch with Jane Finn’s only living relation. “And put like that,” she added confidentially to Tommy, “nobody could boggle at the expense!”

Nobody did, which was the great thing.

“And now,” said the young lady on the morning after their installation, “to work!”

Mr. Beresford put down the _Daily Mail_ and applauded with somewhat unnecessary vigour.

“Dash it all, Tommy, we’ve got to _do_ something for our money.”

“My union, Tuppence, my union! It does not permit me to work before 11 a.m.” And he picked up the _Daily Mail_ again.

“It is absolutely essential that we should without delay map out a plan of campaign.”

Tommy laid his paper finally aside. “Fire ahead. I’m listening.”

“To begin with,” said Tuppence, “what have we to go upon?”

“Absolutely nothing,” said Tommy cheerily.

“Wrong!” Tuppence wagged an energetic finger. “We have two distinct clues. First clue, we know one of the gang. Whittington. I’d recognize him anywhere.”

“Hum,” said Tommy doubtfully, “you don’t know where to look for him, and it’s about a thousand to one against your running against him by accident.”

“I’m not so sure about that,” replied Tuppence thoughtfully. “I’ve often noticed that once coincidences start happening they go on happening in the most extraordinary way.”

“Whittington mayn’t be in London at all,” said the practical Tommy.

“That’s true. Anyway, I think clue No. 2 is more promising. It’s nothing much. Only a Christian name--Rita. Whittington mentioned it that day. That man, Danvers, was shadowed on the way over, wasn’t he? And it’s more likely to have been a woman than a man----”

“I don’t see that at all.”

“I am absolutely certain that it would be a woman, and a good-looking one, and obviously this woman, whoever she was, was saved.”

“How do you make that out?”

“If she wasn’t, how would they have known Jane Finn had got the papers?”

“Correct. Proceed, O Sherlock!”

“Now there’s just a chance, I admit it’s only a chance, that this woman may have been ‘Rita.’ If so, we’ve got to hunt through the survivors of the _Lusitania_ till we find her. I wrote a long list of things I wanted to know, and sent it to Mr. Carter. I got his reply this morning, and it encloses the official statement of those saved.”

“Is there a ‘Rita’ on the list?”

“That’s just what I don’t know,” confessed Tuppence. “Very few Christian names are given. They’re nearly all Mrs. or Miss.”

“That complicates matters,” Tommy murmured thoughtfully.

Tuppence gave her characteristic “terrier” shake. “We’ll start with the London area. Just note down the addresses of any of the females who live in London or roundabout, while I put on my hat.”

Soon a taxi was bearing them to The Laurels, Glendower Road, N.7, the residence of Mrs. Edgar Keith, whose name figured first in a list of seven reposing in Tommy’s pocket-book. At the door he arrested Tuppence’s hand.

“What are you going to say?”

“Why, I shall say--Oh dear, I don’t know.”

“I thought as much,” said Tommy with satisfaction. “How like a woman! No foresight! Now just stand aside, and see how easily the mere male deals with the situation.” He pressed the bell. Tuppence withdrew to a suitable spot.

A slatternly looking servant answered the door. Tommy had produced a notebook and pencil.

“From the Hampstead Borough Council. The new Voting Register. Mrs. Edgar Keith lives here, does she not? Christian name?”

“Missus’s? Eleanor Jane.”

“Thank you. Good morning.”

“I thought perhaps as you’d come about the gas,” she observed cryptically, and shut the door.

“You see, Tuppence,” Tommy observed. “Child’s play to the masculine mind.”

“I don’t mind admitting that for once you’ve scored handsomely.”

By lunch-time they had drawn four more blanks.

Tuppence picked up the notebook. “Mrs. Vandemeyer,” she read, “20 South Audley Mansions. Tommy, I’m getting discouraged.”

“Buck up, old bean. We always knew it was an outside chance. And, anyway, we’re only starting.”

“True,” said Tuppence, reviving. “But, oh, Tommy, I do like things to happen quickly.”

“You must stifle this longing for vulgar sensation, Tuppence. Remember that if Mr. Brown is all he is reported to be, it’s a wonder that he has not ere now done us to death. That’s a good sentence, quite a literary flavour about it.”

“You’re really more conceited than I am--with less excuse! But it certainly is queer.”

“Perhaps he doesn’t think us worth bothering about,” suggested the young man simply.

“How horrid you are, Tommy. Just as though we didn’t count.”

“Sorry, Tuppence. What I meant was that we work like moles in the dark, and that he has no suspicion of our nefarious schemes. Ha ha!”

“Ha ha!” echoed Tuppence approvingly, as she rose.

South Audley Mansions was an imposing-looking block of flats. Tommy rattled off the formula to the elderly woman who opened the door.

“Christian name?”

“Margaret.”

Tommy spelt it, but she interrupted him. “No, _g u e_.”

“Oh, Marguerite; French way, I see.” He paused, then plunged boldly. “We had her down as Rita Vandemeyer, but I suppose that’s incorrect?”

“She’s mostly called that, sir, but Marguerite’s her name.”

Hardly able to contain his excitement, Tommy hurried down the stairs. Tuppence was waiting at the angle of the turn.

“It’s--it’s so lovely to think of things--and then for them really to happen!” cried Tuppence enthusiastically.

They had reached the entrance hall. There were footsteps on the stairs above them, and voices. Suddenly Tuppence dragged him into the little space by the side of the lift where the shadow was deepest.

“What the----”

“Hush!”

Two men came down the stairs and passed out through the entrance. Tuppence’s hand closed tighter on Tommy’s arm.

“Quick--follow them. I daren’t. He might recognize me. I don’t know who the other man is, but the bigger of the two was Whittington.”

## CHAPTER VII. THE HOUSE IN SOHO

WHITTINGTON and his companion were walking at a good pace. Tommy started in pursuit at once.

The sport was a new one to him. He knew the technicalities only from a course of novel reading. Supposing, for instance, that they should suddenly hail a taxi? In books, you simply leapt into another, promised the driver a sovereign--or its modern equivalent--and there you were. In actual fact, Tommy foresaw that there would very likely be no second taxi.

At this juncture in his thoughts a taxi with flag erect turned the corner of the street ahead. Tommy held his breath. Would they hail it?

He drew a sigh of relief as they allowed it to pass unchallenged. Their zigzag course brought them to Oxford Street.

Just before the Bond Street Tube station they crossed the road and entered the big Lyons'. Tommy took the next table, directly behind Whittington, with a full view of the second man. He was fair, with a weak, unpleasant face, and Tommy put him down as being either a Russian or a Pole. He was probably about fifty years of age, and his eyes, small and crafty, shifted unceasingly.

Whittington ordered lunch and began to talk earnestly in a low voice. The gist seemed to be orders which the big man was impressing on his companion, and with which the latter from time to time disagreed. Whittington addressed the other as Boris.

Tommy caught the word "Ireland" several times, also "propaganda," but of Jane Finn there was no mention. Suddenly, in a lull in the clatter of the room, he got one phrase entire. Whittington was speaking. "Ah, but you don't know Flossie. She's a marvel. An archbishop would swear she was his own mother. She gets the voice right every time, and that's really the principal thing."

Tommy did not hear Boris's reply, but in response to it Whittington said something that sounded like: "Of course--only in an emergency...."

Presently two words, uttered by Boris, had a most stimulating effect upon the listener: "Mr. Brown."

Whittington seemed to remonstrate with him, but he merely laughed.

"Why not, my friend? It is a name most respectable--most common. Did he not choose it for that reason? Ah, I should like to meet him--Mr. Brown."

There was a steely ring in Whittington's voice as he replied:

"Who knows? You may have met him already."

"Bah!" retorted the other. "Do you know what I say to myself sometimes? That he is a fable invented by the Inner Ring, a bogy to frighten us with. It might be so."

"And it might not," said Whittington.

"I wonder," Boris went on, "... or is it indeed true that he is with us and amongst us, unknown to all but a chosen few? We look at each other-- _one of us is Mr. Brown_--which? He commands--but also he serves. And no one knows which he is...."

With an effort the Russian shook off the vagary of his fancy. He looked at his watch.

"Yes," said Whittington. "We might as well go."

Tommy paid his bill likewise and followed them down the stairs. Outside, Whittington hailed a taxi, and another was drawing up to the curb in obedience to Tommy's peremptory hand.

At Waterloo Tommy was behind Whittington at the booking-office. Whittington took a first-class single ticket to Bournemouth, and Tommy did the same. Boris remarked, glancing up at the clock: "You are early. You have nearly half an hour."

Clearly Whittington was making the journey alone, while the other remained in London. Obviously, Tommy could not follow both of them unless---- The Bournemouth train left at 3.30. He hurried into a telephone box. He dared not waste time trying to get hold of Tuppence; in all probability she was still in the neighbourhood of South Audley Mansions. He rang up the _Ritz_ and asked for Julius Hersheimmer. "Hello" came over the wire in unmistakable accents.

"That you, Hersheimmer? Beresford speaking. I'm at Waterloo. I've followed Whittington and another man here. Whittington's off to Bournemouth by the 3.30. Can you get there by then?"

"Sure. I'll hustle."

The first-class ticket had left Tommy with only a few shillings. It was to be hoped that Julius would arrive better provided.

The minutes were creeping by: 3.15.... 3.29.... Doors were banging. Tommy felt cold waves of despair pass over him. Then a hand fell on his shoulder.

"Here I am, son. Your British traffic beats description! Put me wise to the crooks right away."

"That's Whittington--there, getting in now, that big dark man. The other is the foreign chap he's talking to."

"I'm on to them. Which of the two is my bird?"

"Got any money with you?" Tommy asked.

Julius shook his head, and Tommy's face fell.

"I guess I haven't more than three or four hundred dollars with me at the moment," explained the American.

Tommy gave a faint whoop of relief.

"Oh, Lord, you millionaires! You don't talk the same language! Climb aboard the lugger. Here's your ticket. Whittington's your man."

"Me for Whittington!" said Julius darkly. The train was just starting as he swung himself aboard, and it slid out of the station.

Tommy drew a deep breath. Boris was coming along the platform towards him. Tommy allowed him to pass and then took up the chase once more.

From Waterloo Boris took the tube as far as Piccadilly Circus. Then he walked up Shaftesbury Avenue, finally turning off into the maze of mean streets round Soho.

They reached at length a small dilapidated square, an almost deserted cul-de-sac. Tommy watched Boris go up the steps of an evil-looking house and rap sharply, with a peculiar rhythm, on the door. It was opened promptly, he said a word or two to the doorkeeper, then passed inside. The door was shut to again.

It was at this juncture that Tommy lost his head. What he did was entirely foreign to the sober common sense which was, as a rule, his leading characteristic. Without a moment's pause for reflection he, too, went up the steps, and reproduced as far as he was able the peculiar knock.

The door swung open with the same promptness as before. A villainous-faced man with close-cropped hair stood in the doorway.

"Well?" he grunted.

It was at that moment that the full realization of his folly began to come home to Tommy. He seized at the first words that came into his mind.

"Mr. Brown?" he said.

To his surprise the man stood aside.

"Upstairs," he said, jerking his thumb over his shoulder, "second door on your left."

## CHAPTER VIII. THE ADVENTURES OF TOMMY

TAKEN aback though he was by the man’s words, Tommy did not hesitate. He quietly passed into the house and mounted the ramshackle staircase. Everything in the house was filthy beyond words.

At the top of the stairs Tommy halted. From the door nearest him on the left came a low murmur of voices. But what held his glance was a small recess immediately on his right, half concealed by a torn velvet curtain, directly opposite the left-hand door and commanding a good view of the upper part of the staircase. He thought things over in his usual slow and steady way, deciding that the mention of “Mr. Brown” was not a request for an individual, but in all probability a password used by the gang. Luck had served him very well so far, but there was such a thing as trusting it too far. To enter that room was a colossal risk.

A repetition of the signal knock sounded on the door below, and Tommy, his mind made up, slipped quickly into the recess and drew the curtain across. Several rents in the ancient material afforded him a good view.

The man who came up the staircase was obviously of the very dregs of society. He gave the signal knock at the door opposite and passed in, affording Tommy a momentary glimpse of a tall man with close-cropped hair and a short, pointed, naval-looking beard, who sat at the head of a long table. As the new-comer entered he glanced up, and with a correct, but curiously precise enunciation, he asked:

“Your number, comrade?”

“Fourteen, gov’nor,” replied the other hoarsely.

“Correct.”

The door shut again.

“If that isn’t a Hun, I’m a Dutchman!” said Tommy to himself. “Lucky I didn’t roll in. I’d have given the wrong number, and there would have been the deuce to pay.”

Tommy recognized the next visitor as an Irish Sinn Feiner. Certainly Mr. Brown’s organization was a far-reaching concern. The common criminal, the well-bred Irish gentleman, the pale Russian, and the efficient German master of the ceremonies! Who was this man who held in his finger these curiously variegated links of an unknown chain?

Two knocks followed in quick succession. The first man Tommy put down as a city clerk. The second was of the working classes, and his face was vaguely familiar to the young man.

Three minutes later came another, a man of commanding appearance, exquisitely dressed, and evidently well born. His face, again, was not unknown to the watcher, though he could not for the moment put a name to it.

The last-comer was a small man, very pale, with a gentle almost womanish air. As he passed the recess, he turned his head slowly. The strange light eyes seemed to burn through the curtain; Tommy could hardly believe that the man did not know he was there, and in spite of himself he shivered. The creature reminded him of a venomous snake.

The new-comer knocked as all had done, but his reception was very different. The bearded man rose to his feet, and all the others followed suit. The German came forward and shook hands.

“We are honoured,” he said. “We are greatly honoured. I much feared that it would be impossible.”

The other answered in a low voice that had a kind of hiss in it:

“There were difficulties. It will not be possible again, I fear. But one meeting is essential--to define my policy. I can do nothing without--Mr. Brown. He is here?”

The German replied with slight hesitation:

“We have received a message. It is impossible for him to be present in person.” He stopped, giving a curious impression of having left the sentence unfinished.

A very slow smile overspread the face of the other. He looked round at a circle of uneasy faces.

“Ah! I understand. I have read of his methods. He works in the dark and trusts no one. But, all the same, it is possible that he is among us now....” He looked round him again, and again that expression of fear swept over the group. Each man seemed eyeing his neighbour doubtfully.

The German indicated the place he had been occupying at the head of the table. The Russian demurred, but the other insisted.

“It is the only possible place,” he said, “for--Number One. Perhaps Number Fourteen will shut the door?”

The voices within sank once more to a mere undistinguishable murmur. Tommy felt that, by hook or by crook, he must hear more. He removed his shoes and crept out on his stockinged feet to the closed door.

Very slowly he turned the handle and pressed ever so slightly inward. The door did not budge. He pushed with all his might, and at last the truth dawned upon him. It was locked or bolted on the inside.

For a moment or two Tommy’s indignation got the better of him.

“Well, I’m damned!” he said. “What a dirty trick!”

There was a certain bulldog tenacity about Tommy that made him slow to admit defeat. A little farther along the passage on the left was a second door. He tried the handle. It yielded, and he slipped inside.

The room, which was untenanted, was furnished as a bedroom. But what interested Tommy was the thing he had hoped to find, a communicating door between the two rooms. By gently wriggling the rusty bolt and repeating his manœuvres with the handle, Tommy managed to open the door a crack, enough to hear. A velvet _portière_ on the inside prevented him from seeing, but he was able to recognize the voices with a reasonable amount of accuracy.

The Sinn Feiner was speaking:

“That’s all very well. But more money is essential. No money--no results!”

Another voice which Tommy rather thought was that of Boris replied:

“Will you guarantee that there _are_ results?”

“In a month from now--sooner or later as you wish--I will guarantee you such a reign of terror in Ireland as shall shake the British Empire to its foundations.”

Then came the soft, sibilant accents of Number One:

“Good! You shall have the money. Boris, you will see to that.”

“I guess that’ll be all right!” said a new voice, with a transatlantic intonation, “though things are getting a mite difficult.”

Tommy felt that Boris had shrugged his shoulders as he answered:

“Does that matter, since the money only nominally comes from the States?”

“The chief difficulty is the landing of the ammunition,” said the Sinn Feiner. “The money is conveyed in easily enough--thanks to our colleague here.”

Another voice, which Tommy fancied was that of the tall, commanding-looking man whose face had seemed familiar to him, said:

“Think of the feelings of Belfast if they could hear you!”

“That is settled, then,” said the sibilant tones. “Now, in the matter of the loan to an English newspaper, you have arranged the details satisfactorily, Boris?”

“I think so.”

Then the German broke the silence:

“I am directed by--Mr. Brown, to place the summaries of the reports from the different unions before you. That of the miners is most satisfactory. We must hold back the railways.”

“And--the date, my friend?” said Number One.

“The 29th.”

The Russian seemed to consider:

“That is rather soon.”

“I know. But it was settled by the principal Labour leaders, and we cannot seem to interfere too much. They must believe it to be entirely their own show.”

The Russian laughed softly.

“Yes, yes,” he said. “They are honest men--and that is their value to us.” He paused, and then repeated, as though the phrase pleased him: “Every revolution has had its honest men. They are soon disposed of afterwards.”

The German resumed:

“Clymes must go. He is too far-seeing. Number Fourteen will see to that.”

There was a hoarse murmur.

“That’s all right, gov’nor. Suppose I’m nabbed.”

“You will have the best legal talent to defend you,” replied the German quietly. “But in any case you will wear gloves fitted with the finger-prints of a notorious housebreaker.”

“Oh, I ain’t afraid, gov’nor. The streets is going to run with blood, so they say.” He spoke with a grim relish.

Then Number One spoke:

“Then all is arranged. We are assured of success?”

“I--think so.” But the German spoke with less than his usual confidence.

Number One’s voice held suddenly a dangerous quality:

“What has gone wrong?”

“The Labour leaders,” said the German. “If they do not declare a general strike on the 29th---- As you’ve said, they’re honest. I’m not sure that they haven’t got a sneaking faith and belief in the Government. They will not go against it.”

The Russian’s fingers drummed on the table.

“To the point, my friend. I was given to understand that there was a certain document in existence which assured success.”

“That is so. If that document were placed before the leaders, they would declare for the revolution without a moment’s hesitation.”

“Then what more do you want?”

“The document itself,” said the German bluntly.

“Ah! It is not in your possession? But you know where it is?”

“No.”

“Does anyone know where it is?”

“One person--perhaps. And we are not sure of that even.”

“Who is this person?”

“A girl.”

Tommy held his breath.

“A girl?” The Russian’s voice rose contemptuously. “And you have not made her speak? In Russia we have ways of making a girl talk.”

“This case is different,” said the German sullenly.

“How--different? Where is the girl now?”

“She is----”

But Tommy heard no more. A crashing blow descended on his head, and all was darkness.

## CHAPTER IX. TUPPENCE ENTERS DOMESTIC SERVICE

When Tommy set forth on the trail of the two men, it took all Tuppence's self-command to refrain from accompanying him. She was consoled by the reflection that her reasoning had been justified: that one slender thread of the name "Rita" had set the Young Adventurers once more upon the track of the abductors of Jane Finn.

The question was what to do next? Tuppence hated letting the grass grow under her feet. She retraced her steps to the entrance hall, now tenanted by a small lift-boy who was polishing brass fittings. She reflected that an ally in the enemy's camp, so to speak, was not to be despised.

"Well, William," she remarked cheerfully, in the best approved hospital-early-morning style, "getting a good shine up?"

The boy grinned responsively. "Albert, miss," he corrected.

"Albert be it," said Tuppence. She leaned towards the boy and dropped her voice. "Look! Do you know what this is?" She flung back the left side of her coat and exposed a small enamelled badge. In reality it was the device of a local training corps originated by the archdeacon in the early days of the war. But Tuppence had noted the corner of a threepenny detective novel protruding from Albert's pocket.

"American Detective Force!" she hissed.

Albert fell for it. "Lord!" he murmured ecstatically.

"Know who I'm after?" she inquired genially.

"One of the flats?" Albert demanded breathlessly.

Tuppence nodded and jerked a thumb up the stairs. "No. 20. Calls herself Vandemeyer. Vandemeyer! Ha! ha!"

"A crook?" Albert queried eagerly.

"A crook? I should say so. Ready Rita they call her in the States."

"Ready Rita," repeated Albert deliriously. "Oh, ain't it just like the pictures!"

It was. Tuppence was a great frequenter of the cinema.

"Annie always said as how she was a bad lot," continued the boy. Annie, the house-parlourmaid, was leaving to-day.

Tuppence explained that they were after her for the emeralds of Rysdale, the oil king. "Worth a million dollars!"

"Mind you, sonny, not a word of this," said Tuppence.

"I'll not breathe a word," protested Albert eagerly. "Ain't there anything I could do?"

"See here, son, my brain's got busy. How would it be if you mentioned that you'd got a young cousin, or a friend of yours had, that might suit the place. You get me?"

"I'm there," said Albert instantly. "You leave it to me, miss, and I'll fix the whole thing up in two ticks."

"Some lad!" commented Tuppence. "You let me know, and if it's O.K. I'll be round to-morrow at eleven o'clock."

"Where am I to let you know to?"

"_Ritz_," replied Tuppence laconically. "Name of Cowley."

Albert eyed her enviously. "It must be a good job, this tec business."

"It sure is," drawled Tuppence, "especially when old man Rysdale backs the bill. But don't fret, son. If this goes well, you shall come in on the ground floor."

With which promise she took leave of her new ally, well pleased with her morning's work.

She went straight back to the _Ritz_ and wrote a few brief words to Mr. Carter. She started off on a shopping expedition that finished at a well-known hairdresser's. That evening, with an actress's pencil, she slightly altered the line of her eyebrows. Taken with the new luxuriant growth of fair hair above, this so changed her appearance that she felt confident that even Whittington would not recognize her. The cap and apron would be an even more valuable disguise. From hospital experience she knew that a nurse out of uniform is frequently unrecognized by her patients.

"Yes," said Tuppence aloud, nodding at the pert reflection in the glass, "you'll do."

Dinner was a solitary meal. Tommy had not returned, and Julius was absent, but his abrupt "hustling" appearances and disappearances were accepted as part of the day's work.

"All very well," thought Tuppence, "but this is very dull for _me!_ Here I am bursting with news, and absolutely no one to tell it to! Tommy might have wired, or something. I wonder where he is."

The morning brought a note from Mr. Carter:

"DEAR MISS TUPPENCE,

"You have made a splendid start, and I congratulate you. Those people are absolutely desperate and incapable of either mercy or pity. I warn you again that I can promise you no protection.

"If, in spite of my warnings, you make up your mind to go through with it, you will find everything arranged. You have lived for two years with Miss Dufferin, The Parsonage, Llanelly, and Mrs. Vandemeyer can apply to her for a reference.

"May I be permitted a word or two of advice? Stick as near to the truth as possible--it minimizes the danger of 'slips.' I suggest that you should represent yourself to be what you are, a former V.A.D., who has chosen domestic service as a profession. That explains away any incongruities of voice or manner.

"Whichever way you decide, good luck to you.

"Your sincere friend,

"MR. CARTER."

Tuppence's spirits rose mercurially. Mr. Carter's warnings passed unheeded, but she had too much common sense not to recognize the force of his arguments.

The morning post brought a somewhat dirty postcard with the words "It's O.K." scrawled upon it.

At ten-thirty Tuppence drove to Paddington with a slightly battered tin trunk and left it in the cloak room. Ten minutes later a metamorphosed Tuppence walked demurely out of the station and entered a bus.

It was a few minutes past eleven when she again entered the hall of South Audley Mansions. Albert's admiration was unbounded.

"Blest if I'd have known you! That rig-out's top-hole."

"Glad you like it, Albert," replied Tuppence modestly. "By the way, am I your cousin, or am I not?"

"Your voice too," cried the delighted boy. "It's as English as anything! No, I said as a friend of mine knew a young gal. Annie wasn't best pleased. She's stopped on till to-day--to oblige, _she_ said, but really it's so as to put you against the place."

As Tuppence rang the bell of No. 20, a smart young woman opened the door.

"I've come about the place," said Tuppence.

"It's a rotten place," said the young woman without hesitation. "Regular old cat--always interfering. There's never anything in the waste-paper basket--she burns everything. Cook knows something about her--but she won't tell--scared to death of her. And suspicious! She's on to you in a minute if you as much as speak to a fellow. I can tell you----"

But what more Annie could tell, Tuppence was never destined to learn. At that moment a clear voice with a peculiarly steely ring to it called:

"Annie!"

The smart young woman jumped as if she had been shot. "Yes, ma'am."

"Who are you talking to?"

"It's a young woman about the situation, ma'am."

"Show her in then. At once."

A woman was standing by the fireplace. She was no longer in her first youth, and the beauty she undeniably possessed was hardened and coarsened. Her eyes, of a piercing electric blue, seemed to possess a faculty of boring into the very soul of the person she was looking at. And yet, despite her swaying grace, you felt instinctively the presence of something hard and menacing, a kind of metallic strength that found expression in the tones of her voice and in that gimlet-like quality of her eyes.

For the first time Tuppence felt afraid. She had not feared Whittington, but this woman was different. Mr. Carter's warning recurred to her mind. Here, indeed, she might expect no mercy.

Fighting down the panic that urged her to turn tail and run, Tuppence returned the lady's gaze firmly and respectfully. Mrs. Vandemeyer motioned to a chair.

"You can sit down. How did you hear I wanted a house-parlourmaid?"

"Through a friend who knows the lift boy here. He thought the place might suit me."

Again that basilisk glance seemed to pierce her through. "You speak like an educated girl?"

Glibly enough, Tuppence ran through her imaginary career on the lines suggested by Mr. Carter.

"I see," Mrs. Vandemeyer remarked at length. "Is there anyone I can write to for a reference?"

"I lived last with a Miss Dufferin, The Parsonage, Llanelly. I was with her two years."

"And then you thought you would get more money by coming to London, I suppose? Well, it doesn't matter to me. I will give you £50--£60--whatever you want. You can come in at once?"

"Yes, ma'am. To-day, if you like. My box is at Paddington."

"Go and fetch it in a taxi, then. By the way, what's your name?"

"Prudence Cooper, ma'am."

"Very well, Prudence. Go away and fetch your box."

"Thank you, ma'am."

Tuppence withdrew and passed meekly out.

The adventure had begun, but she felt less elated than she had done earlier in the morning. It crossed her mind that if the unknown Jane Finn had fallen into the hands of Mrs. Vandemeyer, it was likely to have gone hard with her.

## CHAPTER X. ENTER SIR JAMES PEEL EDGERTON

TUPPENCE betrayed no awkwardness in her new duties; the daughters of the archdeacon were well grounded in household tasks. Mrs. Vandemeyer’s cook puzzled her. She evidently went in deadly terror of her mistress. Mrs. Vandemeyer was expecting a guest to dinner.

At a few minutes past eight the front door bell rang. Tuppence saw that the visitor was the second of the two men whom Tommy had taken upon himself to follow.

He gave his name as Count Stepanov. Mrs. Vandemeyer rose from her divan with a quick murmur of pleasure.

“It is delightful to see you, Boris Ivanovitch,” she said.

Tuppence returned to the kitchen.

“Count Stepanov, or some such,” she remarked, and affecting a frank and unvarnished curiosity: “Who’s he?”

“I’m not quite easy in my mind about the _soufflé_,” explained the other.

“You know something,” thought Tuppence to herself, but aloud she only said: “Going to dish up now? Right-o.”

Already, although she would hardly admit it, she was becoming uneasy about her partner. She had arranged to have all letters or messages sent on to a small stationer’s shop where Albert was to call in frequently.

But the dinner conversation presented no clue. Tuppence brought in the coffee and liqueurs to the boudoir and unwillingly retired, lingering by the door which she had carefully neglected to close. She heard Boris say:

“Quite safe, I suppose?”

“Really, Boris, you are absurdly suspicious. I believe she’s the cousin of the hall porter, or something of the kind. And nobody even dreams that I have any connection with our--mutual friend, Mr. Brown.”

“For heaven’s sake, be careful, Rita. That door isn’t shut.”

“Well, shut it then,” laughed the woman.

Tuppence removed herself speedily.

She slipped back to the boudoir door. Alas! The conversation inside was being carried on in too low a tone to permit of her hearing anything, and she dared not reopen it. Then her face brightened. She went quickly to Mrs. Vandemeyer’s bedroom, which had long French windows leading on to a balcony, and crept noiselessly along till she reached the boudoir window. It stood a little ajar, and the voices within were plainly audible.

Mrs. Vandemeyer and the Russian seemed to be at variance, and finally the latter exclaimed bitterly:

“You are going about everywhere with Peel Edgerton. Not only is he, perhaps, the most celebrated K.C. in England, but his special hobby is criminology! It is madness!”

“I know that his eloquence has saved untold men from the gallows,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer calmly. “I may need his assistance in that line myself some day. If so, how fortunate to have such a friend at court--or perhaps it would be more to the point to say _in_ court.”

“Be guided by me, and give up Peel Edgerton.”

Mrs. Vandemeyer shook her head gently. “I think not.”

“Then, by Heaven,” snarled the Russian, “we will see----”

“You forget, Boris,” she said. “I am accountable to no one. I take my orders only from--Mr. Brown.”

The other threw up his hands in despair. “They say Peel Edgerton can _smell_ a criminal! He guesses----”

Mrs. Vandemeyer eyed him scornfully. “He suspects nothing. You seem to forget that I am commonly accounted a beautiful woman. I assure you that is all that interests Peel Edgerton.”

“He has studied crime as no other man in this kingdom has studied it. Do you fancy that you can deceive him?”

Mrs. Vandemeyer’s eyes narrowed.

“If he is all that you say--it would amuse me to try! Besides, he is extremely rich. I am not one who despises money.”

“Money--money! That is always the danger with you, Rita.” He paused, then in a low, sinister voice he said slowly: “Sometimes I believe that you would sell-- _us!_”

Mrs. Vandemeyer smiled and shrugged her shoulders.

“The price, at any rate, would have to be enormous,” she said lightly. “It would be beyond the power of anyone but a millionaire to pay.”

“Ah!” snarled the Russian. “You see, I was right!”

“My dear Boris, can you not take a joke?”

“Was it a joke?”

“Of course,” she said. “Let us not quarrel, Boris. Touch the bell. We will have some drinks.”

Tuppence beat a hasty retreat, and answered the bell demurely.

The conversation that she had overheard, although interesting in that it proved beyond doubt the complicity of both Rita and Boris, threw very little light on the present preoccupations. The name of Jane Finn had not even been mentioned.

The following morning a few brief words with Albert informed her that nothing was waiting for her at the stationer’s. A cold hand seemed to close round her heart.... Supposing.... She choked her fears down bravely.

“What day do you usually go out, Prudence?”

“Friday’s my usual day, ma’am.”

“And to-day is Friday! But I suppose you hardly wish to go out to-day, as you only came yesterday.”

“I was thinking of asking you if I might, ma’am.”

Mrs. Vandemeyer looked at her a minute longer, and then smiled.

“I wish Count Stepanov could hear you. He made a suggestion about you last night.” Her smile broadened, catlike. “Your request is very--typical. I am satisfied. You do not understand all this--but you can go out to-day.”

Once again Tuppence admitted to herself that she was afraid, horribly afraid, of the beautiful woman with the cruel eyes.

Tuppence was disturbed by the front door bell. This time the visitor was a man of striking appearance. Magnetism seemed to radiate from him.

He gave her his name: Sir James Peel Edgerton.

This, then, was the famous K.C. whose name was familiar all over England. She had heard it said that he might one day be Prime Minister.

Tuppence went back to her pantry thoughtfully. She understood Boris’s agitation. Peel Edgerton would not be an easy man to deceive.

When she showed him out, she was conscious of his eyes raking her through. In the doorway he stopped.

“Not been doing this long, eh?”

Tuppence raised her eyes, astonished. She read in his glance kindliness, and something else more difficult to fathom.

“V.A.D. and hard up, I suppose?”

“Did Mrs. Vandemeyer tell you that?” asked Tuppence suspiciously.

“No, child. The look of you told me. Good place here?”

“Very good, thank you, sir.”

“Ah, but there are plenty of good places nowadays. And a change does no harm sometimes.”

“Do you mean----?” began Tuppence.

But Sir James was already on the topmost stair. He looked back with his kindly, shrewd glance.

“Just a hint,” he said. “That’s all.”

Tuppence went back to the pantry more thoughtful than ever.

## CHAPTER XI. JULIUS TELLS A STORY

DRESSED appropriately, Tuppence duly sallied forth for her “afternoon out.” Albert was in temporary abeyance, but Tuppence went herself to the stationer’s to make quite sure that nothing had come for her. She made her way to the _Ritz_. On inquiry she learnt that Tommy had not yet returned. It was the answer she had expected, but it was another nail in the coffin of her hopes. She resolved to appeal to Mr. Carter. The prospect of his aid revived her mercurial spirits, and she next inquired for Julius Hersheimmer. He had returned about half an hour ago, but had gone out immediately.

She wrote her note to Mr. Carter in Julius’s sitting-room, and was just addressing the envelope when the door burst open.

“What the hell----” began Julius, but checked himself abruptly. “I beg your pardon, Miss Tuppence. Those fools down at the office would have it that Beresford wasn’t here any longer--hadn’t been here since Wednesday. Is that so?”

Tuppence nodded.

“You don’t know where he is?” she asked faintly.

“I? How should I know? I haven’t had one darned word from him, though I wired him yesterday morning.”

“I expect your wire’s at the office unopened,” said Tuppence.

“We parted at the depot on Wednesday. Waterloo. Didn’t he tell you?”

“I haven’t seen him either,” replied Tuppence impatiently. “Go on about Waterloo. What were you doing there?”

“He gave me a call. Told me to get a move on, and hustle. Said he was trailing two crooks. Beresford pointed out the crooks. The big one was mine, the guy you bluffed. Tommy shoved a ticket into my hand and told me to get aboard the cars. He was going to sleuth the other crook.”

“Julius,” said Tuppence firmly, “stop walking up and down. It makes me giddy. Sit down in that armchair, and tell me the whole story with as few fancy turns of speech as possible.”

“Well,” began Julius, “I got into one of your dear old-fashioned first-class British compartments. I did a bit of prospecting along the corridor to the next coach. Whittington was there right enough.

“We got to Bournemouth all right. Whittington took a cab and gave the name of an hotel. I did likewise. About nine o’clock he went out. Took a car across the town, and then paid it off and struck out along those pine-woods on the top of the cliff. There’s a lot of villas all the way along, and in the end we got to one that seemed the last of the bunch.

“I turned a curve and I was just in time to see him ring the bell and get admitted to the house. It was beginning to rain, and I was soon pretty near soaked through.

“Whittington didn’t come out again, and by and by I got kind of restive. All the ground floor windows were shuttered tight, but upstairs I noticed a window with a light burning and the curtains not drawn. Just opposite that window there was a tree growing. I guess I’d got the hump from standing so long in the rain. So I started up.

“Bit by bit I managed it, until at last I was level with the window. But I was too far to the left. A bit of curtain, and a yard of wallpaper was all I could command. Just as I was going to give it up, some one inside moved and threw his shadow on my little bit of wall--and, by gum, it was Whittington!

“After that, my blood was up. There was a long branch running out in the right direction, but it was mighty uncertain whether it would bear my weight. Very cautiously, I crawled along, and at last I got to where I wanted to be.

“Sitting at a table, facing towards me, was Whittington right enough. He was talking to a woman dressed as a hospital nurse. She was sitting with her back to me, so I couldn’t see her face. The window was shut, so I couldn’t catch a word of what they said. He seemed very emphatic--once or twice he beat with his fist on the table.

“Presently he got up, and so did she. She came right across and looked out. Just then the moon came out from behind the clouds. I was scared the woman would catch sight of me, for I was full in the moonlight. I tried to move back a bit. The jerk I gave was too much for that rotten old branch. With an almighty crash, down it came, and Julius P. Hersheimmer with it!”

“Oh, Julius,” breathed Tuppence, “how exciting! Go on.”

“Well, luckily for me, I pitched down into a good soft bed of earth--but it put me out of action for the time. The next thing I knew, I was lying in bed with a hospital nurse (not Whittington’s one) on one side of me, and a little black-bearded man with gold glasses, and medical man written all over him, on the other. I tried to sit up, but my right foot gave me a nasty twinge. ‘A slight sprain,’ explained the doctor. ‘Nothing serious.’”

“I noticed you walked lame,” interpolated Tuppence.

Julius nodded, and continued:

“‘How did it happen?’ I asked. He replied dryly. ‘You fell, with a considerable portion of one of my trees, into one of my newly planted flower-beds.’

“I liked the man. He seemed to have a sense of humour. I felt sure that he, at least, was plumb straight. ‘You are an American, are you not?’ he asked. I told him my name. ‘And you?’ ‘I am Dr. Hall, and this, as you doubtless know, is my private nursing home.’

“I didn’t know, but I wasn’t going to put him wise. So I mumbled out something about a girl. I trotted out the stern guardian business, and a nervous breakdown, and explained that I had fancied I recognized her among the patients at the home. ‘Now, doc,’ I went on, ‘will you be frank with me? Have you here now, or have you had here at any time, a young girl called Jane Finn?’ He repeated the name thoughtfully. ‘Jane Finn?’ he said. ‘No.’

“I was chagrined, and I guess I showed it. ‘You are sure?’ ‘Quite sure, Mr. Hersheimmer. It is an uncommon name, and I should not have been likely to forget it.’

“Well, that was flat. ‘Now, there’s another matter,’ I said. ‘When I was hugging that darned branch I thought I recognized an old friend of mine talking to one of your nurses.’ I purposely didn’t mention any name, but the doctor answered at once. ‘Mr. Whittington, perhaps?’ ‘That’s the fellow. Don’t tell me _his_ nerves are out of order?’

“Dr. Hall laughed. ‘No. He came down to see one of my nurses, Nurse Edith, who is a niece of his.’ ‘Is he still here?’ ‘No, he went back to town almost immediately.’ ‘But perhaps I could speak to his niece?’

“But the doctor shook his head. ‘I’m afraid that, too, is impossible. Nurse Edith left with a patient to-night also.’ ‘Have you Mr. Whittington’s address in town?’ ‘I don’t know his address. I can write to Nurse Edith for it if you like.’ I thanked him. ‘Don’t say who it is wants it. I’d like to give him a little surprise.’

“Next thing I wrote out a wire to Beresford saying where I was, and telling him to come down if he wasn’t busy. However, I didn’t hear from him, so to-day I said good-bye to the little doctor chap, asked him to send me word if he heard from Nurse Edith, and came right away back to town. Say, Miss Tuppence, you’re looking mighty pale!”

“It’s Tommy,” said Tuppence. “What can have happened to him?”

“Buck up, I guess he’s all right really. See here, it was a foreign-looking guy he went off after.”

“I’ve seen that man, Boris Something, since. He dined with Mrs. Vandemeyer last night.”

“Mrs. Who?” asked Julius.

“I forgot. Of course you don’t know all that.”

“I’m listening,” said Julius. “Put me wise.”

Tuppence thereupon related the events of the last two days. Julius’s astonishment and admiration were unbounded.

“Bully for you! Fancy you a menial. It just tickles me to death!” Then he added seriously: “But say now, I don’t like it, Miss Tuppence. These crooks we’re up against would as soon croak a girl as a man any day.”

“Do you think I’m afraid?” said Tuppence indignantly, valiantly repressing memories of the steely glitter in Mrs. Vandemeyer’s eyes. “Let’s think about what can have happened to Tommy. I’ve written to Mr. Carter about it.”

“I guess that’s good as far as it goes. But I guess we’d better get on the track of Boris. Is he likely to come again?”

“He might. I really don’t know,” said Tuppence.

“Well, I guess I’d better buy a car, a slap-up one, dress as a chauffeur and hang about outside. Then if Boris comes, you could make some kind of signal, and I’d trail him. What make do you like?”

“Oh,” said Tuppence faintly, “I _like_ Rolls-Royces, but----”

“Sure,” agreed Julius. “I’ll be round in the car in half an hour.”

“You’re awfully good, Julius. But I’m really pinning my faith to Mr. Carter.”

“Then I shouldn’t,” said Julius.

“Why?”

“Just an idea of mine.”

“Oh; but he must do something. There’s no one else,” said Tuppence. And she narrated her encounter that morning with Sir James Peel Edgerton.

“What did the guy mean, do you think?” Julius asked.

“I don’t quite know,” said Tuppence meditatively. “But I think that, in an ambiguous, legal, without prejudishish lawyer’s way, he was trying to warn me. He looked kind, and simply awfully clever. I wouldn’t mind going to him and telling him everything.”

Somewhat to her surprise, Julius negatived the idea sharply.

“See here,” he said, “we don’t want any lawyers mixed up in this. That guy couldn’t help us any.”

“Well, I believe he could,” reiterated Tuppence obstinately.

“Don’t you think it. So long. I’ll be back in half an hour.”

Thirty-five minutes had elapsed when Julius returned. He took Tuppence by the arm, and walked her to the window.

“There she is.”

“Oh!” said Tuppence with a note of reverence in her voice, as she gazed down at the enormous car. “How did you get it?”

“She was just being sent home to some bigwig,” said Julius. “I went round to his house. I said that I reckoned a car like that was worth every penny of twenty thousand dollars. Then I told him that it was worth just about fifty thousand dollars to me if he’d get out.”

“Well?” said Tuppence, intoxicated.

“Well,” returned Julius, “he got out, that’s all.”

## CHAPTER XII. A FRIEND IN NEED

Friday and Saturday passed uneventfully. Tuppence had received a brief answer to her appeal from Mr. Carter. In it he pointed out that the Young Adventurers had undertaken the work at their own risk. If anything had happened to Tommy he regretted it deeply, but he could do nothing.

This was cold comfort. Somehow, without Tommy, all the savour went out of the adventure, and, for the first time, Tuppence felt doubtful of success. His common sense and soundness of vision were so unvarying that without him Tuppence felt much like a rudderless ship. He might be slow, but he was very sure.

Tommy--that was all that mattered. “Little fool,” Tuppence would apostrophize herself, “don’t snivel. Of course you’re fond of him. But there’s no need to be sentimental about it.”

In the meantime, nothing more was seen of Boris. Tuppence had not entirely relinquished the idea of appealing to Sir James Peel Edgerton. Had he meant to warn her that day? If so, why?

Anyway, Tuppence decided, it was worth trying. Sunday was her afternoon out. She would meet Julius, persuade him to her point of view, and they would beard the lion in his den.

When the day arrived Julius needed a considerable amount of persuading, but Tuppence held firm. “It can do no harm,” was what she always came back to. In the end Julius gave in, and they proceeded in the car to Carlton House Terrace.

An irreproachable butler took in Tuppence’s request that Sir James would see her about an important message, and presently ushered them into a library at the back of the house. Tuppence noticed that all one wall was devoted to works on crime and criminology.

“You have a message for me? Ah”--Sir James recognized Tuppence with a smile--“it’s you, is it?”

“Not exactly,” said Tuppence. “In fact, I’m afraid I only said that to be quite sure of getting in. This is Mr. Hersheimmer, Sir James Peel Edgerton.”

When they were seated, Tuppence plunged boldly. “Sir James, of course Tommy and I are very unimportant. What I really want to know is what you meant by what you said to me the other day? Did you mean to warn me against Mrs. Vandemeyer?”

“My dear young lady, as far as I recollect I only mentioned that there were equally good situations to be obtained elsewhere.”

“Yes, I know. But it was a hint, wasn’t it?”

“Well, perhaps it was,” admitted Sir James gravely.

“Well, I want to know just _why_ you gave me a hint.”

Sir James smiled at her earnestness. “Suppose the lady brings a libel action against me for defamation of character?”

“Of course,” said Tuppence. “I know lawyers are always dreadfully careful. But can’t we say ‘without prejudice’ first, and then say just what we want to.”

“Well,” said Sir James, still smiling, “without prejudice, then, if I had a young sister forced to earn her living, I should not like to see her in Mrs. Vandemeyer’s service. It is no place for a young and inexperienced girl. That is all I can tell you.”

“Thank you very much. But I’m not _really_ inexperienced, you know. I knew perfectly that she was a bad lot when I went there--as a matter of fact that’s _why_ I went----” She broke off. “I think perhaps I’d better tell you the whole story, Sir James. What do you think, Julius?”

“As you’re bent on it, I’d go right ahead with the facts,” replied the American.

“Yes, tell me all about it,” said Sir James. “I want to know who Tommy is.”

Thus encouraged Tuppence plunged into her tale, and the lawyer listened with close attention.

“Very interesting,” he said, when she finished. “A great deal of what you tell me, child, is already known to me. I’ve had certain theories of my own about this Jane Finn. You’ve done extraordinarily well so far, but it’s rather too bad of--what do you know him as?--Mr. Carter to pitchfork you two young things into an affair of this kind.”

“Oh, Sir James,” broke out Tuppence, “what do you think has become of Tommy?”

“H’m.” The lawyer rose, and paced slowly up and down. “When you arrived, young lady, I was just packing up my traps. Going to Scotland by the night train for a few days’ fishing. But there are different kinds of fishing. I’ve a good mind to stay, and see if we can’t get on the track of that young chap.”

“Now, Miss Tuppence, about this young Tommy of yours. Frankly, things look bad for him. But don’t give up hope.”

“And you really will help us? There, Julius! He didn’t want me to come,” she added by way of explanation.

“H’m,” said the lawyer, favouring Julius with a keen glance. “And why was that?”

“I reckoned it would be no good worrying you with a petty little business like this.”

“This petty little business, as you call it, bears directly on a very big business, bigger perhaps than either you or Miss Tuppence know. We must find him. And there’s one person quite near at hand who in all probability knows where he is--Mrs. Vandemeyer. I think it quite likely that I shall be able to make Mrs. Vandemeyer tell me what I want to know.”

“And if she won’t tell?” asked Julius suddenly.

“I think she will. I have one or two powerful levers. Still, in that unlikely event, there is always the possibility of bribery.”

“Sure. And that’s where I come in!” cried Julius, bringing his fist down on the table with a bang. “You can count on me, if necessary, for one million dollars. Yes, sir, one million dollars!”

Sir James subjected Julius to a long scrutiny. “At the present rate of exchange it amounts to considerably over two hundred and fifty thousand pounds.”

“That’s so. Maybe you think I’m talking through my hat, but I can deliver the goods all right, with enough over to spare for your fee.”

Sir James flushed slightly. “There is no question of a fee, Mr. Hersheimmer. I am not a private detective.”

“Sorry. Now, let’s get down to it. What’s your idea?”

“There is no time to be lost.” Sir James turned to Tuppence. “Is Mrs. Vandemeyer dining out to-night?”

“Yes, I think so, but she will not be out late.”

“Good. Be back by nine-thirty--it might arouse suspicion if you returned earlier. I will arrive at ten. Mr. Hersheimmer will wait below.”

“Now, Miss Tuppence, my advice to you is to go and have a good dinner, a _really_ good one, mind. And don’t think ahead more than you can help.”

“Isn’t he a duck?” inquired Tuppence ecstatically, as she skipped down the steps. “Oh, Julius, isn’t he just a duck?”

“Well, I allow he seems to be the goods all right. And I was wrong about its being useless to go to him.”

Tuppence had herself dropped in the park, but it was not so easy to follow Sir James’s advice. As she drew nearer to Hyde Park corner, the temptation to return to South Audley Mansions was almost irresistible. At any rate, she decided, it would do no harm just to go and _look_ at the building.

She was just turning away when she heard a piercing whistle, and the faithful Albert came running from the building, purple with suppressed excitement.

“I say, miss, she’s a-going!”

“Who’s going?” demanded Tuppence sharply.

“The crook. Ready Rita. Mrs. Vandemeyer. She’s a-packing up, and she’s just sent down word for me to get her a taxi.”

“What?” Tuppence clutched his arm. “Albert, you’re a brick. I’ve got to stop her. At all costs I must keep her here until----” She broke off. “Is there a telephone?”

“There’s a box just round the corner, miss.”

“Go to it then, at once, and ring up the _Ritz Hotel_. Ask for Mr. Hersheimmer, and when you get him tell him to get Sir James and come on at once, as Mrs. Vandemeyer is trying to hook it. If you can’t get him, ring up Sir James Peel Edgerton, and tell him what’s happening.”

Albert repeated the names glibly. “You trust to me, miss.”

“_Go and telephone_. Be quick.”

Drawing a long breath, Tuppence ran up to the door of No. 20. How she was to detain Mrs. Vandemeyer until the two men arrived, she did not know.

She rang twice before Mrs. Vandemeyer herself opened the door, and lifted her eyebrows at the sight of the girl.

“You?”

“I had a touch of toothache, ma’am,” said Tuppence glibly. “So thought it better to come home and have a quiet evening.”

“How unfortunate for you,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer coldly. “You had better go to bed.”

“Oh, I shall be all right in the kitchen, ma’am. Cook will----”

“Cook is out. I sent her out. So you see you had better go to bed.”

Suddenly Tuppence felt afraid. The other woman was slowly edging her up the passage. Tuppence turned at bay.

“I don’t want----”

Then, in a flash, a rim of cold steel touched her temple, and Mrs. Vandemeyer’s voice rose cold and menacing:

“You damned little fool! Do you think I don’t know? No, don’t answer. If you struggle or cry out, I’ll shoot you like a dog.”

“Now then, march. This way--into my room. And you’ll sleep--oh yes, my little spy, you’ll sleep all right!”

Tuppence walked obediently into the bedroom. The room was in a state of wild disorder; a suit-case and a hat box, half-packed, stood in the middle of the floor.

“You’re a clever girl,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer. “You deceived _me_ all right. So I’ve no doubt that you understand perfectly well that this is where I’m on top and you’re underneath. Now then--sit on the bed. Put your hands above your head, and if you value your life don’t move them.”

Tuppence obeyed passively. Every minute of delay gained was valuable.

Mrs. Vandemeyer laid down the revolver on the edge of the washstand within reach of her hand, and took a little stoppered bottle from the marble and poured some of its contents into a glass which she filled up with water.

“What’s that?” asked Tuppence sharply.

“Something to make you sleep soundly.”

“Are you going to poison me?” she asked in a whisper.

“Perhaps,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer, smiling agreeably.

“Then I shan’t drink it,” said Tuppence firmly. “I’d much rather be shot. At any rate that would make a row. But I won’t be killed off quietly like a lamb.”

Mrs. Vandemeyer stamped her foot. “Don’t be a little fool! Do you really think I want a hue and cry for murder out after me? It’s a sleeping draught, that’s all.”

In her heart of hearts Tuppence believed her. Nevertheless, she felt that once Mrs. Vandemeyer gave them the slip, the last hope of finding Tommy would be gone. She saw where a chance, a very problematical chance, lay, and she determined to risk all.

She lurched suddenly off the bed and fell on her knees before Mrs. Vandemeyer, clutching her skirts frantically.

“I don’t believe it,” she moaned. “It’s poison--I know it’s poison. Oh, don’t make me drink it”--her voice rose to a shriek--“don’t make me drink it!”

“Get up, you little idiot!” said Mrs. Vandemeyer, glass in hand, with a curling lip.

But Tuppence continued to cling and sob. Moreover, as she grovelled, she moved imperceptibly nearer to her objective.

Mrs. Vandemeyer jerked the girl to her knees. “Drink it at once!” Imperiously she pressed the glass to the girl’s lips.

“You swear it won’t hurt me?” Tuppence temporized.

“Of course it won’t hurt you. Don’t be a fool.”

“Will you swear it?”

“Yes, yes,” said the other impatiently. “I swear it.”

Tuppence raised a trembling left hand to the glass. “Very well.” Her mouth opened meekly.

Mrs. Vandemeyer gave a sigh of relief, off her guard for the moment. Then, quick as a flash, Tuppence jerked the glass upward as hard as she could. The fluid splashed into Mrs. Vandemeyer’s face, and during her momentary gasp, Tuppence’s right hand shot out and grasped the revolver where it lay on the edge of the washstand. The next moment she had sprung back a pace, and the revolver pointed straight at Mrs. Vandemeyer’s heart.

“Now who’s on top and who’s underneath?” she crowed.

The other’s face was convulsed with rage. Then a slow evil smile crept over it.

“Not a fool, then, after all! You did that well, girl. But you shall pay for it--oh, yes, you shall pay for it! I have a long memory!”

“Supposing we sit down,” said Tuppence pleasantly. “I’ll sit opposite you with the revolver in front of me--just in case of accidents. Now, let’s talk.”

“What about?” said Mrs. Vandemeyer sullenly.

Tuppence eyed her thoughtfully. She was remembering Boris’s words, “I believe you would sell-- _us!_” and the answer, “The price would have to be enormous.” Long ago, had not Whittington asked: “Who’s been blabbing? Rita?” Would Rita Vandemeyer prove to be the weak spot in the armour of Mr. Brown?

“Money----” Tuppence replied quietly.

Mrs. Vandemeyer started. “What do you mean?”

“You said just now that you had a long memory. A long memory isn’t half as useful as a long purse! Revenge is very unsatisfactory. Every one always says so. But money”--Tuppence warmed to her pet creed--“well, there’s nothing unsatisfactory about money, is there?”

“Do you think,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer scornfully, “that I am the kind of woman to sell my friends?”

“Yes,” said Tuppence promptly. “If the price was big enough.”

“A paltry hundred pounds or so!”

“No,” said Tuppence. “I should suggest--a hundred thousand!”

Her economical spirit did not permit her to mention the whole million dollars suggested by Julius.

A flush crept over Mrs. Vandemeyer’s face. In that moment Tuppence knew that the fish was hooked, and for the first time she felt a horror of her own money-loving spirit. It gave her a dreadful sense of kinship to the woman fronting her.

“Bah!” said Mrs. Vandemeyer, leaning back. “You haven’t got it.”

“No,” admitted Tuppence, “I haven’t--but I know some one who has. A friend of mine. He’s an American millionaire.”

“What does he want to know, this friend of yours?” said Mrs. Vandemeyer slowly.

Tuppence went through a momentary struggle, but it was Julius’s money, and his interests must come first. “He wants to know where Jane Finn is.”

Mrs. Vandemeyer showed no surprise. “I’m not sure where she is at the present moment. But there would be no difficulty about finding out.”

“Then”--Tuppence’s voice shook a little--“there’s a boy, a friend of mine. I’m afraid something’s happened to him, through your pal Boris. Tommy Beresford.”

“Never heard of him. But I’ll ask Boris. He’ll tell me anything he knows.”

Tuppence felt a terrific rise in her spirits. She leaned forward and lowered her voice.

_“Who is Mr. Brown?”_

Her quick eyes saw the sudden paling of the beautiful face.

“You can’t have learnt much about us if you don’t know that _nobody knows who Mr. Brown is_....”

“You do,” said Tuppence quietly.

“What makes you think that?”

“I don’t know,” said the girl truthfully. “But I’m sure.”

Mrs. Vandemeyer stared in front of her for a long time.

“Yes,” she said hoarsely, at last, “_I_ know. I was beautiful, you see--very beautiful----”

“You are still,” said Tuppence with admiration.

Mrs. Vandemeyer shook her head.

“Not beautiful enough,” she said in a soft dangerous voice. “Not--beautiful--enough! And sometimes, lately, I’ve been afraid.... It’s dangerous to know too much!” She leaned forward across the table. “Swear that my name shan’t be brought into it--that no one shall ever know.”

“I swear it. And, once’s he caught, you’ll be out of danger.”

“Shall I? Shall I ever be?” She clutched Tuppence’s arm. “You’re sure about the money? When shall I have it? There must be no delay.”

“This friend of mine will be here presently. There won’t be any delay--he’s a terrific hustler.”

A resolute look settled on Mrs. Vandemeyer’s face. “I’ll do it. It’s a great sum of money, and besides”--she gave a curious smile--“it is not--wise to throw over a woman like me!”

Suddenly she started, and her face blanched. “What was that?”

“I heard nothing.”

“Even the walls might have ears,” whispered the other, gazing round her fearfully. “I tell you I’m frightened. You don’t know him!”

“You don’t know him,” Mrs. Vandemeyer reiterated hoarsely. “He’s--ah!”

With a shriek of terror she sprang to her feet. Her outstretched hand pointed over Tuppence’s head. Then she swayed to the ground in a dead faint.

Tuppence looked round to see what had startled her.

In the doorway were Sir James Peel Edgerton and Julius Hersheimmer.

## CHAPTER XIII. THE VIGIL

Sir James brushed past Julius and hurriedly bent over the fallen woman.

"Heart," he said sharply. "Seeing us so suddenly must have given her a shock. Brandy--and quickly, or she'll slip through our fingers."

Julius hurried to the washstand.

"Not there," said Tuppence over her shoulder. "In the tantalus in the dining-room. Second door down the passage."

Between them Sir James and Tuppence lifted Mrs. Vandemeyer and carried her to the bed.

At that moment Julius re-entered the room, carrying a glass half full of the spirit which he handed to Sir James. While Tuppence lifted her head the lawyer tried to force a little of the spirit between her closed lips. Finally the woman opened her eyes feebly. Tuppence held the glass to her lips.

"Drink this."

Mrs. Vandemeyer complied. She tried to sit up--then fell back with a groan, her hand to her side.

"It's my heart," she whispered. "I mustn't talk."

"She'll do now," said Sir James.

All three moved away, conscious of a certain feeling of anticlimax. Tuppence related how Mrs. Vandemeyer had declared herself willing to disclose the identity of Mr. Brown, and to discover the whereabouts of Jane Finn.

"Splendid!" said Julius. "I guess that hundred thousand pounds will look just as good in the morning to the lady as it did over night."

"What you say is true," said Sir James meditatively. "I must confess, however, that I cannot help wishing we had not interrupted at the minute we did."

"Well," said Tuppence, "I don't think we ought to leave the flat. She seemed very frightened of 'Mr. Brown.' She looked round and said even walls had ears."

"Maybe she meant a dictaphone," said Julius with interest.

"Miss Tuppence is right," said Sir James quietly. "We must not leave the flat--if only for Mrs. Vandemeyer's sake. I would suggest that Miss Tuppence should go to bed, and that you and I, Mr. Hersheimmer, should share the vigil."

Tuppence was about to protest, but happening to glance at the bed she saw Mrs. Vandemeyer, her eyes half-open, with such an expression of mingled fear and malevolence on her face that it quite froze the words on her lips.

For a moment she wondered whether the faint and the heart attack had been a gigantic sham, but remembering the deadly pallor she could hardly credit the supposition. But she determined nevertheless to be on the alert.

Mrs. Vandemeyer lifted her lids. She seemed to be struggling to speak. Tuppence bent over her.

"Don't--leave----" she seemed unable to proceed, murmuring something that sounded like "sleepy." Then she tried again.

Tuppence bent lower still. It was only a breath.

"Mr.--Brown----" The voice stopped.

Moved by a sudden impulse, the girl said quickly:

"I shan't leave the flat. I shall sit up all night."

A flash of relief showed before the lids descended once more. But her words had awakened a new uneasiness in Tuppence. The big wardrobe loomed up in a sinister fashion before her eyes. Half-ashamed of herself, Tuppence pulled it open and looked inside. No one--of course! She stooped down and looked under the bed. There was no other possible hiding-place.

It was absurd, this giving way to nerves! Slowly she went out of the room. Sir James turned to her.

"Lock the door on the outside, please, Miss Tuppence, and take out the key. There must be no chance of anyone entering that room."

"How did you get in, by the way?" asked Tuppence suddenly.

"Well, Albert got me on the phone all right," said Julius. "The boy suggested sending us up in the coal lift instead of ringing the bell. And sure enough we landed in the scullery. I guess I'd better go down and ease his young mind."

Tuppence led Sir James to Mrs. Vandemeyer's boudoir, and shook her head resolutely when he urged her to go to bed.

Julius reappeared, having reassured Albert. The power of the money bribe could not fail.

"What put her up to clearing out?" asked Julius.

Sir James stroked his chin thoughtfully.

"The room was in great disorder. That looks as though her flight was unpremeditated. Almost as though she got a sudden warning to go from some one."

"Mr. Brown, I suppose," said Julius scoffingly.

"Why not?" said the lawyer. "Remember, you yourself have once been worsted by him."

Julius flushed with vexation.

"I feel just mad when I think of how I handed out Jane's photograph to him like a lamb. Gee, if I ever lay hands on it again, I'll freeze on to it like--like hell!"

"That contingency is likely to be a remote one," said the other dryly.

"I guess you're right," said Julius frankly. "It's the original I'm out after. Where do you think she can be, Sir James?"

"Impossible to say. But I've a very good idea where she _has_ been. The Bournemouth nursing home."

"There? Impossible. I asked."

"No, my dear sir, you asked if anyone of the name of Jane Finn had been there. Now, if the girl had been placed there it would almost certainly be under an assumed name."

"Perhaps the doctor's in it too," suggested Tuppence.

"I don't think so," said Julius. "I'm pretty sure Dr. Hall's all right."

"Hall, did you say?" asked Sir James. "That is curious--really very curious. Because I happened to meet him this morning. Staying at the _Métropole_, he told me."

A silence settled down over the party. There were sudden creaks of the furniture, imperceptible rustlings in the curtains. Suddenly Tuppence sprang up with a cry.

"I can't help it. I know Mr. Brown's somewhere in the flat! I can _feel_ him."

"Sure, Tuppence, how could he be?" said Julius. "This door's open into the hall."

"I can't help it. I _feel_ he's here!"

She looked appealingly at Sir James, who replied gravely:

"With due deference to your feelings, Miss Tuppence (and mine as well for that matter), I do not see how it is humanly possible for anyone to be in the flat without our knowledge."

"Sitting up at night is always rather jumpy," Tuppence confessed.

"Yes," said Sir James. "We are in the condition of people holding a séance."

The hours drew on. With the first faint glimmerings of dawn, Sir James drew aside the curtains. With the coming of the light, the dreads and fancies of the past night seemed absurd.

"Hooray!" said Tuppence. "And we shall find Tommy. And Jane Finn. I shall ask Mr. Carter if I can't be made a Dame!"

At seven o'clock Tuppence went to make some tea. She returned with a tray, containing the teapot and four cups.

"Who's the other cup for?" inquired Julius.

"The prisoner, of course."

Sir James and Julius accompanied her to the door. She put the key in the lock, and turned it, then paused.

"Supposing, after all, she's escaped?" she murmured in a whisper.

"Plumb impossible," replied Julius reassuringly.

But Sir James said nothing.

Tuppence drew a long breath and entered.

"Good morning," she remarked cheerfully. "I've brought you some tea."

Mrs. Vandemeyer did not reply. With a sudden fear clutching at her heart, Tuppence ran to the bed. The hand she lifted was cold as ice.... Mrs. Vandemeyer would never speak now....

Her cry brought the others. Mrs. Vandemeyer was dead--must have been dead some hours. She had evidently died in her sleep.

"If that isn't the cruellest luck," cried Julius in despair.

The lawyer was calmer, but there was a curious gleam in his eyes.

"If it is luck," he replied.

"You don't think--but, say, that's plumb impossible--no one could have got in."

"No," admitted the lawyer. "I don't see how they could. And yet--she is on the point of betraying Mr. Brown, and--she dies. Is it only chance? We must find out."

"The window's open," remarked Julius. "Do you think----"

Tuppence shook her head. "The balcony only goes along as far as the boudoir. We were there."

"Mr. Brown's methods are not so crude," said Sir James. "We must send for a doctor, but before we do so, is there anything in this room that might be of value to us?"

Hastily, the three searched. A charred mass in the grate indicated that Mrs. Vandemeyer had been burning papers. Nothing of importance remained.

"There's that," said Tuppence suddenly, pointing to a small, old-fashioned safe let into the wall.

The key was in the lock, and Julius swung open the door, and searched inside.

"Well," said Tuppence impatiently.

There was a pause before Julius answered, then he withdrew his head and shut to the door.

"Nothing," he said.

In five minutes a brisk young doctor arrived.

"Heart failure, or possibly an overdose of some sleeping-draught." He sniffed. "Rather an odour of chloral in the air."

Tuppence remembered the glass she had upset. A new thought drove her to the washstand. She found the little bottle from which Mrs. Vandemeyer had poured a few drops.

It had been three parts full. Now-- _it was empty_.

## CHAPTER XIV. A CONSULTATION

NOTHING was more surprising and bewildering to Tuppence than the ease and simplicity with which everything was arranged, owing to Sir James’s skilful handling. The doctor accepted quite readily the theory that Mrs. Vandemeyer had accidentally taken an overdose of chloral. He doubted whether an inquest would be necessary.

Shortly afterwards a nurse arrived to take charge, and the others left the ill-omened building.

“And what now?” asked Julius, with a gesture of despair. “I guess we’re down and out for good.”

“No,” said Sir James quietly. “There is still the chance that Dr. Hall may be able to tell us something. I think I told you that he is staying at the _Métropole_. Shall we say after a bath and breakfast?”

A little after eleven they drew up before the _Métropole_, and in a few minutes the little doctor came hurrying towards them.

A quizzical gleam came into the doctor’s eye as he shook hands with Julius.

“Ah, yes, my young friend of the tree episode! Ankle all right, eh?”

“To come to the point, can we have a word with you in private?” asked Sir James.

The doctor led the way to an undisturbed room, and looked inquiringly at Sir James.

“Dr. Hall, I am very anxious to find a certain young lady for the purpose of obtaining a statement from her. I have reason to believe that she has been at one time or another in your establishment at Bournemouth.”

“I suppose it is a matter of testimony?”

Sir James hesitated a moment, then he replied:

“Yes.”

“What is the young lady’s name?”

“The name,” said Sir James bluntly, “is really immaterial. She would be almost certainly sent to you under an assumed one. But I should like to know if you are acquainted with a Mrs. Vandemeyer?”

“Mrs. Vandemeyer, of 20 South Audley Mansions? I know her slightly.”

“You do not know that Mrs. Vandemeyer is dead?”

“Dear, dear, I had no idea of it! When did it happen?”

“She took an overdose of chloral last night.”

“Purposely?”

“Accidentally, it is believed. I should not like to say myself. Anyway, she was found dead this morning.”

“I presume she was a friend of yours, since you are acquainted with all these details.”

“I am acquainted with the details because--well, it was I who found her dead.”

“Indeed,” said the doctor, starting.

“Yes,” said Sir James, and stroked his chin reflectively. “Is it not a fact that Mrs. Vandemeyer committed a young relative of hers to your charge?”

“That is the case,” said the doctor quietly.

“Under the name of----?”

“Janet Vandemeyer. I understood her to be a niece of Mrs. Vandemeyer’s.”

“And she came to you?”

“As far as I can remember in June or July of 1915.”

“Was she a mental case?”

“She is perfectly sane, if that is what you mean. I understood from Mrs. Vandemeyer that the girl had been with her on the _Lusitania_, and had suffered a severe shock in consequence.”

The doctor looked at them all curiously.

“You spoke of wanting a statement from her,” he said. “Supposing she is not able to give one?”

“What? You have just said that she is perfectly sane.”

“So she is. Nevertheless, if you want a statement from her concerning any events prior to May 7, 1915, she will not be able to give it to you.”

“But why, man? Darn it all, why?” cried Julius.

“Because Janet Vandemeyer is suffering from a complete loss of memory.”

_“What?”_

“Quite so. An interesting case, a _very_ interesting case.” There was something rather ghoulish in the little man’s satisfaction.

“Then the first thing she remembers?”

“Is landing with the survivors. She did not know her own name. She couldn’t even speak her own tongue. I suggested a specialist, of course. There’s a very good man in Paris--makes a study of these cases--but Mrs. Vandemeyer opposed the idea of publicity that might result from such a course.”

“I can imagine she would,” said Sir James grimly.

“Sooner or later, the memory will return--as suddenly as it went. But in all probability the girl will have entirely forgotten the intervening period, and will take up life where she left off--at the sinking of the _Lusitania_. Sometimes another shock does the trick. One restores what the other took away.”

“Another shock, eh?” said Julius thoughtfully. Suddenly he hit the table such a resounding bang with his fist that every one jumped, the doctor most of all.

“I’ve got it! Say Jane was to cross the herring pond again, and the same thing was to happen. Wouldn’t that give a mighty big bump to her subconscious self, or whatever the jargon is, and start it functioning again right away?”

“In my own opinion, it would be successful. It is unfortunate that there is no chance of the conditions repeating themselves as you suggest.”

“Not by nature, perhaps, doc. But I’m talking about art. Hire a liner----”

“A liner!” murmured Dr. Hall faintly.

“Hire some passengers, hire a submarine--that’s the only difficulty, I guess. Still, ever heard of the word ‘graft,’ sir? Well, graft gets there every time! If every one hustles round and screams loud enough that the ship is sinking, with a well-drilled lot of artistes doing the hysterical stunt on deck, why--she ought to be right back where she was in May, 1915.”

Dr. Hall looked at Julius. Everything that he was for the moment incapable of saying was eloquent in that look.

Dr. Hall found his voice.

“But the expense, my dear sir.” His voice rose. “The expense! It will be _colossal!_”

“Money doesn’t worry me any,” explained Julius simply.

Dr. Hall turned an appealing face to Sir James, who smiled slightly.

“Mr. Hersheimmer is very well off--very well off indeed.”

The doctor’s glance came back to Julius with a new and subtle quality in it. The doctor’s eyes held the deference accorded to a really rich man.

“Very remarkable plan. Very remarkable,” he murmured. “And you really mean to carry out this remarkable plan of yours.”

“You bet your bottom dollar I do.”

The doctor believed him--which was a tribute to his nationality.

“I cannot guarantee a cure,” he pointed out.

“Sure, that’s all right,” said Julius. “You just trot out Jane, and leave the rest to me.”

“Jane?”

“Miss Janet Vandemeyer, then. Can we get on the long distance to your place right away, and ask them to send her up?”

The doctor stared.

“I beg your pardon, Mr. Hersheimmer. I thought you understood.”

“Understood what?”

“That Miss Vandemeyer is no longer under my care.”

## CHAPTER XV. TUPPENCE RECEIVES A PROPOSAL

JULIUS sprang up.

“What? When did she leave?”

“Let me see. To-day is Monday, is it not? It must have been last Wednesday--why, surely--yes, it was the same evening that you--er--fell out of my tree.”

“That evening? Before, or after?”

“Let me see--oh yes, afterwards. A very urgent message arrived from Mrs. Vandemeyer. The young lady and the nurse who was in charge of her left by the night train.”

Julius sank back again into his chair.

“Nurse Edith--left with a patient--I remember,” he muttered. “My God, to have been so near!”

Dr. Hall looked bewildered.

“I don’t understand. Is the young lady not with her aunt, after all?”

Tuppence was about to speak when a warning glance from Sir James made her hold her tongue. The lawyer rose.

The doctor hesitated.

“Surely the girl has not been kidnapped. You do not think I ought to go to the police?”

“No, no. In all probability the young lady is with other relations,” said Sir James.

The doctor wished them good-bye, and they left the hotel.

Sir James shrugged his shoulders.

“You might advertise for the nurse who accompanied the girl. That is the only course I can suggest, and I must confess I do not hope for much result. Otherwise there is nothing to be done.”

“Nothing?” said Tuppence blankly. “And--Tommy?”

Over her downcast head his eyes met Julius’s, and almost imperceptibly he shook his head. Julius understood. The lawyer considered the case hopeless. Sir James took Tuppence’s hand.

“You are going away?”

“I told you. Don’t you remember? To Scotland. My dear young lady, I can do nothing more, I fear.”

His words gave Tuppence an extraordinarily desolate feeling.

Julius was bending over the car. A momentary pity came into Sir James’s keen eyes.

“Don’t be too disconsolate, Miss Tuppence,” he said in a low voice. “Remember, holiday-time isn’t always all playtime. One sometimes manages to put in some work as well.”

Something in his tone made Tuppence glance up sharply. He shook his head with a smile.

“No, I shan’t say any more. Great mistake to say too much. Remember that. Never tell all you know--not even to the person you know best. Understand? Good-bye.”

He strode away. Was this a hint? Did he mean that, after all, he had not abandoned the case; that, secretly, he would be working on it still while----

“You’re looking kind of thoughtful,” Julius remarked as they started off. “Did the old guy say anything more?”

Tuppence opened her mouth impulsively, and then shut it again. Sir James’s words sounded in her ears. And like a flash there came into her mind another memory. Julius before the safe in the flat, her own question and the pause before his reply, “Nothing.” Was there really nothing? If he could make a reservation, so could she.

“Nothing particular,” she replied.

“Say, shall we go for a spin in the park?”

For a while they ran on under the trees in silence.

“Say, Miss Tuppence, do you think I’m ever going to find Jane? Sir James to-day hadn’t got any hope at all, I could see that. I don’t like him--we don’t gee together somehow--but he’s pretty cute, and I guess he wouldn’t quit if there was any chance of success--now, would he?”

“He suggested advertising for the nurse,” she reminded him.

“Yes, with a ‘forlorn hope’ flavour to his voice! I’ve half a mind to go back to the States right away.”

“Oh no!” cried Tuppence. “We’ve got to find Tommy.”

“I sure forgot Beresford,” said Julius contritely. “We must find him. Say, Miss Tuppence, there’s something I’d like to ask you. You and Beresford. Not got a sort of kindly feeling for one another?”

“Certainly not,” said Tuppence with warmth. “Tommy and I are friends--nothing more.”

“I guess every pair of lovers has said that sometime or another,” observed Julius. “What about marriage?”

“I intend to marry, of course,” replied Tuppence. “That is, if”--she paused, knew a momentary longing to draw back, and then stuck to her guns bravely--“I can find some one rich enough to make it worth my while. That’s frank, isn’t it?”

“I never despise business instinct,” said Julius. “What about me?”

_“You?”_

“Sure thing.”

“Oh, I couldn’t!”

“Why not?”

“It would seem so unfair.”

“I don’t see anything unfair about it. I admire you immensely, Miss Tuppence. You’re so darned plucky. Say the word, and we’ll run round right away to some high-class jeweller, and fix up the ring business.”

“I can’t,” gasped Tuppence.

“Because of Beresford?”

“No, no, _no!_”

“I’d be obliged if you’d do me the favour to think it over until to-morrow.”

“It’s no use.”

“Still, I guess we’ll leave it like that.”

Neither of them spoke again until they reached the _Ritz_.

Tuppence went upstairs to her room, feeling morally battered to the ground. Sitting down in front of the glass, she stared at her own reflection.

“Fool,” murmured Tuppence at length, making a grimace. “Little fool. Everything you want--everything you’ve ever hoped for, and you go and bleat out ‘no’ like an idiotic little sheep. It’s your one chance. Why don’t you take it?”

As if in answer, her eyes fell on a small snapshot of Tommy in a shabby frame. She held it to her lips and burst into a fit of sobbing.

“Oh, Tommy, Tommy,” she cried, “I do love you so--and I may never see you again....”

“That’s that,” she observed sternly. “I seem to have fallen in love--with an idiot of a boy who probably doesn’t care two straws about me. It’s dreadful to feel you’ve been false to your principles. I don’t know what to say to Julius, I’m sure. I wonder if he did find anything in that safe----”

Tuppence’s meditations went off on another tack. She reviewed the events of last night carefully and persistently. Somehow, they seemed bound up with Sir James’s enigmatical words....

Suddenly she gave a great start--the colour faded out of her face.

“Impossible,” she murmured. “Impossible! I must be going mad even to think of such a thing....”

Monstrous--yet it explained everything....

After a moment’s reflection she sat down and wrote a note, weighing each word as she did so, and addressed it to Julius. She went down the passage to his sitting-room. As she had expected, the room was empty. She left the note on the table.

A small page-boy was waiting outside her own door when she returned.

“Telegram for you, miss.”

Tuppence tore it open carelessly. Then she gave a cry. The telegram was from Tommy!

## CHAPTER XVI. FURTHER ADVENTURES OF TOMMY

FROM a darkness punctuated with throbbing stabs of fire, Tommy dragged his senses slowly back to life.

“Damn!” said Tommy, and tried to sit up. He had remembered. He was in that sinister house in Soho.

“He is coming to,” remarked a voice very near Tommy’s ear. He recognized it at once for that of the bearded and efficient German, and lay artistically inert.

“Well, here goes,” murmured Tommy to himself. “Damn!” he observed, and this time succeeded in sitting up.

In a minute the German stepped forward and placed a glass to his lips, with the brief command “Drink.” Tommy obeyed.

He was lying on a couch in the room in which the meeting had been held. Tommy missed one face. The man known as Number One was no longer of the company.

“Ah, my young friend, it is lucky for you your skull is so thick. The good Conrad struck hard.” He indicated the evil-faced doorkeeper by a nod.

“Oh,” said Tommy, “so you’re Conrad, are you? It strikes me the thickness of my skull was lucky for you too.”

Tommy Beresford was one of those young Englishmen not distinguished by any special intellectual ability, but who are emphatically at their best in what is known as a “tight place.” Tommy realized perfectly that in his own wits lay the only chance of escape, and behind his casual manner he was racking his brains furiously.

“How did you get in?” the German asked.

“Dear old Conrad here.” Tommy smiled deprecatingly at him. “I hesitate to suggest pensioning off a faithful servant, but you really ought to have a better watchdog.”

Conrad snarled impotently, and said sullenly, as the man with the beard swung round upon him:

“He gave the word. How was I to know?”

“Can you give us any reason why we should not put you to death?” asked the German.

“Several,” replied Tommy. “Why didn’t you kill me off at once before I regained consciousness?”

The German hesitated, and Tommy seized his advantage.

“Because you didn’t know how much I knew--and where I obtained that knowledge. If you kill me now, you never will know.”

But here the emotions of Boris became too much for him.

“You hell-hound of a spy,” he screamed. “Kill him! Kill him!”

“You hear?” said the German. “What have you to say to that?”

“Say?” Tommy shrugged his shoulders. “Pack of fools. How did I get into this place? Remember what dear old Conrad said-- _with your own password_, wasn’t it? How did I get hold of that? You don’t suppose I came up those steps haphazard and said the first thing that came into my head?”

Tommy was pleased with the concluding words of this speech. His only regret was that Tuppence was not present to appreciate its full flavour.

“That is true,” said the working man suddenly. “Comrades, we have been betrayed!”

“You will tell us who has betrayed us,” said the German. “Boris, here, knows pretty ways of making people speak!”

“Bah!” said Tommy scornfully, fighting down a singularly unpleasant feeling in the pit of his stomach. “You will neither torture me nor kill me.”

“And why not?” asked Boris.

“Because you’d kill the goose that lays the golden eggs,” replied Tommy quietly.

The man in the shabby clothes stared at Tommy searchingly.

“He’s bluffing you, Boris,” he said quietly.

Tommy hated him. Had the man seen through him? “Do I look as though I thought there were the least chance of your killing me?” said Tommy calmly.

He looked confidently round, and was glad they could not hear the persistent beating of his heart which gave the lie to his words.

“And why am I so confident? Because I know something that puts me in a position to propose a bargain.”

“A bargain?” The bearded man took him up sharply.

“Yes--a bargain. My life and liberty against----” He paused.

“Against what?”

Slowly Tommy spoke.

“The papers that Danvers brought over from America in the _Lusitania_.”

The effect of his words was electrical. The German leaned over Tommy, his face purple with excitement.

“_Himmel!_ You have got them, then?”

With magnificent calm Tommy shook his head.

“You know where they are?” persisted the German.

Again Tommy shook his head. “Not in the least.”

“I don’t know where the papers are--but I believe that I can find them. I have a theory----”

“Pah!”

“I call it a theory--but I’m pretty sure of my facts. If I can produce the papers--you give me my life and liberty in exchange. Is it a bargain?”

“And if we refuse?” said the German quietly. Tommy lay back on the couch.

“The 29th,” he said thoughtfully, “is less than a fortnight ahead----”

For a moment the German hesitated. Then he made a sign to Conrad.

“Take him into the other room.”

For five minutes, Tommy sat on the bed in the dingy room next door. His heart was beating violently. He had risked all on this throw. How would they decide? And all the while he talked flippantly to Conrad, enraging the cross-grained doorkeeper to the point of homicidal mania.

At last the German called imperiously to Conrad to return.

“Let’s hope the judge hasn’t put his black cap on,” remarked Tommy frivolously. “That’s right, Conrad, march me in. The prisoner is at the bar, gentlemen.”

“We accept,” said the German harshly, “on terms. The papers must be delivered to us before you go free.”

“Idiot!” said Tommy amiably. “How do you think I can look for them if you keep me tied by the leg here?”

“We prefer,” said the German coldly, “that you should remain here. One of our number will carry out your instructions minutely.”

“You’re tying my hands,” complained Tommy. “The other fellow will muff it up as likely as not.”

The German rapped the table.

“Those are our terms. Otherwise, death!”

“I like your style. Curt, but attractive. So be it, then. But one thing is essential, I must see the girl.”

“What girl?”

“Jane Finn, of course.”

The other looked at him curiously for some minutes, then he said slowly, and as though choosing his words with care:

“Do you not know that she can tell you nothing?”

“I shall not ask her to tell me anything,” said Tommy quietly. “Not in so many words, that is.”

“Then why see her?”

Tommy paused.

“To watch her face when I ask her one question,” he replied at last.

Again there was a look in the German’s eyes that Tommy did not quite understand.

“And you think that will tell you anything?” More than ever, Tommy felt that there was a factor somewhere that he did not understand. “I wonder whether, after all, you know as much as we think?” the German said softly.

Tommy felt his ascendancy less sure. What had he said wrong? He spoke out on the impulse of the moment.

“There may be things that you know which I do not. But equally I’ve got something up my sleeve that _you_ don’t know about. Danvers was a damned clever fellow----” He broke off as if he had said too much.

But the German’s face had lightened a little.

“Danvers,” he murmured. “I see----” He waved to Conrad. “Take him away. Upstairs--you know.”

“Wait a minute,” said Tommy. “What about the girl?”

“We will see about it. Only one person can decide that.”

“Who?” asked Tommy. But he knew the answer.

“Mr. Brown----”

“Shall I see him?”

“Perhaps.”

“Come,” said Conrad harshly.

On the floor above Conrad opened a door and Tommy passed into a small room. Tommy heard the sound of the key being turned in the lock.

There was something peculiarly airless about the atmosphere. Then he realized that there was no window. Four pictures hung crookedly on the wall representing scenes from Faust. Marguerite with her box of jewels, the church scene, Siebel and his flowers, and Faust and Mephistopheles. The latter brought Tommy’s mind back to Mr. Brown again. The place was a living tomb....

He sank on to the bed. His head ached badly; also, he was hungry.

“Anyway,” said Tommy, trying to cheer himself, “I shall see the chief--the mysterious Mr. Brown and with a bit of luck in bluffing I shall see the mysterious Jane Finn also. After that----”

After that Tommy was forced to admit the prospect looked dreary.

## CHAPTER XVII. ANNETTE

The troubles of the future, however, soon faded before the troubles of the present. And of these, the most immediate and pressing was that of hunger. He regretfully recognized the fact that he would not make a success of a hunger strike.

“It’s that sour-faced brute Conrad,” he decided. “This is just a bit of spite on his part. I’m certain of it.”

Why not wait in ambush for Conrad behind the door, and when he entered bring down one of the decrepit pictures smartly on to his head? Intoxicated by his plan, Tommy gently unhooked the picture of the Devil and Faust, and settled himself in position.

At ten o’clock hope deserted him, and he flung himself on the bed. In five minutes his woes were forgotten.

The sound of the key turning in the lock awoke him. It was eight o’clock.

The door swung open. It was not Conrad who entered, but a girl. She carried a tray which she set down on the table.

Tommy decided at once that she was one of the most beautiful girls he had ever seen. Her hair was a full rich brown, with sudden glints of gold in it as though there were imprisoned sunbeams struggling in its depths. Her eyes, set wide apart, were hazel, a golden hazel that again recalled a memory of sunbeams.

A delirious thought shot through Tommy’s mind.

“Are you Jane Finn?” he asked breathlessly.

The girl shook her head wonderingly.

“My name is Annette, monsieur.”

She spoke in a soft, broken English.

The girl turned away to the door.

“Wait a sec,” cried Tommy. “What are you doing in this house?”

“I do the _service_, monsieur.”

“I see,” said Tommy. “You know what I asked you just now. Have you ever heard that name?”

“I have heard people speak of Jane Finn, I think.”

“You don’t know where she is? She’s not in this house, for instance?”

“Oh no, monsieur. I must go now--they will be waiting for me.”

She hurried out. The key turned in the lock.

At one o’clock Annette reappeared, but this time Conrad accompanied her.

“Good morning,” said Tommy amiably. “You have _not_ used Pear’s soap, I see.”

“Talk away,” grunted the man. “It’s little enough time you’ll have to talk in, maybe.”

That evening at eight o’clock the key turned. The girl was alone.

“Shut the door,” he commanded. “I want to speak to you.” She obeyed.

“Look here, Annette, I want you to help me get out of this.” She shook her head.

“Impossible. There are three of them on the floor below.”

“But you would help me if you could?”

“No, monsieur.”

“Why not?”

“I think--they are my own people. You have spied upon them. They are quite right to keep you here.”

“They’re a bad lot, Annette. If you’ll help me, I’ll take you away from the lot of them.”

“I dare not, monsieur; I am afraid of them.”

“Won’t you save another girl from their clutches?” cried Tommy.

“You mean Jane Finn?”

The girl looked at him, then passed her hand across her forehead.

“Jane Finn. Always I hear that name. It is familiar.”

But the girl turned away abruptly.

“I know nothing--only the name.” She walked towards the door. Suddenly she uttered a cry. She had caught sight of the picture he had laid against the wall the night before. For a moment he caught a look of terror in her eyes. As inexplicably it changed to relief. He rehung the picture on the wall thoughtfully.

Three more days went by in dreary inaction. He saw no one but Conrad and Annette, and the girl had become dumb. A kind of dark suspicion smouldered in her eyes. He gathered from Conrad that they were waiting for orders from “Mr. Brown.”

But the evening of the third day brought a rude awakening.

It was barely seven o’clock when the door was flung open. Conrad entered. With him was the evil-looking Number 14.

“Evenin’, gov’nor,” said the man with a leer. “Got those ropes, mate?”

The silent Conrad produced a length of fine cord. The next minute Number 14’s hands, horribly dexterous, were winding the cord round his limbs, while Conrad held him down. Then at last Conrad spoke:

“Thought you’d bluffed us, did you? With what you knew, and what you didn’t know. Bargained with us! And all the time it was bluff! Bluff! You know less than a kitten. But your number’s up now all right, you b---- swine.”

Tommy lay silent. He had failed. Suddenly a thought occurred to him.

“A very good speech, Conrad,” he said approvingly. “But wherefore the bonds and fetters? Why not let this kind gentleman here cut my throat without delay?”

“Garn,” said Number 14 unexpectedly. “Think we’re as green as to do you in here, and have the police nosing round? Not ‘alf! We’ve ordered the carriage for your lordship to-morrow mornin’, but in the meantime we’re not taking any chances, see!”

“Nothing,” said Tommy, “could be plainer than your words--unless it was your face.”

“You don’t kid us that way again,” said Number 14. “Talking as though you were still at the blooming _Ritz_, aren’t you?”

Tommy made no reply. He was engaged in wondering how Mr. Brown had discovered his identity. He decided that Tuppence, in the throes of anxiety, had gone to the police, and that his disappearance having been made public the gang had not been slow to put two and two together.

The two men departed and the door slammed. He was utterly helpless.

About an hour had passed when the key was softly turned. It was Annette.

Suddenly he heard Conrad’s voice:

“Come out of it, Annette. He doesn’t want any supper to-night.”

Without looking at Tommy the girl went over to the table, and picked up the tray. She raised a hand and turned out the light.

“Le beau petit monsieur,” cried Annette, pausing by the bed in the darkness. “You have tied him up well, _hein?_ He is like a trussed chicken!” The frank amusement in her tone jarred on the boy; but at that moment, to his amazement, he felt her hand running lightly over his bonds, and something small and cold was pressed into the palm of his hand.

The door shut. Tommy heard Conrad say:

“Lock it and give me the key.”

The object Annette had thrust into his hand was a small penknife, the blade open. From the way she had studiously avoided looking at him, and her action with the light, he came to the conclusion that the room was overlooked. There must be a peep-hole somewhere in the walls. He saw that he had probably been under observation all the time. Were her denials intended primarily for the listeners?

Could he, bound as he was, manage to cut his bonds? He essayed cautiously to rub the open blade up and down on the cord that bound his wrists. It was an awkward business, and drew a smothered “Ow” of pain from him as the knife cut into his wrist. He cut the flesh badly, but at last he felt the cord slacken. The only outlet was the door, and he would have to wait until the two men returned to fetch him. But when they did.... Tommy smiled! Moving with infinite caution in the dark room, he found and unhooked the famous picture. He waited.

The night passed slowly, but at last he heard footsteps. He clutched the picture firmly.

The door opened. Conrad went straight towards the gas to light it. Number 14 followed. As he stepped across the threshold, Tommy brought the picture down with terrific force on his head. Number 14 went down amidst a stupendous crash of broken glass. In a minute Tommy had slipped out and pulled to the door. The key was in the lock. He turned it and withdrew it just as Conrad hurled himself against the door from the inside with a volley of curses.

The German’s voice came up the stairs.

“Gott im Himmel! Conrad, what is it?”

Tommy felt a small hand thrust into his. Beside him stood Annette. She pointed up a rickety ladder that apparently led to some attics.

“Quick--up here!” She dragged him after her up the ladder into a dusty garret littered with lumber.

“This won’t do,” said Tommy. “It’s a regular trap. There’s no way out.”

“Hush! Wait.” She crept to the top of the ladder and listened. The German and another were trying to force the door in. Annette explained in a whisper:

“They will think you are still inside. They cannot hear what Conrad says. The door is too thick.”

“I thought you could hear what went on in the room?”

“There is a peep-hole into the next room. It was clever of you to guess. But they will not think of that--they are only anxious to get in.”

“Yes--but look here----”

“Leave it to me.” To his amazement, Tommy saw that she was fastening the end of a long piece of string to the handle of a big cracked jug.

“Have you the key of the door? Give it to me.”

He handed it to her.

“I am going down. Do you think you can go halfway, and then swing yourself down _behind_ the ladder, so that they will not see you? There’s a big cupboard in the shadow of the landing. Stand behind it. Take the end of this string in your hand. When I’ve let the others out--_pull!_”

She had flitted lightly down the ladder and was in the midst of the group with a loud cry:

“Mon Dieu! Mon Dieu! Qu’est-ce qu’il y a?”

Very cautiously Tommy swung himself down the back of the ladder and crouched behind the cupboard.

“Ah!” Annette appeared to stumble over something. She stooped. “Mon Dieu, voilà la clef!”

The German snatched it from her. He unlocked the door. Conrad stumbled out, swearing.

“Where is he? Have you got him?”

“We have seen no one,” said the German sharply.

“He’s got away,” said Conrad.

“Impossible. He would have passed us.”

At that moment, with an ecstatic smile Tommy pulled the string. A crash of crockery came from the attic above. In a trice the men were pushing each other up the rickety ladder and had disappeared into the darkness above.

Quick as a flash Tommy leapt from his hiding-place and dashed down the stairs, pulling the girl with him. There was no one in the hall. He fumbled over the bolts and chain. At last the door swung open. He turned. Annette had disappeared. He would not go without her.

And suddenly there was an outcry overhead, an exclamation from the German, and then Annette’s voice, clear and high:

“Ma foi, he has escaped! And quickly! Who would have thought it?”

And then, louder still, the words floated down to him:

“This is a terrible house. I want to go back to Marguerite. To Marguerite. _To Marguerite!_”

Tommy had run back to the stairs. Then his heart sank. Conrad was leaping down the stairs, uttering a savage cry at the sight of him. After him came the others.

Tommy stopped Conrad’s rush with a straight blow that caught him on the point of the jaw, and he fell like a log. From higher up the staircase there was a flash, and a bullet grazed Tommy’s ear. As regards Annette he could do nothing.

He leapt for the door, slamming it behind him. In front of the house was a baker’s van. The driver jumped to the pavement and tried to bar Tommy’s way. Again Tommy’s fist shot out, and the driver sprawled on the pavement.

Tommy took to his heels and ran--none too soon. A hail of bullets followed him. He turned the corner of the square, the footsteps of his pursuers behind him. Then he stumbled over a prostrate figure, which started up with a yell of alarm and dashed off down the street. Tommy drew back into a doorway, and had the pleasure of seeing his two pursuers industriously tracking down the red herring!

After a Turkish Bath he turned into an A.B.C. shop and ordered eggs and bacon. Whilst he ate, he read a morning paper. Suddenly he stiffened. There was a long article on Kramenin, described as the “man behind Bolshevism” in Russia. In the centre of the page was his portrait.

“So that’s who Number 1 is,” said Tommy with his mouth full of eggs and bacon. “Not a doubt about it, I must push on.”

He betook himself to Whitehall, sent up his name, and the message that it was urgent. A few minutes later he was in the presence of the man who did not here go by the name of “Mr. Carter.” There was a frown on his face.

“Look here, you’ve no business to come asking for me in this way. I thought that was distinctly understood?”

“It was, sir. But I judged it important to lose no time.”

And as briefly as possible he detailed the experiences of the last few days. All traces of displeasure had now left his face.

“Quite right. Every moment’s of value. You say you’ve recognized Number 1 to be Kramenin? That’s important. You say two faces were familiar to you? Just look through these photos.”

Tommy held one up.

“Ah, Westway! Shouldn’t have thought it. As for the other fellow----” He handed another photograph to Tommy, and smiled at the other’s exclamation. “I’m right, then. Irishman. Prominent Unionist M.P. All a blind, of course. The 29th, you say, is the date. That gives us very little time--very little time indeed.”

“But----” Tommy hesitated.

Mr. Carter read his thoughts.

“We can deal with the General Strike menace, I think. But if that draft treaty turns up--we’re done. England will be plunged in anarchy. Come on, Beresford, we’ll go and have a look at this house of yours.”

Two constables were on duty in front of the house in Soho.

“The birds have flown,” said Mr. Carter.

Everything was just as it had been: the prison room with the crooked pictures, the broken jug in the attic. But nowhere was there a trace of papers. And there was no sign of Annette.

“What you tell me about the girl puzzled me,” said Mr. Carter. “You believe that she deliberately went back?”

“It would seem so, sir. She ran upstairs while I was getting the door open.”

“H’m, she must belong to the gang, then; but, being a woman, didn’t feel like standing by to see a personable young man killed.”

“By the way,” observed Mr. Carter, “have you shown yourself to Miss Tuppence yet?”

“Tuppence? I was afraid she might get a bit rattled. Did she go to the police?”

Mr. Carter shook his head.

“Then I wonder how they twigged me.”

Tommy explained. Mr. Carter nodded thoughtfully.

“True, that’s rather a curious point. Unless the mention of the _Ritz_ was an accidental remark?”

“It might have been, sir. But they must have found out about me suddenly in some way.”

Hailing a taxi briskly Tommy was swiftly borne to the _Ritz_.

“Wonder what she’s been up to. Dogging ‘Rita’ most likely. By the way, I suppose that’s who Annette meant by Marguerite. I didn’t get it at the time.”

The taxi drew up at the _Ritz_. Tommy burst into its sacred portals eagerly, but his enthusiasm received a check. He was informed that Miss Cowley had gone out a quarter of an hour ago.

## CHAPTER XVIII. THE TELEGRAM

BAFFLED for the moment, Tommy strolled into the restaurant, and ordered a meal of surpassing excellence.

He was in the middle of conveying a particularly choice morsel of Sole à la Jeanette to his mouth, when he caught sight of Julius entering the room. Julius strode across, and pump-handled Tommy's hand with what seemed to the latter quite unnecessary vigour.

"Holy snakes!" he ejaculated. "Is it really you?"

"Of course it is. Why shouldn't it be?"

"Why shouldn't it be? Say, man, don't you know you've been given up for dead?"

"Who thought I was dead?" demanded Tommy.

"Tuppence."

"Where is Tuppence, by the way?" Tommy asked.

"Isn't she here?"

"No, the fellows at the office said she'd just gone out."

"Gone shopping, I guess. What on God's earth have you been doing all this time?"

Tommy plunged into his recital.

Julius listened spellbound.

"Bully for you. Reads like a dime novel!"

"And now for the home front," said Tommy.

Julius, in his turn, assumed the rôle of narrator. Beginning with his unsuccessful reconnoitring at Bournemouth, he passed on to his return to London, the buying of the car, the growing anxieties of Tuppence, the call upon Sir James, and the sensational occurrences of the previous night.

"But who killed her?" asked Tommy. "I don't quite understand."

"The doctor kidded himself she took it herself," replied Julius dryly.

"And Sir James? What did he think?"

"Being a legal luminary, he is likewise a human oyster," replied Julius. "I should say he 'reserved judgment.'" He went on to detail the events of the morning.

"Lost her memory, eh?" said Tommy with interest. "By Jove, that explains why they looked at me so queerly when I spoke of questioning her."

"That bluff of yours was the goods all right," Julius said. "How you ever came to think of it all so pat beats me to a frazzle!"

"I was in such a funk I had to think of something," said Tommy simply.

Tommy reverted to Mrs. Vandemeyer's death.

"There's no doubt it was chloral?"

"I believe not. But I guess Tuppence and I and even the highbrow Sir James have all got the same idea."

"Mr. Brown?" hazarded Tommy.

"Sure thing."

Tommy nodded.

"All the same," he said thoughtfully, "Mr. Brown hasn't got wings. I don't see how he got in and out."

"How about some high-class thought transference stunt?" Julius suggested. "Some magnetic influence that irresistibly impelled Mrs. Vandemeyer to commit suicide?"

Tommy looked at him with respect.

"Good, Julius. Distinctly good. Especially the phraseology. But it leaves me cold. I yearn for a real Mr. Brown of flesh and blood."

Inquiry at the office revealed the fact that Tuppence had not yet returned.

"All the same, I guess I'll have a look round upstairs," said Julius. "She might be in my sitting-room." He disappeared.

Suddenly a diminutive boy spoke at Tommy's elbow:

"The young lady--she's gone away by train, I think, sir," he murmured shyly.

"What?" Tommy wheeled round upon him.

"The taxi, sir. I heard her tell the driver Charing Cross and to look sharp."

Emboldened, the small boy proceeded. "So I thought, having asked for an A.B.C. and a Bradshaw."

"When did she ask for an A.B.C. and a Bradshaw?" Tommy interrupted.

"When I took her the telegram, sir. About half-past twelve."

"Tell me exactly what happened."

"I took up a telegram to No. 891--the lady was there. She opened it and gave a gasp, and then she said, very jolly like: 'Bring me up a Bradshaw, and an A.B.C., and look sharp, Henry.' My name isn't Henry, but----"

"Never mind your name," said Tommy impatiently. "Go on."

"Yes, sir. I brought them, and she looked up something. And then 'Hurry up,' she says. 'Tell them to get me a taxi,' and she begins a-shoving on of her hat in front of the glass, and I seed her going into the taxi."

At that moment Julius rejoined Tommy. He held an open letter in his hand.

"I say, Hersheimmer"--Tommy turned to him--"Tuppence has gone off sleuthing on her own. She went off in a taxi to Charing Cross after getting a telegram." His eye fell on the letter in Julius's hand. "Oh; she left a note for you. That's all right. Where's she off to?"

Almost unconsciously, he held out his hand for the letter, but Julius folded it up and placed it in his pocket. He seemed a trifle embarrassed.

"I guess this is nothing to do with it. It's about something else--something I asked her that she was to let me know about."

"Oh!" Tommy looked puzzled, and seemed waiting for more.

"See here," said Julius suddenly, "I'd better put you wise. I asked Miss Tuppence to marry me this morning."

"Oh!" said Tommy mechanically. He felt dazed.

"I'd like to tell you," continued Julius, "that before I suggested anything of the kind to Miss Tuppence, I made it clear that I didn't want to butt in in any way between her and you----"

Tommy roused himself.

"That's all right," he said quickly. "Tuppence and I have been pals for years. Nothing more." He lit a cigarette with a hand that shook ever so little. "Tuppence always said that she was looking out for----"

He stopped abruptly, his face crimsoning, but Julius was in no way discomposed.

"Oh, I guess it'll be the dollars that'll do the trick. Miss Tuppence put me wise to that right away. There's no humbug about her. We ought to gee along together very well."

Tuppence and Julius! Well, why not? Had she not openly avowed her intention of marrying for money if she ever had the chance? Why blame her because she had been true to her creed?

Nevertheless, Tommy did blame her. He was filled with a passionate and utterly illogical resentment. It was all very well to _say_ things like that--but a _real_ girl would never marry for money. Tuppence was utterly cold-blooded and selfish, and he would be delighted if he never saw her again!

Julius's voice broke in on these meditations.

"Yes, we ought to gee along together very well. I've heard that a girl always refuses you once--a sort of convention."

Tommy caught his arm.

"Refuses? Did you say _refuses?_"

"Sure thing. Didn't I tell you that? She just rapped out a 'no' without any kind of reason to it. But she'll come round right enough----"

"What did she say in that note?" Tommy demanded fiercely.

The obliging Julius handed it to him.

"There's no earthly clue in it as to where she's gone," he assured Tommy.

The note, in Tuppence's well-known schoolboy writing, ran as follows:

"DEAR JULIUS,

"It's always better to have things in black and white. I don't feel I can be bothered to think of marriage until Tommy is found. Let's leave it till then.

"Yours affectionately,

"TUPPENCE."

Tommy handed it back, his eyes shining. He now felt that Tuppence was all that was noble and disinterested. Had she not refused Julius without hesitation? Darling Tuppence, there was not a girl in the world to touch her! When he saw her----His thoughts were brought up with a sudden jerk.

"As you say," he remarked, pulling himself together, "there's not a hint here as to what she's up to. Hi--Henry!"

The small boy came obediently. Tommy produced five shillings.

"Do you remember what the young lady did with the telegram?"

"She crumpled it up into a ball and threw it into the grate, and made a sort of noise like 'Whoop!' sir," Henry said.

"Very graphic, Henry," said Tommy. "Here's your five shillings. Come on, Julius. We must find that telegram."

They hurried upstairs. In the fireplace was a crumpled ball of orange and white. Tommy disentangled it and smoothed out the telegram.

"Come at once, Moat House, Ebury, Yorkshire, great developments--TOMMY."

They looked at each other in stupefaction. Julius spoke first:

"You didn't send it?"

"Of course not. What does it mean?"

"I guess it means the worst," said Julius quietly. "They've got her."

_"What?"_

"Sure thing! They signed your name, and she fell into the trap like a lamb. But we've got to hustle. Where's that Bradshaw?"

After a few muttered imprecations Julius handed the Bradshaw to Tommy, who abandoned it in favour of an A.B.C.

"Here we are. Ebury, Yorks. From King's Cross. (Boy must have made a mistake. It was King's Cross, not _Charing_ Cross.) 12.50, that's the train she went by. 2.10, that's gone. 3.20 is the next--and a damned slow train too. The great thing is to keep calm."

Julius groaned.

"That's so. But it gets my goat to think of that innocent young girl in danger!"

Tommy nodded abstractedly. He was thinking.

"I say, Julius, what do they want her for, anyway? I don't think it's their game to do her any harm. She's a hostage, that's what she is. She's in no immediate danger, because if we tumble on to anything, she'd be damned useful to them. As long as they've got her, they've got the whip hand of us. See?"

"Sure thing," said Julius thoughtfully. "That's so."

"Besides," added Tommy, as an afterthought, "I've great faith in Tuppence."

The journey was wearisome. Ebury was a deserted station with a solitary porter, to whom Tommy addressed himself:

"Look here, do you remember a young lady who arrived by an earlier train, the 12.50 from London? She'd probably ask you the way to the Moat House."

The porter shook his head. Several people had arrived by the train in question. But he was quite certain that no one had asked him the way to the Moat House.

It was past seven o'clock when a small boy told them that "t' Moat House" was just past the next corner.

A rusty iron gate swinging dismally on its hinges! An overgrown drive thick with leaves. The house, too, seemed empty and deserted, the shutters closed, the steps overgrown with moss.

Julius jerked the rusty bell handle. They rang again and again--but there was no sign of life.

"Nothing doing," said Julius.

On the outskirts of a little hamlet, they met a workman, and Tommy stopped him with a question.

"The Moat House? It's empty. Been empty for years. Mrs. Sweeny's got the key if you want to go over it--next to the post office."

Mrs. Sweeny readily produced the key.

Tommy asked, "You've not had a young lady here asking for this key to-day?"

The woman shook her head.

"No one's been over the place for a long time."

They retraced their steps to the Moat House. As the front door swung back, Julius struck a match and examined the floor carefully.

"I'd swear no one's passed this way. Look at the dust. Thick. Not a sign of a footmark."

"This gets me," said Julius. "I don't believe Tuppence was ever in this house."

"She must have been," Tommy said. "We'll go over it again to-morrow."

On the morrow, as they were retracing their steps to the gate, Tommy gave a sudden cry, and stooping, picked something up from among the leaves. It was a small gold brooch.

"That's Tuppence's!"

"Are you sure?"

"Absolutely. I've often seen her wear it."

Julius drew a deep breath.

"I guess that settles it. She came as far as here, anyway. Somebody _must_ have seen her."

Forthwith the campaign began. Nobody answering to Tuppence's description had been seen in the vicinity.

Julius wired to town for his own car, and they scoured the neighbourhood daily. Every car that had passed through the village on the fateful day was tracked down; but, as day succeeded day, they were no nearer to discovering Tuppence's whereabouts.

"Do you know how long we've been here?" Tommy asked one morning at breakfast. "A week! We're no nearer to finding Tuppence, _and next Sunday is the_ 29_th!_"

"Shucks!" said Julius thoughtfully. "I'd almost forgotten about the 29th."

"To-day's the 23rd, and time's getting short," Tommy said. "If we're ever going to get hold of her at all, we must do it before the 29th--her life won't be worth an hour's purchase afterwards. The hostage game will be played out by then."

"I'm with you there. We've been a couple of mutts. I'm going right back to London to put the case in the hands of your British police. Scotland Yard for me! Are you coming along with me?"

Tommy shook his head.

"One of us is enough. I might as well stay here and nose round a bit longer. Something _might_ turn up."

"Sure thing. Well, so long. I'll be back in a couple of shakes with a few inspectors along."

Later in the day Tommy received a wire:

"Join me Manchester Midland Hotel. Important news--JULIUS."

At 7.30 that night Tommy alighted from a slow cross-country train. Julius was on the platform.

"What is it? Is Tuppence found?"

Julius shook his head.

"No. But I found this waiting in London. Just arrived."

He handed the telegraph form to the other. Tommy's eyes opened as he read:

"Jane Finn found. Come Manchester Midland Hotel immediately--PEEL EDGERTON."

Julius took the form back and folded it up.

"Queer," he said thoughtfully. "I thought that lawyer chap had quit!"

## CHAPTER XIX. JANE FINN

“MY train got in half an hour ago,” explained Julius. “Sir James has booked rooms for us, and will be round to dine at eight.”

“What made you think he’d ceased to take any interest in the case?” asked Tommy.

“What he said,” replied Julius dryly. “The old bird’s as close as an oyster!”

“I wonder,” said Tommy thoughtfully, “whether that was his real reason.”

Sir James arrived punctually at eight o’clock, and Julius introduced Tommy.

“I am delighted to make your acquaintance, Mr. Beresford. I have heard so much about you from Miss Tuppence”--he smiled involuntarily--“that it really seems as though I already know you quite well.”

“Thank you, sir,” said Tommy with his cheerful grin. Like Tuppence, he felt the magnetism of the other’s personality. He was reminded of Mr. Carter. Beneath the weary manner of the one and the professional reserve of the other, lay the same quality of mind, keen-edged like a rapier.

Sir James took in everything, but gave out only what he chose.

Julius broke out into a flood of eager questions. How had Sir James managed to track the girl?

Sir James stroked his chin and smiled. “Just so, just so. Well, she’s found. And that’s the great thing, isn’t it?”

“But where is she?” demanded Julius. “I thought you’d be sure to bring her along?”

“That would hardly be possible,” said Sir James gravely. “The young lady was knocked down in a street accident. She was taken to the infirmary, and on recovering consciousness gave her name as Jane Finn. When--ah!--I heard that, I arranged for her to be removed to the house of a doctor--a friend of mine, and wired at once for you. She relapsed into unconsciousness and has not spoken since.”

“Her memory’s come back?” cried Julius excitedly.

Sir James tapped the table impatiently. “Undoubtedly, Mr. Hersheimmer, since she was able to give her real name.”

“And you just happened to be on the spot,” said Tommy. “Seems quite like a fairy tale.”

But Sir James was far too wary to be drawn.

“Coincidences are curious things,” he said dryly.

Nevertheless Tommy was now certain of what he had before only suspected. Sir James’s presence in Manchester was not accidental. Far from abandoning the case, as Julius supposed, he had by some means of his own successfully run the missing girl to earth. The only thing that puzzled Tommy was the reason for all this secrecy. He concluded that it was a foible of the legal mind.

“After dinner,” Julius announced, “I shall go right away and see Jane.”

“That will be impossible, I fear,” said Sir James. “It is very unlikely they would allow her to see visitors at this time of night. I should suggest to-morrow morning about ten o’clock.”

Julius flushed. “All the same, I reckon I’ll go round there to-night and see if I can’t ginger them up to break through their silly rules.”

“It will be quite useless, Mr. Hersheimmer.”

The words came out like the crack of a pistol. For a moment the hostility between the two seemed likely to burst into flame, but in the end Julius lowered his eyes, defeated.

“For the moment, I reckon you’re the boss.”

“Thank you,” said the other. “We will say ten o’clock then?” With consummate ease of manner he turned to Tommy. “Miss Tuppence was inclined to think you had got into difficulties.”

Helped out by questions from Sir James, Tommy gave an abbreviated account of his adventures.

“You got yourself out of a tight place very well,” said the lawyer gravely. “I congratulate you.”

“I couldn’t have got away but for the girl, sir.”

“No.” Sir James smiled a little. “It was lucky for you she happened to--er--take a fancy to you. There’s no doubt about her being one of the gang, I suppose?”

“I’m afraid not, sir. You see, she went back to them when she could have got away.”

Sir James nodded thoughtfully. “What did she say? Something about wanting to be taken to Marguerite?”

“Yes, sir. I suppose she meant Mrs. Vandemeyer.”

“She always signed herself Rita Vandemeyer. All her friends spoke of her as Rita. And, at the moment she was crying out to her, Mrs. Vandemeyer was either dead or dying! Curious! There are one or two points that strike me as being obscure--their sudden change of attitude towards yourself, for instance. By the way, the house was raided, of course?”

“Yes, sir, but they’d all cleared out.”

“I wish you’d been there, sir, to go over the house!” Tommy said impulsively.

“I wish I had,” said Sir James quietly. “And since then? What have you been doing?”

“I forgot that you didn’t know about Tuppence,” Tommy said slowly.

The lawyer laid down his knife and fork sharply. “Has anything happened to Miss Tuppence?”

“She’s disappeared,” said Julius.

“When?”

“A week ago.”

Sir James’s questions fairly shot out. Between them Tommy and Julius gave the history of the last week and their futile search.

“A wire signed with your name? They knew enough of you both for that,” said Sir James. “Their kidnapping of Miss Tuppence is the counter-move to your escape.”

“That’s just what I thought, sir,” said Tommy.

“You had worked that out, had you? The curious thing is that they certainly did not know anything about you when they first held you prisoner.”

“That’s so,” said Julius with a nod. “Therefore I reckon some one put them wise--and not earlier than Sunday afternoon.”

“Yes, but who?”

“That almighty omniscient Mr. Brown, of course! I reckon it out that he’s a figurehead--just a bogy name to frighten the children with. The real head of this business is that Russian chap Kramenin.”

“I disagree with you,” said Sir James shortly. “Mr. Brown exists.” He turned to Tommy. “Did you happen to notice where that wire was handed in?”

“No, sir, I’m afraid I didn’t. It’s upstairs, sir, in my kit.”

“I’d like to have a look at it sometime. No hurry. You’ve wasted a week”--Tommy hung his head--“a day or so more is immaterial. I don’t think Miss Tuppence is in any immediate danger. That is, so long as they don’t know that we’ve got Jane Finn, and that her memory has returned. We must keep that dark at all costs.”

At ten o’clock the next morning, Sir James joined the two young men on the doctor’s doorstep.

“Mr. Hersheimmer--Mr. Beresford--Dr. Roylance. How’s the patient?”

“Going on well,” said the doctor. “Asked this morning how many had been saved from the _Lusitania_.”

Tommy’s heart beat sensibly faster as they followed the doctor upstairs. Jane Finn at last! If only Tuppence could have been at his side to share in the triumphant conclusion of their joint venture! And suddenly a dread clutched at his heart. It seemed too easy.... Suppose they should find her dead ... stricken down by the hand of Mr. Brown?

In another minute he was laughing at these melodramatic fancies. On the white bed, bandages round her head, lay the girl. It was so exactly what one expected that it gave the effect of being beautifully staged.

“Miss Finn,” said Sir James, “this is your cousin, Mr. Julius P. Hersheimmer.”

“How do, Cousin Jane?” said Julius lightly. But Tommy caught the tremor in his voice.

“Are you really Uncle Hiram’s son?” she asked wonderingly.

Her voice, with the slight warmth of the Western accent, had an almost thrilling quality. It seemed vaguely familiar to Tommy, but he thrust the impression aside as impossible.

“Sure thing.”

“Mother figured it out that Uncle Hiram would never get over being mad with her.”

A shadow passed over the girl’s face. “They’ve been telling me things--dreadful things--that my memory went, and that there are years I shall never know about--years lost out of my life.”

“Don’t worry any,” said Julius. “Now, see here, Jane, there was a man aboard that boat with some mighty important papers on him.” As her glance shifted to the others, he added: “Mr. Beresford is commissioned by the British Government to get those papers back. Did Danvers give you the papers?”

“Yes. He said they’d have a better chance with me, because they would save the women and children first.”

“Then you can hand them over to us right away?” said Julius.

“But I can’t. I haven’t got them.”

“You--haven’t--got them?”

“No--I hid them. I got uneasy. People seemed to be watching me.”

“Go on,” said Sir James, in his quiet penetrating tones. “What do you remember?”

“It was at Holyhead. In the confusion on the quay I slipped away. I took a car. I saw a path at the side of the road. I told the man to wait.

“The path led to the cliff, and down to the sea between big yellow gorse bushes--they were like golden flames. But just level with my head there was a hole in the rock. It was quite small--I could only just get my hand in. I took the oilskin packet from round my neck and shoved it right in as far as I could. Then I tore off a bit of gorse--My! but it did prick--and plugged the hole with it so that you’d never guess there was a crevice of any kind there. There was a queer boulder in the path just there--for all the world like a dog sitting up begging. Then I went back to the road and just caught the train. By and by, I saw the man opposite me wink at a woman who was sitting next to me, and I felt scared again, and was glad the papers were safe. I went out in the corridor. But the woman called me back, said I’d dropped something, and when I stooped to look, something seemed to hit me--here.” She placed her hand to the back of her head.

Julius stepped forward and took her hand.

“So long, Cousin Jane. I’m going to get busy after those papers, but I’ll be back in two shakes of a dog’s tail, and I’ll tote you up to London and give you the time of your young life before we go back to the States!”

## CHAPTER XX. TOO LATE

SIR James had drawn a watch from his pocket. “The boat train to Holyhead stops at Chester at 12.14. If you start at once I think you can catch the connection.”

Tommy looked up, puzzled.

“Is there any need to hurry, sir? To-day is only the 24th.”

“We’ll make tracks for the depot right away,” said Julius, before the lawyer had time to reply.

“I wish I could come with you. I am due to speak at a meeting at two o’clock. It is unfortunate.”

It was clear that Julius was easily disposed to put up with the loss of the other’s company.

“Just a game of hide-and-seek, that’s all,” he remarked.

“You are still young, Mr. Hersheimmer,” said Sir James. “At my age you will probably have learnt one lesson. ‘Never underestimate your adversary.’”

Julius slapped his pocket. “I carry a gun. Little Willie here travels round with me everywhere.” He produced a murderous-looking automatic, and tapped it affectionately before returning it to its home. “But he won’t be needed this trip. There’s nobody to put Mr. Brown wise.”

The lawyer shrugged his shoulders.

“There was nobody to put Mr. Brown wise to the fact that Mrs. Vandemeyer meant to betray him. Nevertheless, _Mrs. Vandemeyer died without speaking_.”

Julius was silenced for once, and Sir James added on a lighter note:

“I only want to put you on your guard. If there is any reason to believe that you have been shadowed, destroy the papers at once.”

Ten minutes later the two young men were seated in a first-class carriage _en route_ for Chester.

“Say,” observed Julius thoughtfully, “did you ever make a darned fool of yourself over a girl’s face?”

“Can’t say I have. Why?”

“Because for the last two months I’ve been making a sentimental idiot of myself over Jane! First moment I clapped eyes on her photograph my heart did all the usual stunts you read about in novels. I came over here determined to find her and take her back as Mrs. Julius P. Hersheimmer! One look at the girl in the flesh, and I was cured!”

“I thought her a very good-looking girl,” said Tommy, finding his tongue.

“Sure she is. But she’s not like her photo one bit. At least I suppose she is in a way--must be--because I recognized her right off. But there was something about that photo”--Julius shook his head, and heaved a sigh--“I guess romance is a mighty queer thing!”

“It must be,” said Tommy coldly, “if you can come over here in love with one girl, and propose to another within a fortnight.”

Julius had the grace to look discomposed.

“Well, you see, I’d got a sort of tired feeling that I’d never find Jane--and that it was all plumb foolishness anyway. And then--oh, well, the French set about marriage in a businesslike way.”

“If you ask me,” said Tommy, “we’re all too damned businesslike nowadays. We’re always saying, ‘Will it pay?’ The men are bad enough, and the girls are worse!”

“Cool down, son. Don’t get so heated,” said Julius.

“I feel heated,” said Tommy.

However, Tommy had plenty of time to cool down before they reached Holyhead.

A taxi took them out on the road to Treaddur Bay, and at the path they paid off the man.

“It’s the right one, I suppose?” asked Tommy doubtfully.

“Sure it is. Look at the gorse. Remember what Jane said?”

Tommy looked at the swelling hedges of golden blossom which bordered the path on either side, and was convinced.

They went down in single file, Julius leading. Twice Tommy turned his head uneasily. Julius looked back.

“What is it?”

“I don’t know. I’ve got the wind up somehow. Keep fancying there’s some one following us.”

“Can’t be,” said Julius positively. “We’d see him.”

Nevertheless, Tommy’s sense of uneasiness deepened. In spite of himself he believed in the omniscience of the enemy.

Suddenly Julius came to such an abrupt halt that Tommy cannoned into him.

“Look there. If that doesn’t beat the band!”

Tommy looked. Standing out half obstructing the path was a huge boulder which certainly bore a fanciful resemblance to a “begging” terrier.

“Well,” said Tommy, refusing to share Julius’s emotion, “it’s what we expected to see, isn’t it?”

“British phlegm! Sure we expected it--but it kind of rattles me, all the same, to see it sitting there just where we expected to find it!”

“Push on. What about the hole?”

Tommy suddenly pointed with a shaking hand.

“What about that crevice there?”

Julius replied in an awestricken voice: “That’s it--for sure.”

“Damn it!” Tommy cried. “It’s impossible! Five years! Think of it! It’s a hundred to one against its being there! It’s against all reason!”

Julius looked at him with a widening smile.

“I guess you’re rattled now all right,” he drawled. “Well, here goes!” He thrust his hand into the crevice. “I don’t feel anything--no--say, what’s this? Gee whiz!” And with a flourish he waved aloft a small discoloured packet. “It’s the goods all right. Sewn up in oilskin.”

The unbelievable had happened. Tommy held the precious packet tenderly between his hands.

“It’s queer,” he murmured idly, “you’d think the stitches would have rotted. They look just as good as new.”

They cut them carefully and ripped away the oilskin. Inside was a small folded sheet of paper. The sheet was blank!

“A dummy?” hazarded Julius. “Was Danvers just a decoy?”

Suddenly Tommy’s face cleared.

“I’ve got it! _Sympathetic ink!_ Heat usually does the trick. Get some sticks. We’ll make a fire.”

In a few minutes the little fire of twigs and leaves was blazing merrily. Tommy held the sheet of paper near the glow. Suddenly Julius grasped his arm, and pointed to where characters were appearing in a faint brown colour.

Tommy held the paper in position some minutes longer, then withdrew it. A moment later he uttered a cry.

Across the sheet in neat brown printing ran the words: WITH THE COMPLIMENTS OF MR. BROWN.

## CHAPTER XXI. TOMMY MAKES A DISCOVERY

FOR a moment or two they stood staring at each other stupidly, dazed with the shock. Somehow, inexplicably, Mr. Brown had forestalled them.

“How in tarnation did he get ahead of us? That’s what beats me!”

Tommy shook his head, and said dully:

“It accounts for the stitches being new. We might have guessed....”

“Never mind the darned stitches. How did he know? Do you reckon there was a dictaphone in Jane’s room?”

But Tommy’s common sense pointed out objections.

“No one could have known beforehand that she was going to be in that house--much less that particular room.”

“I wish that chap Peel Edgerton had been with us,” said Tommy thoughtfully.

“Why?” Julius stared. “The mischief was done when we came.”

“Yes----” Tommy hesitated. He could not explain his own feeling--the illogical idea that the K.C.’s presence would somehow have averted the catastrophe. “The game’s up. I must get back to London. Mr. Carter must be warned.”

He took the midnight mail to London. Julius elected to stay the night at Holyhead.

Tommy stood before his chief.

“I’ve come to report, sir. I’ve failed--failed badly.”

“Ah!” said Mr. Carter quietly. The expression on his face did not change, but Tommy caught the flicker of despair in his eyes.

Through Tommy’s mind flashed the assurance: “It’s hopeless, and he knows it’s hopeless!”

“Don’t take it to heart, lad,” Mr. Carter said kindly. “You did your best. And you came very near success. I blame myself. I have been blaming myself ever since I heard this other news.”

A new fear gripped at Tommy’s heart.

“Tuppence----?” faltered Tommy.

“Read for yourself.”

The typewritten words danced before his eyes. The description of a green toque, a coat with a handkerchief in the pocket marked P.L.C.

“Washed up on the Yorkshire coast--near Ebury. I’m afraid--it looks very much like foul play.”

“My God!” gasped Tommy. “_Tuppence!_ Those devils--I’ll never rest till I’ve got even with them! I’ll----”

The pity on Mr. Carter’s face stopped him.

“I know what you feel like, my poor boy. But it’s no good. Cut your losses. Time’s merciful. You’ll forget.”

“Forget Tuppence? Never!”

“There’s no need for you to blame yourself, sir,” Tommy said with an effort. “But I wish to God I’d been the one to get it in the neck. Good-bye, sir.”

Back at the _Ritz_, Tommy packed up his few belongings mechanically. _Tuppence--dead!_

They brought him a note, a few kind words of sympathy from Peel Edgerton, who had read the news in the paper. (There had been a large headline: EX-V.A.D. FEARED DROWNED.) The letter ended with the offer of a post on a ranch in the Argentine.

The door opened, and Julius burst in with an open newspaper in his hand.

“Say, what’s all this? They seem to have got some fool idea about Tuppence.”

“It’s true,” said Tommy quietly.

“Well, I’m darned!” said Julius. “Little Tuppence. She sure was the pluckiest little girl----”

But suddenly something seemed to crack in Tommy’s brain.

“Oh, get out! You don’t really care, damn you! You asked her to marry you in your rotten cold-blooded way, but I _loved_ her. I’d have given the soul out of my body to save her from harm.”

“See here,” began Julius temperately.

“Oh, go to the devil! Go and look after your cousin. Tuppence is my girl! I shall never forget when I was in hospital, and she came in in that ridiculous cap and apron! It was like a miracle to see the girl I loved turn up in a nurse’s kit----”

But Julius interrupted him.

“A nurse’s kit! Gee whiz! I could swear I’ve seen Jane in a nurse’s cap too. No, by gum, I’ve got it! It was her I saw talking to Whittington at that nursing home in Bournemouth. She wasn’t a patient there! She was a nurse!”

“I dare say,” said Tommy angrily, “she’s probably been in with them from the start. I shouldn’t wonder if she stole those papers from Danvers to begin with.”

“I’m darned if she did!” shouted Julius. “She’s my cousin, and as patriotic a girl as ever stepped.”

The young men were on the point of coming to blows. But suddenly, with an almost magical abruptness, Julius’s anger abated.

“All right, son,” he said quietly, “I’m going. I don’t blame you any for what you’ve been saying. It’s mighty lucky you did say it. I’ve been the most almighty blithering darned idiot that it’s possible to imagine. I’m going right away now--going to the London and North Western Railway depot, if you want to know.”

When the door closed, Tommy finished packing and rang for his luggage to be taken down.

“Going away, sir?”

“I’m going to the devil,” said Tommy.

Beyond a fixed determination to get even with Mr. Brown he had no plans. Still, Sir James’s letter had been kind; better answer it. Finding no paper in his room, he remembered the good supply in Julius’s sitting-room. He was beginning to be rather ashamed of the things he had said. He’d apologize if he found him there.

But the room was deserted. Tommy opened the middle drawer of the writing-table. A photograph, carelessly thrust in face upwards, caught his eye. For a moment he stood rooted to the ground.

What on earth was a photograph of the French girl Annette doing in Julius Hersheimmer’s writing-table?

## CHAPTER XXII. IN DOWNING STREET

THE Prime Minister tapped the desk with nervous fingers. His face was worn and harassed. "I don't understand," he said. "Do you really mean that things are not so desperate after all?"

"So this lad seems to think."

Mr. Carter handed over the letter.

"DEAR MR. CARTER,

"Something's turned up that has given me a jar. If my conclusions are right, that girl at Manchester was just a plant. The whole thing was prearranged, sham packet and all, with the object of making us think the game was up.

"I think I know who the real Jane Finn is, and I've even got an idea where the papers are. Anyhow, I enclose it in a sealed envelope for what it's worth. I'm going to ask you not to open it until the very last moment, midnight on the 28th. You see, I've figured it out that those things of Tuppence's are a plant too, and she's no more drowned than I am. The way I reason is this: as a last chance they'll let Jane Finn escape in the hope that she's been shamming this memory stunt, and that once she thinks she's free she'll go right away to the cache. _But if they know that the papers have been recovered by us_, neither of those two girls' lives will be worth an hour's purchase. I must try and get hold of Tuppence before Jane escapes.

"I want a repeat of that telegram that was sent to Tuppence at the _Ritz_. Sir James Peel Edgerton said you would be able to manage that for me. He's frightfully clever.

"One last thing--please have that house in Soho watched day and night.

"Yours, etc.,

"THOMAS BERESFORD."

"The enclosure?"

"In the vaults of the Bank. I am taking no chances."

"You don't think it would be better to open it now?"

"Once it's known I wouldn't give that"--he snapped his fingers--"for the life of those two girls. No, the boy trusted me, and I shan't let him down."

"What's he like, this lad?"

"Outwardly, he's an ordinary clean-limbed, rather block-headed young Englishman. Slow in his mental processes. On the other hand, it's quite impossible to lead him astray through his imagination. He hasn't got any--so he's difficult to deceive. Once he's got hold of anything he doesn't let go. The little lady's quite different. More intuition and less common sense. Pace and stamina."

"And it is this--boy who will defeat the master criminal of our time?"

"This--boy, as you say! But I sometimes fancy I see a shadow behind."

"You mean?"

"Peel Edgerton. I see his hand in _this_." Carter struck the open letter. "He's there--working in the dark, silently, unobtrusively. By the way, he sent me a cutting from some American paper. It referred to a man's body found near the docks in New York about three weeks ago. He asked me to collect any information I could. Young fellow about thirty-five--poorly dressed--face very badly disfigured. He was never identified. Somehow I fancy the two matters are connected. I may be wrong, of course. I asked him to come round here. Ah, here he is!"

The two men rose to greet the new-comer.

"We've had a letter from young Beresford," said Mr. Carter. "You've seen him, I suppose?"

"You suppose wrong," said the lawyer. "He rang me up. He said that in a drawer in Mr. Hersheimmer's room he had discovered a photograph. I asked him if the photograph bore the name and address of a Californian photographer. He replied: 'You're on to it, sir. It had.' Then he told me something I _didn't_ know. The original of that photograph was the French girl, Annette, who saved his life."

"What?"

"Exactly. He had put it back where he found it. I advised him to apply to you for a copy of the original wire. It had occurred to me as probable that certain words might have been erased and altered with the express intention of setting searchers on a false trail."

Carter took a sheet from his pocket, and read aloud:

"Come at once, Astley Priors, Gatehouse, Kent. Great developments--TOMMY."

"Very simple," said Sir James. "And the one important clue they overlooked."

"What was that?"

"The page-boy's statement that Miss Cowley drove to Charing Cross."

"Then young Beresford is now?"

"At Gatehouse, Kent, unless I am much mistaken."

Mr. Carter looked at him curiously. "I rather wonder you're not there too, Peel Edgerton?"

"Ah, I'm preparing a case. Any more facts about that American chap for me?"

"I'm afraid not. Is it important to find out who he was?"

"Oh, I know who he was," said Sir James easily. "I can't prove it yet--but I know."

"But what I don't understand," said the Prime Minister suddenly, "is how that photograph came to be in Mr. Hersheimmer's drawer?"

"Perhaps it never left it," suggested the lawyer gently.

"But the bogus inspector? Inspector Brown?"

"Ah!" said Sir James thoughtfully. "I must get back to--my case."

Two days later Julius Hersheimmer returned from Manchester. A note from Tommy lay on his table:

"DEAR HERSHEIMMER,

"Sorry I lost my temper. In case I don't see you again, good-bye. I've been offered a job in the Argentine, and might as well take it.

"Yours,

"TOMMY BERESFORD."

A peculiar smile lingered for a moment on Julius's face. He threw the letter into the waste-paper basket.

"The darned fool!" he murmured.

## CHAPTER XXIII. A RACE AGAINST TIME

AFTER ringing up Sir James, Tommy's next procedure was to make a call at South Audley Mansions. He found Albert discharging his professional duties, and introduced himself as a friend of Tuppence's. Albert unbent immediately.

"Hope the young lady's keeping well, sir?"

"That's just the point, Albert. She's disappeared."

"You don't mean as the crooks have got her?"

"They have."

"In the Underworld?"

"No, dash it all, in this world!"

"It's a h'expression, sir," explained Albert. "At the pictures the crooks always have a restoorant in the Underworld. But do you think as they've done her in, sir?"

"I hope not," said Tommy. "By the way, have you by any chance an aunt, a cousin, a grandmother, or any other suitable female relation who might be represented as being likely to kick the bucket?"

"I'm on, sir. My poor aunt what lives in the country has been mortal bad for a long time, and she's asking for me with her dying breath."

"Can you report this in the proper quarter and meet me at Charing Cross in an hour's time?"

"I'll be there, sir," said Albert.

The two took up their quarters at the inn in Gatehouse.

Astley Priors was the property of a Dr. Adams. The doctor no longer practiced, but he took a few private patients. Here the landlord tapped his forehead knowingly. "Balmy ones! You understand!" The doctor was a popular figure in the village. "A very pleasant, affable gentleman."

Tommy felt doubts. Suppose it was all a gigantic mistake? Tommy felt a cold chill at the thought. He inquired carefully if there was a young lady amongst the patients, describing Tuppence. But the patients were seldom seen outside the grounds. A guarded description of Annette also failed to provoke recognition.

On the first evening Tommy, accompanied by Albert, explored the well-wooded grounds. Owing to Albert's insistence they dragged themselves along painfully on their stomachs, thereby producing a great deal more noise than if they had stood upright. But they reached a shrubbery near the house quite unmolested.

There was a large company assembled round the table. Through the open window scraps of conversation floated out. It was a heated discussion on county cricket!

Again Tommy felt that cold chill of uncertainty.

The indefatigable Albert, having cemented an alliance with the greengrocer's boy, took the latter's place, and on the second day brought back the first piece of hopeful news. There _was_ a French young lady staying in the house.

Tommy put his doubts aside. But time pressed. To-day was the 27th. The 29th was the much-talked-of "Labour Day."

With the fatal document in the hands of Mr. Brown, public opinion would swing to the side of the Labour extremists and revolutionists. But Tommy nourished another and a preposterous dream. With Mr. Brown unmasked and captured he believed, rightly or wrongly, that the whole organization would crumble ignominiously and instantaneously. Without him, Tommy believed an instant panic would set in; and, the honest men left to themselves, an eleventh-hour reconciliation would be possible.

"This is a one-man show," said Tommy to himself. "The thing to do is to get hold of the man."

It was partly in furtherance of this ambitious design that he had requested Mr. Carter not to open the sealed envelope. The draft treaty was Tommy's bait. Every now and then he was aghast at his own presumption. Nevertheless, he stuck tenaciously to his idea.

That evening he and Albert once more penetrated the grounds of Astley Priors. As they approached cautiously, Tommy gave a sudden gasp.

On the second floor window some one standing between the window and the light in the room threw a silhouette on the blind. It was one Tommy would have recognized anywhere! Tuppence was in that house!

He clutched Albert by the shoulder.

"Stay here! When I begin to sing, watch that window."

He retreated hastily to a position on the main drive, and began in a deep roar, coupled with an unsteady gait, the following ditty:

          I am a Soldier
          A jolly British Soldier;
          You can see that I'm a Soldier by my feet....

It had been a favourite on the gramophone in Tuppence's hospital days.

Presently an unimpeachable butler, accompanied by an equally unimpeachable footman, issued from the front door. Tommy continued to sing, addressing the butler affectionately as "dear old whiskers." They ran him down the drive, and neatly out of the gate. Anyone would have sworn that the butler was a real butler, the footman a real footman--only, as it happened, the butler was Whittington!

Tommy retired to the inn and waited for Albert's return.

"Well?" cried Tommy eagerly.

"It's all right. While they was a-running of you out the window opened, and something was chucked out," said Albert. He handed over a scrap of paper. On it were scrawled three words: "To-morrow--same time."

"Good egg!" cried Tommy. "We're getting going."

"I wrote a message, wrapped it round a stone, and chucked it through the window," continued Albert breathlessly. "Said we was a-staying at the inn. If she could get away, to come there and croak like a frog."

Tommy groaned.

"Cheer up," said Tommy. "No harm done. That butler's an old friend of mine--I bet he knew who I was, though he didn't let on. I'm a pawn in their game, Albert, that's what I am. You see, if the spider lets the fly walk out too easily, the fly might suspect it was a put-up job."

Tommy retired for the night in a state of some elation.

About twelve o'clock, however, Tommy's calm was rudely shaken. A rude-looking carter was demanding him in the bar.

"Might this be for you, sir?" The carter held out a folded note, on the outside of which was written: "Take this to the gentleman at the inn near Astley Priors. He will give you ten shillings."

The handwriting was Tuppence's. Tommy unfastened it.

"DEAR TOMMY,

"I knew it was you last night. Don't go this evening. They'll be lying in wait for you. They're taking us away this morning. I heard something about Wales--Holyhead, I think. I'll drop this on the road if I get a chance. Annette told me how you'd escaped. Buck up.

"Yours,

"TWOPENCE."

Tommy raised a shout for Albert before he had even finished perusing this characteristic epistle.

"Pack my bag! We're off!"

"Yes, sir." The boots of Albert could be heard racing upstairs. Holyhead? Did that mean that, after all---- Tommy was puzzled. He read on slowly.

Suddenly a second shout came from below.

"Albert! I'm a damned fool! Unpack that bag!"

"Yes, sir."

Tommy smoothed out the note thoughtfully.

"Yes, a damned fool," he said softly. "But so's some one else! And at last I know who it is!"

## CHAPTER XXIV. JULIUS TAKES A HAND

IN his suite at Claridge’s, Kramenin reclined on a couch and dictated to his secretary in sibilant Russian.

The telephone at the secretary’s elbow purred, and he took up the receiver, then turned to his employer.

“Some one below is asking for you. He gives the name of Mr. Julius P. Hersheimmer.”

“His father was one of the steel kings of America,” explained the secretary, whose business it was to know everything. “This young man must be a millionaire several times over.”

The other’s eyes narrowed appreciatively.

“A millionaire several times over,” murmured Kramenin. “Bring him up, my dear Ivan.”

The secretary left the room, and returned escorting Julius.

“Monsieur Kramenin?” said the latter abruptly.

The Russian, studying him with his pale venomous eyes, bowed.

“I’ve got some very important business I’d like to talk over with you, if I can see you alone,” said the American. He looked pointedly at the other.

“Ivan,” said the Russian softly, “perhaps you would not mind retiring into the next room----”

“The next room won’t do,” interrupted Julius. “I want this one plumb empty except for you and me.”

“Very good, Ivan. I shall not require you again this evening. Go to the theatre--take a night off.”

The secretary bowed and departed.

“Now, Mr. Hersheimmer, perhaps you will be so kind as to come to the point?”

“I guess that won’t take a minute,” drawled Julius. Then, with an abrupt change of manner: “Hands up--or I shoot!”

For a moment Kramenin stared blindly into the big automatic, then, with almost comical haste, he flung up his hands above his head. In that instant Julius had taken his measure. The man he had to deal with was an abject physical coward--the rest would be easy.

“This is an outrage,” cried the Russian in a high hysterical voice. “Do you mean to kill me?”

“Not if you keep your voice down.”

“What do you want? Money?”

“No. I want Jane Finn.”

“Jane Finn? I--never heard of her!”

“You’re a darned liar! You know perfectly who I mean.”

“I tell you I’ve never heard of the girl.”

“And I tell you,” retorted Julius, “that Little Willie here is just hopping mad to go off!”

Kramenin must have recognized something in the voice that carried conviction, for he said sullenly:

“Well? Granted I do know who you mean--what of it?”

“You will tell me now--right here--where she is to be found.”

“I daren’t. You ask an impossibility.”

“Afraid, eh? Of whom? Mr. Brown? Ah, that tickles you up! And the mere mention of him scares you stiff!”

“I have seen him,” said the Russian slowly. “Spoken to him face to face. I did not know it until afterwards. He was one of a crowd. I should not know him again. Who is he really? I do not know. But I know this--he is a man to fear.”

“Sure that’s a pity for you,” said Julius cheerfully. “But the world in general will benefit.” He raised the revolver.

“Stop,” shrieked the Russian. “You will hang if you shoot me!”

“No, stranger, that’s where you’re wrong. You forget the dollars. A big crowd of solicitors will get busy, and they’ll get some high-brow doctors on the job, and the end of it all will be that they’ll say my brain was unhinged. But don’t you kid yourself I’ll hang for it!”

The Russian believed him. Corrupt himself, he believed implicitly in the power of money. This virile young American had the whip hand of him.

“I’m going to count five,” continued Julius, “and I guess, if you let me get past four, you needn’t worry any about Mr. Brown. Maybe he’ll send some flowers to the funeral, but _you_ won’t smell them! One--two--three--four----”

The Russian interrupted with a shriek:

“Do not shoot. I will do all you wish.”

Julius lowered the revolver.

“I thought you’d hear sense. Where is the girl?”

“At Gatehouse, in Kent. Astley Priors, the place is called.”

“Is she a prisoner there?”

“She’s not allowed to leave the house--though it’s safe enough really. The little fool has lost her memory, curse her!”

“What about the other girl, the one you decoyed away over a week ago?”

“She’s there too,” said the Russian sullenly.

“That’s good,” said Julius. “And a lovely night for the run! Down to Gatehouse, sure.”

“I refuse to go.”

“You must see I’m not such a kid as to leave you here. You’d ring up your friends on that telephone first thing! No, sir, you’re coming along with me.”

They descended the stairs and passed out to the waiting car. A cry hovered on the Russian’s lips, but at the last minute his nerve failed him.

“Get in,” Julius ordered. Then, as he caught the other’s sidelong glance, “No, the chauffeur won’t help you any. Naval man. Was on a submarine in Russia when the Revolution broke out. A brother of his was murdered by your people. George! This gentleman is a Russian Bolshevik. We don’t want to shoot him, but it may be necessary. You understand?”

“Perfectly, sir.”

The car shot forward through the traffic. Julius, his hand in his coat pocket, was urbane to the last degree, and told one cheerful tale of shooting after another. At the end of the hour’s run the unfortunate Kramenin was more dead than alive. Julius’s narrative style, if not strictly accurate, was picturesque! By the time they came into Gatehouse he was as putty in the other’s hands.

The car swept up the drive. The front door was opened by the butler. Kramenin felt the muzzle of the revolver pressed against his ribs.

“Now,” hissed Julius. “And be careful.”

“It is I--Kramenin! Bring down the girl at once! There is no time to lose!”

Whittington had come down the steps. He uttered an exclamation of astonishment.

“You! What’s up? Surely you know the plan----”

Kramenin interrupted him, using the words that have created many unnecessary panics:

“We have been betrayed! Plans must be abandoned. The girl! And at once! It’s our only chance.”

Whittington hesitated, but for hardly a moment.

“You have orders--from _him?_”

“Naturally! Should I be here otherwise? Hurry! The other little fool had better come too.”

Whittington ran back into the house. The agonizing minutes went by. Then--two figures hastily huddled in cloaks were hustled into the car. Julius leaned forward, and the light from the open door lit up his face. Another man on the steps behind Whittington gave a startled exclamation. Concealment was at an end.

“Get a move on, George,” shouted Julius.

The car started with a bound. The man on the steps uttered an oath. There was a flash and a report. The bullet just missed the taller girl by an inch.

“Get down, Jane,” cried Julius. “Flat on the bottom of the car.” Then, standing up, he took careful aim and fired.

“Have you hit him?” cried Tuppence eagerly.

“Sure,” replied Julius. “He isn’t killed, though. Are you all right, Tuppence?”

“Of course I am. Where’s Tommy? And who’s this?” She indicated the shivering Kramenin.

“Tommy’s making tracks for the Argentine. I guess he thought you’d turned up your toes. Let me present Monsieur Kramenin. I persuaded him to come on the trip for his health.”

“Curse you!” burst out the Russian. “They know now that I betrayed them. Let me go. I have done what you asked.”

“Sure thing. Pull up, George.”

Before the car had finally halted, the Russian had swung himself out and disappeared into the night.

“Say, Jane, you can get up on the seat now,” said Julius.

For the first time the girl spoke.

“How did you ‘persuade’ him?” she asked.

Julius tapped his revolver.

“Little Willie here takes the credit!”

“Annette and I didn’t know what was going to happen to us,” said Tuppence.

“Annette,” said Julius. “Is that what you call her?”

“It’s her name,” said Tuppence, opening her eyes very wide.

“Shucks!” retorted Julius. “She may think it’s her name, because her memory’s gone, poor kid. But it’s the one real and original Jane Finn we’ve got here.”

“What?” cried Tuppence.

A bullet embedded itself in the upholstery just behind her head.

“Down with you,” cried Julius. “It’s an ambush. Push her a bit, George.”

Three more shots rang out. Julius raised his hand to his cheek.

“You are hurt?” said Annette quickly.

“Only a scratch.”

The girl sprang to her feet.

“Let me out! It is me they’re after. You shall not lose your lives because of me.”

Julius took her by both arms. She had spoken with no trace of foreign accent.

“Sit down, kid,” he said gently. “I guess there’s nothing wrong with your memory. Been fooling them all the time, eh?”

The girl nodded, and then suddenly burst into tears.

“You’re from home,” she said indistinctly. “I can tell by your voice.”

“Sure I’m from home. I’m your cousin--Julius Hersheimmer. I came over to Europe on purpose to find you.”

The car slowed at cross-roads till it hardly moved. As it did so a figure climbed suddenly over the back, and plunged head first into the midst of them.

“Sorry,” said Tommy, extricating himself. “Was in the bushes by the drive. Hung on behind. Now then, you girls, get out! There’s a station just up that road. Train due in three minutes.”

“What the devil are you driving at?” demanded Julius.

“You and I aren’t going to leave the car. Only the girls.”

Tommy turned to Tuppence.

“Get out at once, Tuppence. Take her with you, and do just as I say. You’re safe. Take the train to London. Go straight to Sir James Peel Edgerton.”

“Darn you!” cried Julius. “Jane, you stay where you are.”

With a sudden swift movement, Tommy snatched the revolver from Julius’s hand, and levelled it at him.

“Now will you believe I’m in earnest? Get out, both of you--or I’ll shoot!”

Tuppence sprang out, dragging the unwilling Jane after her.

“Come on, it’s all right. If Tommy’s sure--he’s sure. Be quick.”

They started running. Julius’s pent-up rage burst forth.

“What the hell----”

Tommy interrupted him.

“Dry up! I want a few words with you, Mr. Julius Hersheimmer.”

## CHAPTER XXV. JANE’S STORY

HER arm through Jane’s, dragging her along, Tuppence reached the station. Her quick ears caught the sound of the approaching train.

They arrived on the platform just as the train came to a standstill. Tuppence opened the door of an empty first-class compartment, and the two girls sank down breathless on the padded seats.

Tuppence took Jane’s hand in hers. “Tommy wouldn’t have told us to do this unless he was sure we’d be all right.”

“But he doesn’t know them as I do!” The girl shivered. “You can’t understand. Five years! Five long years! Sometimes I thought I should go mad.”

“Never mind. It’s all over.”

“Is it?”

The train was moving now, speeding through the night.

“My cousin didn’t think it was safe. He didn’t want us to do this.”

“No,” said Tuppence, rather embarrassed.

“What are you thinking of?” said Jane sharply. “Your voice was so--queer!”

“I _was_ thinking of something,” confessed Tuppence. “But I don’t want to tell you--not now. I may be wrong, but I don’t think so. It’s just an idea that came into my head a long time ago. Tommy’s got it too--I’m almost sure he has. Lie back and don’t think of anything.”

“I’ll try.” The long lashes drooped over the hazel eyes.

Tuppence, for her part, sat bolt upright--much in the attitude of a watchful terrier on guard. In her own mind she was far from feeling the confidence displayed in her words. Not that she disbelieved in Tommy, but occasionally she was shaken with doubts as to whether anyone so simple and honest as he was could ever be a match for the fiendish subtlety of the arch-criminal.

Tuppence mapped out her plan of campaign.

As the train drew into Charing Cross, Jane sat up with a start.

“Have we arrived? I never thought we should!”

“If there’s going to be any fun, now is when it will begin. Quick, get out. We’ll nip into a taxi.”

“King’s Cross,” directed Tuppence.

“You see,” she explained to Jane, “if they think we’re going to Sir James, this will put them off the scent. Now they’ll imagine we’re going to Mr. Carter.”

Crossing Holborn there was a block, and the taxi was held up. This was what Tuppence had been waiting for.

“Quick,” she whispered. “Open the right-hand door!”

The two girls stepped out into the traffic. Two minutes later they were seated in another taxi, this time bound direct for Carlton House Terrace.

“There,” said Tuppence, with great satisfaction, “this ought to do them. I can’t help thinking that I’m really rather clever! What’s this thing swerving----Oh!”

There was a grinding noise and a bump. Another taxi had collided with them.

In a flash Tuppence was out on the pavement. Before the approaching policeman arrived she had handed the driver five shillings, and she and Jane had merged themselves in the crowd.

“It may be my fancy,” said Tuppence suddenly, “but I feel as though there was some one behind us.”

“Hurry!” murmured the other. “Oh, hurry!”

At the corner of Carlton House Terrace a large and apparently intoxicated man barred their way. He clutched Jane by the shoulder. Tuppence heard other footsteps behind. Lowering her head, she repeated a manœuvre of childish days, and butted their aggressor full in the capacious middle. The man sat down abruptly on the pavement. Tuppence and Jane took to their heels, footsteps echoing behind them, and reached Sir James’s door in choking gasps. Tuppence seized the bell and Jane the knocker.

The man who had stopped them reached the foot of the steps. For a moment he hesitated, and as he did so the door opened. They fell into the hall together. Sir James came forward from the library door.

“Hullo! What’s this?”

He put his arm round Jane as she swayed, half carried her into the library, and laid her on the leather couch. From a tantalus on the table he poured out a few drops of brandy, and forced her to drink them.

“It’s all right. Don’t be afraid, my child. You’re quite safe.” Sir James looked at Tuppence quizzically. “So you’re not dead, Miss Tuppence, any more than that Tommy boy of yours was! And this”--he turned to the girl on the couch--“is Miss Jane Finn?”

“Yes,” she said quietly, “I am Jane Finn. I have a lot to tell you.”

“When you are stronger----”

“No--now! I shall feel safer when I have told everything.”

“As you please,” said the lawyer, and sat down facing the couch. In a low voice Jane began her story.

“I came over on the _Lusitania_ to take up a post in a hospital in Paris.

“When the _Lusitania_ was torpedoed, a man came up to me. He asked me if I was a patriotic American, and told me he was carrying papers which were just life or death to the Allies. He asked me to take charge of them.

“Mr. Danvers had told me to watch out. At first I had no suspicions, but on the boat to Holyhead I began to get uneasy. There was one woman who had been very keen to chum up with me--a Mrs. Vandemeyer. I felt there was something about her I didn’t like, and on the Irish boat I saw her talking to some queer-looking men, and from the way they looked I saw that they were talking about me.

“The only thing was to act as though I’d noticed nothing, and hope for the best. One thing I’d done already as a precaution--ripped open the oilskin packet and substituted blank paper, and then sewn it up again.

“What to do with the real thing worried me no end. Finally I opened it out flat--there were only two sheets--and laid it between two of the advertisement pages of a magazine. I stuck the two pages together round the edge with some gum off an envelope. I carried the magazine carelessly stuffed into the pocket of my ulster.

“At Holyhead I tried to get into a carriage with people that looked all right. In the end I found myself in a carriage with Mrs. Vandemeyer after all. There was quite a nice-looking man and his wife sitting just opposite, so I felt almost happy about it until just outside London. I had leaned back and closed my eyes. I guess they thought I was asleep, but my eyes weren’t quite shut, and suddenly I saw the nice-looking man get something out of his bag and hand it to Mrs. Vandemeyer, and as he did so he _winked_....

“I can’t tell you how that wink sort of froze me through and through. I got up, trying to look natural and easy. Suddenly Mrs. Vandemeyer said ‘Now,’ and flung something over my nose and mouth as I tried to scream. At the same moment I felt a terrific blow on the back of my head....”

She shuddered.

“When I came back to consciousness I was lying on a dirty bed. There was a screen round it, but I could hear two people talking in the room. Mrs. Vandemeyer was one of them.

“They hadn’t found the papers. They’d got the oilskin packet with the blanks, and they were just mad! They didn’t know whether _I_’d changed the papers, or whether Danvers had been carrying a dummy message. They spoke of”--she closed her eyes--“torturing me to find out!

“I’d never known what fear--really sickening fear--was before! I knew I wouldn’t be able to stand up against torture very long.

“Suddenly something put the thought of loss of memory into my head. The subject had always interested me, and I’d read an awful lot about it. If only I could succeed in carrying the bluff through, it might save me. Then I opened my eyes and started babbling in _French!_

“Mrs. Vandemeyer came round the screen at once. Her face was so wicked I nearly died, but I smiled up at her doubtfully, and asked her in French where I was.

“It puzzled her, I could see. She called the man she had been talking to. He stood by the screen with his face in shadow. He spoke to me in French. His voice was very ordinary and quiet, but somehow, I don’t know why, he scared me worse than the woman. He asked me my name. I said I didn’t know--that I couldn’t remember anything at all.

“Suddenly he caught my wrist, and began twisting it. The pain was awful. I screamed and screamed, but I managed to shriek out things in French. Luckily I fainted. The last thing I heard was his voice saying: ‘That’s not bluff! Anyway, a kid of her age wouldn’t know enough.’

“When I came to, Mrs. Vandemeyer was sweet as honey to me. I murmured something about the ‘doctor’ having hurt my wrist. She looked relieved when I said that.

“By and by she went out of the room. I got up and walked round, examining it. It was a squalid, dirty place. There were no windows, which seemed queer. There were some battered old pictures on the walls, representing scenes from _Faust_.”

Jane’s two listeners gave a simultaneous “Ah!” The girl nodded.

“Yes--it was the place in Soho where Mr. Beresford was imprisoned. My heart gave a great throb of relief when I saw my ulster lying carelessly over the back of a chair. _And the magazine was still rolled up in the pocket!_

“There didn’t seem to be a peep-hole of any kind--nevertheless I felt kind of sure there must be. All of a sudden I sat down on the edge of the table, and put my face in my hands, sobbing out a ‘Mon Dieu! Mon Dieu!’ I distinctly heard the rustle of a dress, and slight creak. That was enough for me. I was being watched!

“By and by Mrs. Vandemeyer brought me some supper. Presently she produced the oilskin packet, and asked me if I recognized it, watching me like a lynx. I turned it over in a puzzled sort of way and shook my head. Then she told me that I was her niece, and that I was to call her ‘Aunt Rita.’ I did obediently.

“They might throw that magazine away any minute. About two o’clock in the morning I got up as softly as I could, and felt in the dark along the left-hand wall. Very gently, I unhooked one of the pictures from its nail--Marguerite with her casket of jewels. I took the magazine from my coat, damped the brown paper at the back of the picture all round, and pulled it away. I slipped the two stuck-together pages with their precious enclosure between the picture and its backing, and a little gum from an envelope helped me to stick it up again. I rehung it, put the magazine back in my coat pocket, and crept back to bed. They’d never think of pulling to pieces one of their own pictures.

“I learnt afterwards that they nearly did away with me then and there--but the first man, who was the boss, preferred to keep me alive on the chance of my having hidden them, and being able to tell where if I recovered my memory.

“They took me back to Ireland, and over every step of the journey again, in case I’d hidden it somewhere _en route_.

“It ended in my being sent to a sanatorium at Bournemouth. A hospital nurse had charge of me. She seemed so nice and normal that at last I determined to confide in her. My door happened to be ajar, and I heard her talking to some one in the passage. _She was one of them!_

“I think I almost hypnotized myself. After a while, I almost forgot that I was really Jane Finn. I wasn’t even unhappy in the end--just apathetic. And the years went on.

“And then suddenly things seemed to change. There was some talk of sending me to a specialist in Paris. I learnt later that the nurse went to Paris and consulted a specialist, representing herself to be me. He exposed her loss of memory to be fraudulent; but she had taken a note of his methods and reproduced them on me.

“One night I was whisked off to London, back to the house in Soho.

“They sent me in to wait on Mr. Beresford. I was suspicious--I thought it was another trap. But he looked so honest, I could hardly believe it. However, I was careful in all I said, for I knew we could be overheard. There’s a small hole, high up in the wall.

“But on the Sunday afternoon word came that he was to be killed. I thought I’d have time to rush up and get the papers, but I was caught. So I screamed out that he was escaping, and I said I wanted to go back to Marguerite. I shouted the name three times very loud. I knew the others would think I meant Mrs. Vandemeyer, but I hoped it might make Mr. Beresford think of the picture. He’d unhooked one the first day--that’s what made me hesitate to trust him.”

“Then the papers,” said Sir James slowly, “are still at the back of the picture in that room.”

“Yes.” The girl sank back on the sofa exhausted.

Sir James rose and looked at his watch.

“Come,” he said, “we must go at once.”

“To-night?” queried Tuppence, surprised.

“To-morrow may be too late,” said Sir James gravely. “Besides, by going to-night we have the chance of capturing that great man and super-criminal--Mr. Brown! You have been followed here--not a doubt of it. When we leave the house we shall be followed again, but not molested, _for it is Mr. Brown’s plan that we are to lead him_. But the Soho house is under police supervision night and day. When we enter that house, Mr. Brown will not draw back--he will risk all, on the chance of obtaining the spark to fire his mine. And he fancies the risk not great--since he will enter in the guise of a friend!”

Tuppence flushed, then opened her mouth impulsively.

“But there’s something you don’t know--that we haven’t told you.” Her eyes dwelt on Jane in perplexity. “It’s so difficult--if I’m wrong--oh, it would be dreadful.”

“You want me to help you out, eh?” Sir James asked.

“Yes, please. _You_ know who Mr. Brown is, don’t you?”

“Yes,” said Sir James gravely. “At last I do. I have been morally certain of his identity ever since the night of Mrs. Vandemeyer’s mysterious death. Either the chloral was administered by her own hand, which theory I reject utterly, or else it was administered in the brandy you gave her. Only three people touched that brandy--you, Miss Tuppence, I myself, and one other--Mr. Julius Hersheimmer!”

Jane Finn stirred and sat up, regarding the speaker with wide astonished eyes.

“At first, the thing seemed utterly impossible,” Sir James went on. “But you cannot escape from the logic of facts. I took an early opportunity of giving you a hint. Then Mr. Beresford rang me up and told me, what I had already suspected, that the photograph of Miss Jane Finn had never really been out of Mr. Hersheimmer’s possession----”

But the girl interrupted. Springing to her feet, she cried out angrily:

“What do you mean? That Mr. Brown is _Julius?_ Julius--my own cousin!”

“No, Miss Finn,” said Sir James unexpectedly. “Not your cousin. The man who calls himself Julius Hersheimmer is no relation to you whatsoever.”

## CHAPTER XXVI. MR. BROWN

Sir James handed Jane a small newspaper cutting. It referred to the mysterious man found dead in New York.

“When I came across this paragraph,” resumed the lawyer, “my problem was solved. Julius Hersheimmer set out to discover what had become of his cousin. On the eve of his departure from New York he was set upon and murdered. Mr. Brown took his place. Since then he had been hand and glove with those sworn to hunt him down. Mrs. Vandemeyer knew his secret. Exposure stared him in the face. He took a desperate step, trusting in his assumed character to avert suspicion. He nearly succeeded--but not quite.”

“I can’t believe it,” murmured Jane. “He seemed so splendid.”

“But ask Miss Tuppence if she also has not had her suspicions.”

Jane turned mutely to Tuppence. The latter nodded.

“I didn’t want to say it, Jane. And, after all, I couldn’t be sure. I still don’t understand why, if he’s Mr. Brown, he rescued us.”

Tuppence recounted to Sir James the exciting events of the evening.

“Can’t you? I can. So can young Beresford, by his actions. As a last hope Jane Finn was to be allowed to escape. They’ll take care to get him out of the way at the right minute. Then Julius Hersheimmer dashes up and rescues you in true melodramatic style. Bullets fly--but don’t hit anybody. You would have driven straight to the house in Soho and secured the document. And I rather fancy some accident would have happened to both of you. I admit I was caught napping; but somebody else wasn’t.”

“Tommy,” said Tuppence softly.

“Yes. All the same, I’m not too easy in my mind about him.”

“Why?”

“Because Julius Hersheimmer is Mr. Brown,” said Sir James dryly. “And it takes more than one man and a revolver to hold up Mr. Brown....”

Tuppence paled a little. “What can we do?”

“Nothing until we’ve been to the house in Soho.” He took a service revolver, and placed it in his coat pocket.

He suggested that Miss Finn should remain behind. But to Tuppence’s surprise Jane shook her head.

“No. I guess I’m going too. Those papers were my trust.”

At the corner of the square Sir James spoke to a plain-clothes man.

“No one has gone into the house so far. It is being watched at the back as well.”

The three entered the house. Slowly they mounted the rickety stairs. At the top was the ragged curtain hiding the recess where Tommy had hidden that day. Even now Tuppence could almost swear it moved--as though _some one_ was behind it. Supposing Mr. Brown--Julius--was there waiting....

Now they were entering the prison room. She must not give way to this curious insistent feeling that _Mr. Brown was in the house_....

Jane had gone straight to the picture of Marguerite. She ripped away the brown paper from the back.... The advertisement page of a magazine fell out. Holding apart the frayed inner edges she extracted two thin sheets covered with writing!

“We’ve got it,” said Tuppence. “At last....”

Sir James took it, and scrutinized it attentively.

“Yes,” he said quietly, “this is the ill-fated draft treaty!”

“We’ve succeeded,” said Tuppence.

Sir James echoed her words as he folded the paper carefully and put it away in his pocket-book, then he looked curiously round the dingy room.

“A truly sinister room,” he said. “You notice the absence of windows, and the thickness of the close-fitting door. Whatever took place here would never be heard by the outside world.”

Tuppence shivered. What if some one concealed in the house should bar that door on them, and leave them to die like rats in a trap? Sir James gave her an emphatic little nod.

“Quite right, Miss Tuppence. You scent danger. So do I. So does Miss Finn.”

“Yes,” admitted Jane. “It’s absurd--but I can’t help it.”

“You feel--as we all feel-- _the presence of Mr. Brown_. Yes”--as Tuppence made a movement--“not a doubt of it-- _Mr. Brown is here_....”

“In this house?”

“In this room.... You don’t understand? _I am Mr. Brown_....”

Stupefied, unbelieving, they stared at him. The very lines of his face had changed. He smiled a slow cruel smile.

“Neither of you will leave this room alive! You said just now we had succeeded. _I_ have succeeded! The draft treaty is mine. Sooner or later the police will break in, and they will find three victims of Mr. Brown--three, not two, you understand, but fortunately the third will not be dead, only wounded, and will be able to describe the attack with a wealth of detail! The treaty? It is in the hands of Mr. Brown. So no one will think of searching the pockets of Sir James Peel Edgerton!”

He turned to Jane.

“You outwitted me. I make my acknowledgments. But you will not do it again.”

There was a faint sound behind him, but, intoxicated with success, he did not turn his head. He slipped his hand into his pocket.

“Checkmate to the Young Adventurers,” he said, and slowly raised the big automatic.

But, even as he did so, he felt himself seized from behind in a grip of iron. The revolver was wrenched from his hand, and the voice of Julius Hersheimmer said drawlingly:

“I guess you’re caught redhanded with the goods upon you.”

The blood rushed to the K.C.’s face, but his self-control was marvellous, as he looked from one to the other of his two captors. He looked longest at Tommy.

“You,” he said beneath his breath. “_You!_ I might have known.”

Seeing that he was disposed to offer no resistance, their grip slackened. Quick as a flash his left hand, the hand which bore the big signet ring, was raised to his lips....

“‘_Ave, Cæsar! te morituri salutant_,’” he said, still looking at Tommy.

Then his face changed, and with a long convulsive shudder he fell forward in a crumpled heap, whilst an odour of bitter almonds filled the air.

## CHAPTER XXVII. A SUPPER PARTY AT THE _SAVOY_

The supper party given by Mr. Julius Hersheimmer to a few friends on the evening of the 30th will long be remembered in catering circles. The list of guests was small and select. The American Ambassador, Mr. Carter, who had taken the liberty, he said, of bringing an old friend, Sir William Beresford, with him, Archdeacon Cowley, Dr. Hall, those two youthful adventurers, Miss Prudence Cowley and Mr. Thomas Beresford, and last, but not least, as guest of honour, Miss Jane Finn.

A mysterious knock had brought Tuppence to the door. It was Julius. In his hand he held a cheque.

“Say, Tuppence,” he began, “will you do me a good turn? Take this, and get Jane regularly togged up for this evening. Spare no expense. You get me?”

“Sure thing,” mimicked Tuppence. “She’s the loveliest thing I’ve ever seen.”

“By the way, Julius,” she remarked demurely, “I--haven’t given you my answer yet.”

“Answer?” said Julius. His face paled.

“You know--when you asked me to--marry you,” faltered Tuppence. “I’ve thought it well over----”

“Yes?” said Julius. The perspiration stood on his forehead.

Tuppence relented suddenly.

“You great idiot!” she said. “I could see at the time you didn’t care a twopenny dip for me!”

“Not at all. I had--and still have--the highest sentiments of esteem and respect--and admiration for you----”

“H’m!” said Tuppence. “Those are the kind of sentiments that very soon go to the wall when the other sentiment comes along! Don’t they, old thing?”

“I don’t know what you mean,” said Julius stiffly, but a large and burning blush overspread his countenance.

“Shucks!” retorted Tuppence. She laughed, and closed the door, reopening it to add with dignity: “Morally, I shall always consider I have been jilted!”

To most people the 29th, the much-heralded “Labour Day,” had passed much as any other day. In the Sunday papers a brief notice of the sudden death of Sir James Peel Edgerton, the famous K.C., had appeared. The exact manner of his sudden death was never made public.

It had been a one-man show. Deprived of their chief, the organization fell to pieces. Kramenin had made a precipitate return to Russia. The gang had fled from Astley Priors in a panic, leaving behind various damaging documents. With these proofs of conspiracy, aided further by a small brown diary taken from the pocket of the dead man, the Government had called an eleventh-hour conference. It was to be Peace, not War!

But burnt in on Mr. Carter’s brain was the strange scene which had taken place in the house in Soho the night before. From the dead man’s pocket-book he had retrieved the ill-omened draft treaty, and then and there, in the presence of the other three, it had been reduced to ashes.... England was saved!

And now, on the evening of the 30th, in a private room at the _Savoy_, Mr. Julius P. Hersheimmer was receiving his guests.

Mr. Carter was the first to arrive. With him was a choleric-looking old gentleman, at sight of whom Tommy flushed up to the roots of his hair.

“Ha!” said the old gentleman, surveying him apoplectically. “So you’re my nephew, are you? Your mother must have brought you up well after all. Shall we let bygones be bygones, eh? You’re my heir, you know.”

“Thank you, sir, it’s awfully decent of you,” said Tommy.

“Where’s this young lady I’ve been hearing such a lot about?”

Tommy introduced Tuppence.

“Ha!” said Sir William, eyeing her. “Girls aren’t what they used to be in my young days.”

“Yes, they are,” said Tuppence. “Their clothes are different, perhaps, but they themselves are just the same.”

“Well, perhaps you’re right. Minxes then--minxes now!”

“That’s it,” said Tuppence. “I’m a frightful minx myself.”

“I believe you,” said the old gentleman, chuckling, and pinched her ear in high good-humour. Tuppence’s pertness delighted the old misogynist.

Then came the timid archdeacon, unable to help glancing at his daughter from time to time with nervous apprehension. But Tuppence behaved admirably. She steadfastly refused to smoke.

“We might as well sit down,” said Julius. “Tuppence, will you----”

He indicated the place of honour with a wave of his hand. But Tuppence shook her head.

“No--that’s Jane’s place! When one thinks of how she’s held out all these years, she ought to be made the queen of the feast to-night.”

Jane came forward shyly to the allotted seat. The model gown supplied by a famous dressmaker had been entitled “A tiger lily.” There was admiration in every eye.

Soon the supper party was in full swing, and with one accord Tommy was called upon for a full and complete explanation.

“You’ve been too darned close about the whole business,” Julius accused him. “The idea of both you and Tuppence casting me for the part of Mr. Brown just tickles me to death!”

“The idea was not original to them,” said Mr. Carter gravely. “It was suggested, and the poison very carefully instilled, by a past-master in the art.”

“I never liked him,” said Julius. “But it wasn’t till I heard that the order for Tommy’s execution came right on the heels of our interview with him that Sunday that I began to tumble to the fact that he was the big bug himself.”

“There’s nothing to tell,” said Tommy, acutely uncomfortable. “I was an awful mug--right up to the time I found that photograph of Annette, and realized that she was Jane Finn. Then I remembered how persistently she had shouted out that word ‘Marguerite’--and I thought of the pictures, and--well, that’s that.”

“That business about Mrs. Vandemeyer had worried me,” Tommy went on. “It seemed that Julius or Sir James must have done the trick. Finding that photograph in the drawer, after that story of how it had been got from Julius by Inspector Brown, made me suspect Julius. Then I remembered that it was Sir James who had discovered the false Jane Finn. I left a note for Julius, in case he was Mr. Brown, saying I was off to the Argentine, and I dropped Sir James’s letter with the offer of the job by the desk so that he would see it was a genuine stunt. I kept my mind open between the two of them. And then I got a bogus note from Tuppence--and I knew!”

“But how?”

Tommy took the note from his pocket.

“It’s her handwriting all right, but I knew it wasn’t from her because of the signature. She’d never spell her name ‘Twopence,’ but anyone who’d never seen it written might quite easily do so. Julius _had_ seen it--he showed me a note of hers to him once--but _Sir James hadn’t!_ I sent off Albert post-haste to Mr. Carter. I pretended to go away, but doubled back again. Unless Sir James was actually caught in the act, so to speak, I knew Mr. Carter would never believe it of him on my bare word----”

“I didn’t,” interposed Mr. Carter ruefully.

“That’s why I sent the girls off to Sir James,” said Tommy. “I was sure they’d fetch up at the house in Soho sooner or later. I threatened Julius with the revolver, because I wanted Tuppence to repeat that to Sir James, so that he wouldn’t worry about us. Then I told Julius to drive like hell for London. We met Mr. Carter outside the Soho house, went in and hid behind the curtain in the recess. The policemen had orders to say, if they were asked, that no one had gone into the house. That’s all.”

“By the way,” said Julius suddenly, “you’re all wrong about that photograph of Jane. It _was_ taken from me, but I found it again.”

“Where?” cried Tuppence.

“In that little safe on the wall in Mrs. Vandemeyer’s bedroom.”

“I knew you found something,” said Tuppence reproachfully. “To tell you the truth, that’s what started me off suspecting you. Why didn’t you say?”

“I guess I was a mite suspicious too,” said Julius. “I determined I wouldn’t let on I’d got it until a photographer had made a dozen copies of it!”

“We all kept back something or other,” said Tuppence thoughtfully. “I suppose secret service work makes you like that!”

In the pause that ensued, Mr. Carter took from his pocket a small shabby brown book.

“To you, who know the truth, I propose to read certain passages.”

“... It is madness to keep this book. But I have never shrunk from taking risks....

“... From an early age I realized that I had exceptional abilities. Only a fool underestimates his capabilities. My appearance was the only thing against me. I was quiet and insignificant--utterly nondescript....

“... When I was a boy I heard a famous murder trial.... I studied the criminal in the dock.... The man was a fool.... I felt an immeasurable contempt for him....

“... Supposing ... that I became Prime Minister of England? What then? Was that power? No--the power I dreamed of was absolute! An autocrat! A dictator! And such power could only be obtained by working outside the law....

“... I saw that I must lead two lives.... Personality! I put it on like a glove! When I shed it, I was myself, quiet, unobtrusive, a man like every other man. I called myself Mr. Brown. There are hundreds of men called Brown--there are hundreds of men looking just like me....

“... Once or twice I have felt afraid. Professor D----, the great alienist, was present. He said, ‘A great many men are mad, and no one knows it. They do not know it themselves.’ I do not understand why he looked at me when he said that....”

Mr. Carter paused. “There are just two small entries that refer to the three of you.

“... By inducing the girl to come to me of her own accord, I have succeeded in disarming her. But she has intuitive flashes that might be dangerous.... She must be got out of the way.... I can do nothing with the American. He suspects and dislikes me. But he cannot know. Sometimes I fear I have underestimated the other boy. He is not clever, but it is hard to blind his eyes to facts....”

Mr. Carter shut the book.

“A great man,” he said. “Genius, or insanity, who can say?”

There was silence.

Then Mr. Carter rose to his feet.

“I will give you a toast. The Joint Venture which has so amply justified itself by success!”

It was drunk with acclamation.

“There’s something more we want to hear,” continued Mr. Carter. “We’ll ask Miss Jane Finn to tell us the story that only Miss Tuppence has heard so far--but before we do so we’ll drink her health. The health of one of the bravest of America’s daughters!”

## CHAPTER XXVIII. AND AFTER

“That was a mighty good toast, Jane,” said Mr. Hersheimmer, as he and his cousin were being driven back in the Rolls-Royce to the _Ritz_. “You were just wonderful!”

“I don’t feel wonderful. At heart I’m just tired and lonesome--and longing for my own country.”

“I heard the Ambassador telling you his wife hoped you would come to them at the Embassy. Jane--I want you to marry me! I’ve loved you from the very moment I set eyes on your photo. You shall take your own time. Maybe you’ll never come to love me, and if that’s the case I’ll manage to set you free. But I want the right to look after you.”

“That’s what I want,” said the girl wistfully. “Some one who’ll be good to me.”

“Sure thing. Then I guess that’s all fixed up, and I’ll see the archbishop about a special license to-morrow morning.”

“Oh, Julius!”

“Well, I don’t want to hustle you any, Jane, but there’s no sense in waiting about.”

But a small hand was slipped into his.

“I love you now, Julius,” said Jane Finn. “I loved you that first moment in the car when the bullet grazed your cheek....”

Five minutes later Jane murmured softly:

“Is it such a very long way from the _Savoy_ to the _Ritz?_”

“It depends how you go,” explained Julius unblushingly. “We’re going by way of Regent’s Park! The only reason I had the supper at the _Savoy_ was so that I could drive you home. You and Tuppence have been sticking together like Siamese twins. I guess another day of it would have driven me and Beresford stark staring mad!”

“Oh. Is he----?”

“Of course he is. Head over ears.”

“I thought so,” said Jane thoughtfully.

“Why?”

“From all the things Tuppence didn’t say!”

The Young Adventurers were sitting bolt upright in a taxi which, with a singular lack of originality, was also returning to the _Ritz_ via Regent’s Park.

They were tongue-tied--paralysed. All the old _camaraderie_ was gone.

“I like Julius,” essayed Tuppence.

“You’re not going to marry him, do you hear?” he said dictatorially. “I forbid it.”

“Oh!” said Tuppence meekly. “He doesn’t want to marry me--he really only asked me out of kindness. He’s head over ears in love with Jane.”

“She’ll do for him very nicely,” said Tommy condescendingly.

“But I suppose you prefer sterling worth,” said Tuppence demurely.

“I--oh, dash it all, Tuppence, you know!”

“By the way,” said Tuppence, hastily creating a diversion, “are you going to accept Mr. Carter’s offer of a Government job, or take a post on Julius’s ranch?”

“I shall stick to the old ship, I think. I feel you’d be more at home in London.”

“I don’t see where I come in.”

“I do,” said Tommy positively.

“We’re going to get a cheque each,” observed Tuppence. “Mr. Carter told me so.”

“Did you ask how much?” inquired Tommy sarcastically.

“Yes,” said Tuppence triumphantly. “But I shan’t tell you.”

“Tuppence, you are the limit!”

“Think of buying old furniture,” said Tuppence dreamily, “and a divan with lots of cushions.”

“Hold hard,” said Tommy. “What’s all this for?”

“Possibly a house--but I think a flat.”

“Whose flat?”

“You think I mind saying it, but I don’t in the least! _Ours_, so there!”

“You darling!” cried Tommy, his arms tightly round her. “I owe you something for the relentless way you’ve squashed me whenever I’ve tried to be sentimental.”

“You haven’t really proposed now,” pointed out Tuppence. “But after listening to a rotten one like Julius’s, I’m inclined to let you off.”

“You won’t be able to get out of marrying me, so don’t you think it.”

“What fun it will be,” responded Tuppence. “Marriage is called a haven, and a state of bondage. But do you know what I think it is?”

“What?”

“A sport!”

“And a damned good sport too,” said Tommy.

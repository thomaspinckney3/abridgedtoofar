THE next day passed slowly. Carefully husbanded, forty pounds will last a long time.

On Thursday the advertisement had duly appeared. On Friday letters might be expected to arrive at Tommy's rooms.

He had been bound by an honourable promise not to open any such letters if they did arrive, but to repair to the National Gallery, where his colleague would meet him at ten o'clock.

"Well?"

"Well," returned Mr. Beresford provokingly. "Which is your favourite picture?"

"Don't be a wretch. Aren't there _any_ answers?"

Tommy shook his head with a deep and somewhat overacted melancholy.

"I didn't want to disappoint you, old thing, by telling you right off. Good money wasted." He sighed. "Still, there it is. The advertisement has appeared, and--there are only two answers!"

"Tommy, you devil!" almost screamed Tuppence. "Give them to me. How could you be so mean!"

"Your language, Tuppence, your language! And do remember that as a clergyman's daughter----"

"I ought to be on the stage!" finished Tuppence with a snap.

Tuppence snatched the two precious envelopes from him unceremoniously. Her little thumb ripped open the first.

"DEAR SIR,

"Referring to your advertisement in this morning's paper, I may be able to be of some use to you. Perhaps you could call and see me at the above address at eleven o'clock to-morrow morning.

"Yours truly,

"A. CARTER."

"27 Carshalton Gardens," said Tuppence.

"The following," said Tommy, "is the plan of campaign. Mr. Carter says: 'Please take a seat, Mr.--er?' To which I reply promptly and significantly: 'Edward Whittington!' whereupon Mr. Carter turns purple in the face and gasps out: 'How much?'"

"Don't be absurd, Tommy. Now for the other letter. Oh, this is from the _Ritz!_ I'll read it:

"DEAR SIR,

"Re your advertisement, I should be glad if you would call round somewhere about lunch-time.

"Yours truly,

"JULIUS P. HERSHEIMMER."

"Ha!" said Tommy. "Do I smell a Boche? Or only an American millionaire of unfortunate ancestry? At all events we'll call at lunch-time. It's a good time--frequently leads to free food for two."

Tuppence nodded assent.

"Now for Carter. We'll have to hurry."

At No. 27 a neat maid showed them into a small study. Hardly a minute elapsed before a tall man with a lean hawklike face and a tired manner entered the room.

"Do sit down, both of you," he said, and smiled. His smile was distinctly attractive, and something in its quality made Tuppence's usual readiness desert her. She was forced to begin.

"We wanted to know--that is, would you be so kind as to tell us anything you know about Jane Finn?"

"Jane Finn? Ah!" Mr. Carter appeared to reflect. "Well, the question is, what do _you_ know about her?"

Tuppence drew herself up.

"I don't see that that's got anything to do with it."

"No? But it has, you know, really it has. Suppose you tell me...."

There was something very magnetic about Mr. Carter's personality. Tuppence seemed to shake herself free of it with an effort.

"We couldn't do that, could we, Tommy?"

But to her surprise, her companion did not back her up.

"I dare say the little we know won't be any good to you, sir. But such as it is, you're welcome to it."

"Tommy!" cried out Tuppence in surprise.

Mr. Carter's eyes asked a question. Tommy nodded.

"Yes, sir, I recognized you at once. Saw you in France when I was with the Intelligence. As soon as you came into the room, I knew----"

Mr. Carter held up his hand.

"No names, please. I'm known as Mr. Carter here. It's my cousin's house, by the way. She's willing to lend it to me sometimes when it's a case of working on strictly unofficial lines. Well, now, who's going to tell me the story?"

"Fire ahead, Tuppence," directed Tommy. "It's your yarn."

And obediently Tuppence told the whole story from the forming of the Young Adventurers, Ltd., downwards, through Mr. Whittington and his office.

When she had finished Mr. Carter nodded gravely.

"Not much. But suggestive. You might succeed where others have failed ... I believe in luck, you know--always have.... How would you like to work for me? All quite unofficial, you know. Expenses paid, and a moderate screw?"

"What should we have to do?" she breathed.

Mr. Carter smiled.

"Just go on with what you're doing now. _Find Jane Finn_."

"Yes, but--who _is_ Jane Finn?"

"Yes, you're entitled to know that, I think." He leaned back and began in a low monotone:

"In the early days of 1915 a certain document came into being--the draft of a secret agreement, drawn up in America, at that time a neutral country. It was dispatched to England by a special messenger, a young fellow called Danvers.

"Danvers sailed on the _Lusitania_. He carried the precious papers in an oilskin packet which he wore next his skin. On that voyage the _Lusitania_ was torpedoed and sunk. Eventually his body was washed ashore. But the oilskin packet was missing!

"After the torpedo struck, Danvers was seen speaking to a young American girl. No one actually saw him pass anything to her, but he might have done so. By later advice from America it seemed likely that Danvers had been closely shadowed.

"Her name was Jane Finn, and it duly appeared among the list of the survivors. She was an orphan, a pupil teacher in a small school out West, going to join the staff of a hospital in Paris. She never arrived. We tracked her across Ireland, but nothing could be heard of her after she set foot in England. No use was made of the draft treaty, and we therefore concluded that Danvers had destroyed it."

Tuppence broke in impatiently:

"But why has it all cropped up again? The war's over."

"Because it seems that the papers were not destroyed after all. Five years ago, that draft treaty was a weapon in our hands; to-day it is a weapon against us. It might possibly bring about another war--an extreme possibility, and I do not believe in its likelihood myself--but it undoubtedly implicates a number of our statesmen. As a party cry for Labour it would be irresistible--in my opinion a grave disability for British trade--but that is a mere nothing to the _real_ danger. Bolshevist gold is pouring into this country to procure a Revolution. And there is a certain man, whose real name is unknown to us, working in the dark for his own ends. The Bolshevists are behind the Labour unrest--but this man is _behind the Bolshevists_. He is always spoken of by the unassuming title of 'Mr. Brown.'"

"A naturalized German?" asked Tommy.

"On the contrary, I have every reason to believe he is an Englishman. What he seeks we do not know--probably supreme power for himself. Where we have come across his tracks, he has always played a secondary part. Somebody else assumes the chief rôle. But afterwards we always find that there has been some nonentity, a servant or a clerk, who has remained in the background unnoticed, and that the elusive Mr. Brown has escaped us once more."

"Oh!" Tuppence jumped. "I remember in Mr. Whittington's office. The clerk--he called him Brown. You don't think----"

Carter nodded thoughtfully.

"Very likely. Can you describe him at all?"

"I really didn't notice. He was quite ordinary--just like anyone else."

Mr. Carter sighed in his tired manner.

"That is the invariable description of Mr. Brown! Brought a telephone message to the man Whittington, did he? Notice a telephone in the outer office?"

"No, I don't think I did."

"Exactly. That 'message' was Mr. Brown's way of giving an order to his subordinate. He overheard the whole conversation of course. Possibly the finest criminal brain of the age. I don't quite like it, you know. You're such young things, both of you."

"I'll look after her, sir," said Tommy.

"And _I_'ll look after _you_," retorted Tuppence, resenting the manly assertion.

"Well, then, look after each other," said Mr. Carter, smiling. "Now there's something mysterious about this draft treaty. The Revolutionary element as good as declare that it's in their hands, yet they are clearly at fault about many of its provisions. It is as though they couldn't read it because it was in cipher--but we know the draft treaty wasn't in cipher--so that won't wash. The curious thing is that _they're trying to get information about the girl from us_."

"What?"

"Yes. And your story, little lady, confirms my idea. They know we're looking for Jane Finn. Well, they'll produce a Jane Finn of their own--say at a _pensionnat_ in Paris." Tuppence gasped. "No one knows in the least what she looks like. She's primed with a trumped-up tale, and her real business is to get information out of us."

"Then you think"--Tuppence paused--"that it _was_ as Jane Finn that they wanted me to go to Paris?"

Mr. Carter smiled more wearily than ever.

"I believe in coincidences, you know," he said.

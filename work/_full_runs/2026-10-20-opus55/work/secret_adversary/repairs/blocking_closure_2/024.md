AFTER ringing up Sir James, Tommy's next procedure was to make a call at South Audley Mansions. He found Albert discharging his professional duties, and introduced himself as a friend of Tuppence's. Albert unbent immediately.

"Hope the young lady's keeping well, sir?"

"That's just the point, Albert. She's disappeared."

"You don't mean as the crooks have got her?"

"They have."

"In the Underworld?"

"No, dash it all, in this world!"

"It's a h'expression, sir," explained Albert. "At the pictures the crooks always have a restoorant in the Underworld. But do you think as they've done her in, sir?"

"I hope not," said Tommy. "By the way, have you by any chance an aunt, a cousin, a grandmother, or any other suitable female relation who might be represented as being likely to kick the bucket?"

"I'm on, sir. My poor aunt what lives in the country has been mortal bad for a long time, and she's asking for me with her dying breath."

"Can you report this in the proper quarter and meet me at Charing Cross in an hour's time?"

"I'll be there, sir," said Albert.

The two took up their quarters at the inn in Gatehouse.

Astley Priors was the property of a Dr. Adams. The doctor no longer practiced, but he took a few private patients. Here the landlord tapped his forehead knowingly. "Balmy ones! You understand!" The doctor was a popular figure in the village. "A very pleasant, affable gentleman."

Tommy felt doubts. Suppose it was all a gigantic mistake? Tommy felt a cold chill at the thought. He inquired carefully if there was a young lady amongst the patients, describing Tuppence. But the patients were seldom seen outside the grounds. A guarded description of Annette also failed to provoke recognition.

On the first evening Tommy, accompanied by Albert, explored the well-wooded grounds. Owing to Albert's insistence they dragged themselves along painfully on their stomachs, thereby producing a great deal more noise than if they had stood upright. But they reached a shrubbery near the house quite unmolested.

There was a large company assembled round the table. Through the open window scraps of conversation floated out. It was a heated discussion on county cricket!

Again Tommy felt that cold chill of uncertainty.

The indefatigable Albert, having cemented an alliance with the greengrocer's boy, took the latter's place, and on the second day brought back the first piece of hopeful news. There _was_ a French young lady staying in the house.

Tommy put his doubts aside. But time pressed. To-day was the 27th. The 29th was the much-talked-of "Labour Day."

With the fatal document in the hands of Mr. Brown, public opinion would swing to the side of the Labour extremists and revolutionists. But Tommy nourished another and a preposterous dream. With Mr. Brown unmasked and captured he believed, rightly or wrongly, that the whole organization would crumble ignominiously and instantaneously. Without him, Tommy believed an instant panic would set in; and, the honest men left to themselves, an eleventh-hour reconciliation would be possible.

"This is a one-man show," said Tommy to himself. "The thing to do is to get hold of the man."

It was partly in furtherance of this ambitious design that he had requested Mr. Carter not to open the sealed envelope. The draft treaty was Tommy's bait. Every now and then he was aghast at his own presumption. Nevertheless, he stuck tenaciously to his idea.

That evening he and Albert once more penetrated the grounds of Astley Priors. As they approached cautiously, Tommy gave a sudden gasp.

On the second floor window some one standing between the window and the light in the room threw a silhouette on the blind. It was one Tommy would have recognized anywhere! Tuppence was in that house!

He clutched Albert by the shoulder.

"Stay here! When I begin to sing, watch that window."

He retreated hastily to a position on the main drive, and began in a deep roar, coupled with an unsteady gait, the following ditty:

          I am a Soldier
          A jolly British Soldier;
          You can see that I'm a Soldier by my feet....

It had been a favourite on the gramophone in Tuppence's hospital days.

Presently an unimpeachable butler, accompanied by an equally unimpeachable footman, issued from the front door. Tommy continued to sing, addressing the butler affectionately as "dear old whiskers." They ran him down the drive, and neatly out of the gate. Anyone would have sworn that the butler was a real butler, the footman a real footman--only, as it happened, the butler was Whittington!

Tommy retired to the inn and waited for Albert's return.

"Well?" cried Tommy eagerly.

"It's all right. While they was a-running of you out the window opened, and something was chucked out," said Albert. He handed over a scrap of paper. On it were scrawled three words: "To-morrow--same time."

"Good egg!" cried Tommy. "We're getting going."

"I wrote a message, wrapped it round a stone, and chucked it through the window," continued Albert breathlessly. "Said we was a-staying at the inn. If she could get away, to come there and croak like a frog."

Tommy groaned.

"Cheer up," said Tommy. "No harm done. That butler's an old friend of mine--I bet he knew who I was, though he didn't let on. I'm a pawn in their game, Albert, that's what I am. You see, if the spider lets the fly walk out too easily, the fly might suspect it was a put-up job."

Tommy retired for the night in a state of some elation.

About twelve o'clock, however, Tommy's calm was rudely shaken. A rude-looking carter was demanding him in the bar.

"Might this be for you, sir?" The carter held out a folded note, on the outside of which was written: "Take this to the gentleman at the inn near Astley Priors. He will give you ten shillings."

The handwriting was Tuppence's. Tommy unfastened it.

"DEAR TOMMY,

"I knew it was you last night. Don't go this evening. They'll be lying in wait for you. They're taking us away this morning. I heard something about Wales--Holyhead, I think. I'll drop this on the road if I get a chance. Annette told me how you'd escaped. Buck up.

"Yours,

"TWOPENCE."

Tommy raised a shout for Albert before he had even finished perusing this characteristic epistle.

"Pack my bag! We're off!"

"Yes, sir." The boots of Albert could be heard racing upstairs. Holyhead? Did that mean that, after all---- Tommy was puzzled. He read on slowly.

Suddenly a second shout came from below.

"Albert! I'm a damned fool! Unpack that bag!"

"Yes, sir."

Tommy smoothed out the note thoughtfully.

"Yes, a damned fool," he said softly. "But so's some one else! And at last I know who it is!"

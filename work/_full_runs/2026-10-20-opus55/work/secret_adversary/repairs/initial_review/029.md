“That was a mighty good toast, Jane,” said Mr. Hersheimmer, as he and his cousin were being driven back in the Rolls-Royce to the _Ritz_. “You were just wonderful!”

“I don’t feel wonderful. At heart I’m just tired and lonesome--and longing for my own country.”

“I heard the Ambassador telling you his wife hoped you would come to them at the Embassy. That’s good enough, but I’ve got another plan. Jane--I want you to marry me! I’ve loved you from the very moment I set eyes on your photo. You shall take your own time. Maybe you’ll never come to love me, and if that’s the case I’ll manage to set you free. But I want the right to look after you.”

“That’s what I want,” said the girl wistfully. “Some one who’ll be good to me.”

“Sure thing. Then I guess that’s all fixed up, and I’ll see the archbishop about a special license to-morrow morning.”

“Oh, Julius!”

“Well, I don’t want to hustle you any, Jane, but there’s no sense in waiting about.”

But a small hand was slipped into his.

“I love you now, Julius,” said Jane Finn. “I loved you that first moment in the car when the bullet grazed your cheek....”

Five minutes later Jane murmured softly:

“Is it such a very long way from the _Savoy_ to the _Ritz?_”

“It depends how you go,” explained Julius unblushingly. “We’re going by way of Regent’s Park! The only reason I had the supper at the _Savoy_ was so that I could drive you home. You and Tuppence have been sticking together like Siamese twins. I guess another day of it would have driven me and Beresford stark staring mad!”

“Oh. Is he----?”

“Of course he is. Head over ears.”

“I thought so,” said Jane thoughtfully.

“Why?”

“From all the things Tuppence didn’t say!”

The Young Adventurers were sitting bolt upright in a taxi which, with a singular lack of originality, was also returning to the _Ritz_ via Regent’s Park.

They were tongue-tied--paralysed. All the old _camaraderie_ was gone.

“I like Julius,” essayed Tuppence.

Tommy was suddenly galvanized into life.

“You’re not going to marry him, do you hear?” he said dictatorially. “I forbid it.”

“Oh!” said Tuppence meekly. “He doesn’t want to marry me--he really only asked me out of kindness. He’s head over ears in love with Jane.”

“She’ll do for him very nicely,” said Tommy condescendingly.

“But I suppose you prefer sterling worth,” said Tuppence demurely.

“I--oh, dash it all, Tuppence, you know!”

“By the way,” said Tuppence, hastily creating a diversion, “are you going to accept Mr. Carter’s offer of a Government job, or take a richly remunerated post on Julius’s ranch?”

“I shall stick to the old ship, I think. I feel you’d be more at home in London.”

“I don’t see where I come in.”

“I do,” said Tommy positively.

“We’re going to get a cheque each,” observed Tuppence. “Mr. Carter told me so.”

“Did you ask how much?” inquired Tommy sarcastically.

“Yes,” said Tuppence triumphantly. “But I shan’t tell you.”

“Think of buying old furniture,” said Tuppence dreamily, “and a divan with lots of cushions.”

“Hold hard,” said Tommy. “What’s all this for?”

“Possibly a house--but I think a flat.”

“Whose flat?”

“You think I mind saying it, but I don’t in the least! _Ours_, so there!”

“You darling!” cried Tommy, his arms tightly round her. “I owe you something for the relentless way you’ve squashed me whenever I’ve tried to be sentimental.”

“You haven’t really proposed now,” pointed out Tuppence. “But after listening to a rotten one like Julius’s, I’m inclined to let you off.”

“You won’t be able to get out of marrying me, so don’t you think it.”

“What fun it will be,” responded Tuppence. “Marriage is called a haven, and a state of bondage. But do you know what I think it is?”

“What?”

“A sport!”

“And a damned good sport too,” said Tommy.

HER arm through Jane’s, dragging her along, Tuppence reached the station. Her quick ears caught the sound of the approaching train.

They arrived on the platform just as the train came to a standstill. Tuppence opened the door of an empty first-class compartment, and the two girls sank down breathless on the padded seats.

Tuppence took Jane’s hand in hers. “Tommy wouldn’t have told us to do this unless he was sure we’d be all right.”

“But he doesn’t know them as I do!” The girl shivered. “You can’t understand. Five years! Five long years! Sometimes I thought I should go mad.”

“Never mind. It’s all over.”

“Is it?”

The train was moving now, speeding through the night.

“My cousin didn’t think it was safe. He didn’t want us to do this.”

“No,” said Tuppence, rather embarrassed.

“What are you thinking of?” said Jane sharply. “Your voice was so--queer!”

“I _was_ thinking of something,” confessed Tuppence. “But I don’t want to tell you--not now. I may be wrong, but I don’t think so. It’s just an idea that came into my head a long time ago. Tommy’s got it too--I’m almost sure he has. Lie back and don’t think of anything.”

“I’ll try.” The long lashes drooped over the hazel eyes.

Tuppence, for her part, sat bolt upright--much in the attitude of a watchful terrier on guard. In her own mind she was far from feeling the confidence displayed in her words. Not that she disbelieved in Tommy, but occasionally she was shaken with doubts as to whether anyone so simple and honest as he was could ever be a match for the fiendish subtlety of the arch-criminal.

Tuppence mapped out her plan of campaign.

As the train drew into Charing Cross, Jane sat up with a start.

“Have we arrived? I never thought we should!”

“If there’s going to be any fun, now is when it will begin. Quick, get out. We’ll nip into a taxi.”

“King’s Cross,” directed Tuppence.

“You see,” she explained to Jane, “if they think we’re going to Sir James, this will put them off the scent. Now they’ll imagine we’re going to Mr. Carter.”

Crossing Holborn there was a block, and the taxi was held up. This was what Tuppence had been waiting for.

“Quick,” she whispered. “Open the right-hand door!”

The two girls stepped out into the traffic. Two minutes later they were seated in another taxi, this time bound direct for Carlton House Terrace.

“There,” said Tuppence, with great satisfaction, “this ought to do them. I can’t help thinking that I’m really rather clever! What’s this thing swerving----Oh!”

There was a grinding noise and a bump. Another taxi had collided with them.

In a flash Tuppence was out on the pavement. Before the approaching policeman arrived she had handed the driver five shillings, and she and Jane had merged themselves in the crowd.

“It may be my fancy,” said Tuppence suddenly, “but I feel as though there was some one behind us.”

“Hurry!” murmured the other. “Oh, hurry!”

At the corner of Carlton House Terrace a large and apparently intoxicated man barred their way. He clutched Jane by the shoulder. Tuppence heard other footsteps behind. Lowering her head, she repeated a manœuvre of childish days, and butted their aggressor full in the capacious middle. The man sat down abruptly on the pavement. Tuppence and Jane took to their heels, footsteps echoing behind them, and reached Sir James’s door in choking gasps. Tuppence seized the bell and Jane the knocker.

The man who had stopped them reached the foot of the steps. For a moment he hesitated, and as he did so the door opened. They fell into the hall together. Sir James came forward from the library door.

“Hullo! What’s this?”

He put his arm round Jane as she swayed, half carried her into the library, and laid her on the leather couch. From a tantalus on the table he poured out a few drops of brandy, and forced her to drink them.

“It’s all right. Don’t be afraid, my child. You’re quite safe.” Sir James looked at Tuppence quizzically. “So you’re not dead, Miss Tuppence, any more than that Tommy boy of yours was! And this”--he turned to the girl on the couch--“is Miss Jane Finn?”

“Yes,” she said quietly, “I am Jane Finn. I have a lot to tell you.”

“When you are stronger----”

“No--now! I shall feel safer when I have told everything.”

“As you please,” said the lawyer, and sat down facing the couch. In a low voice Jane began her story.

“I came over on the _Lusitania_ to take up a post in a hospital in Paris.

“When the _Lusitania_ was torpedoed, a man came up to me. He asked me if I was a patriotic American, and told me he was carrying papers which were just life or death to the Allies. He asked me to take charge of them.

“Mr. Danvers had told me to watch out. At first I had no suspicions, but on the boat to Holyhead I began to get uneasy. There was one woman who had been very keen to chum up with me--a Mrs. Vandemeyer. I felt there was something about her I didn’t like, and on the Irish boat I saw her talking to some queer-looking men, and from the way they looked I saw that they were talking about me.

“The only thing was to act as though I’d noticed nothing, and hope for the best. One thing I’d done already as a precaution--ripped open the oilskin packet and substituted blank paper, and then sewn it up again.

“What to do with the real thing worried me no end. Finally I opened it out flat--there were only two sheets--and laid it between two of the advertisement pages of a magazine. I stuck the two pages together round the edge with some gum off an envelope. I carried the magazine carelessly stuffed into the pocket of my ulster.

“At Holyhead I tried to get into a carriage with people that looked all right. In the end I found myself in a carriage with Mrs. Vandemeyer after all. There was quite a nice-looking man and his wife sitting just opposite, so I felt almost happy about it until just outside London. I had leaned back and closed my eyes. I guess they thought I was asleep, but my eyes weren’t quite shut, and suddenly I saw the nice-looking man get something out of his bag and hand it to Mrs. Vandemeyer, and as he did so he _winked_....

“I can’t tell you how that wink sort of froze me through and through. I got up, trying to look natural and easy. Suddenly Mrs. Vandemeyer said ‘Now,’ and flung something over my nose and mouth as I tried to scream. At the same moment I felt a terrific blow on the back of my head....”

She shuddered. In a minute she resumed:

“When I came back to consciousness I was lying on a dirty bed. There was a screen round it, but I could hear two people talking in the room. Mrs. Vandemeyer was one of them.

“They hadn’t found the papers. They’d got the oilskin packet with the blanks, and they were just mad! They didn’t know whether _I_’d changed the papers, or whether Danvers had been carrying a dummy message. They spoke of”--she closed her eyes--“torturing me to find out!

“I’d never known what fear--really sickening fear--was before! I knew I wouldn’t be able to stand up against torture very long.

“Suddenly something put the thought of loss of memory into my head. The subject had always interested me, and I’d read an awful lot about it. If only I could succeed in carrying the bluff through, it might save me. Then I opened my eyes and started babbling in _French!_

“Mrs. Vandemeyer came round the screen at once. Her face was so wicked I nearly died, but I smiled up at her doubtfully, and asked her in French where I was.

“It puzzled her, I could see. She called the man she had been talking to. He stood by the screen with his face in shadow. He spoke to me in French. His voice was very ordinary and quiet, but somehow, I don’t know why, he scared me worse than the woman. He asked me my name. I said I didn’t know--that I couldn’t remember anything at all.

“Suddenly he caught my wrist, and began twisting it. The pain was awful. I screamed and screamed, but I managed to shriek out things in French. Luckily I fainted. The last thing I heard was his voice saying: ‘That’s not bluff! Anyway, a kid of her age wouldn’t know enough.’

“When I came to, Mrs. Vandemeyer was sweet as honey to me. I murmured something about the ‘doctor’ having hurt my wrist.

“By and by she went out of the room. In the end I got up and walked round, examining it. It was a squalid, dirty place. There were no windows, which seemed queer. There were some battered old pictures on the walls, representing scenes from _Faust_.”

Jane’s two listeners gave a simultaneous “Ah!” The girl nodded.

“Yes--it was the place in Soho where Mr. Beresford was imprisoned. My heart gave a great throb of relief when I saw my ulster lying carelessly over the back of a chair. _And the magazine was still rolled up in the pocket!_

“There didn’t seem to be a peep-hole of any kind--nevertheless I felt kind of sure there must be. All of a sudden I sat down on the edge of the table, and put my face in my hands, sobbing out a ‘Mon Dieu! Mon Dieu!’ I distinctly heard the rustle of a dress, and slight creak. That was enough for me. I was being watched!

“By and by Mrs. Vandemeyer brought me some supper. Presently she produced the oilskin packet, and asked me if I recognized it, watching me like a lynx all the time. I turned it over in a puzzled sort of way and shook my head. Then she told me that I was her niece, and that I was to call her ‘Aunt Rita.’ I did obediently.

“They might throw that magazine away any minute. About two o’clock in the morning I got up as softly as I could, and felt in the dark along the left-hand wall. Very gently, I unhooked one of the pictures from its nail--Marguerite with her casket of jewels. I took the magazine from my coat, damped the brown paper at the back of the picture all round, and pulled it away. I slipped the two stuck-together pages with their precious enclosure between the picture and its backing, and a little gum from an envelope helped me to stick it up again. I rehung it, put the magazine back in my coat pocket, and crept back to bed. They’d never think of pulling to pieces one of their own pictures.

“I learnt afterwards that they nearly did away with me then and there--but the first man, who was the boss, preferred to keep me alive on the chance of my having hidden them, and being able to tell where if I recovered my memory.

“They took me back to Ireland, and over every step of the journey again, in case I’d hidden it somewhere _en route_.

“It ended in my being sent to a sanatorium at Bournemouth. A hospital nurse had charge of me. She seemed so nice and normal that at last I determined to confide in her. My door happened to be ajar, and I heard her talking to some one in the passage. _She was one of them!_

“I think I almost hypnotized myself. After a while, I almost forgot that I was really Jane Finn. I wasn’t even unhappy in the end--just apathetic. And the years went on.

“And then suddenly things seemed to change. There was some talk of sending me to a specialist in Paris. I learnt later that the nurse went to Paris and consulted a specialist, representing herself to be me. He exposed her loss of memory to be fraudulent; but she had taken a note of his methods and reproduced them on me.

“One night I was whisked off to London, back to the house in Soho.

“They sent me in to wait on Mr. Beresford. I was suspicious--I thought it was another trap. But he looked so honest, I could hardly believe it. However, I was careful in all I said, for I knew we could be overheard. There’s a small hole, high up in the wall.

“But on the Sunday afternoon word came that he was to be killed. I thought I’d have time to rush up and get the papers, but I was caught. So I screamed out that he was escaping, and I said I wanted to go back to Marguerite. I shouted the name three times very loud. I knew the others would think I meant Mrs. Vandemeyer, but I hoped it might make Mr. Beresford think of the picture. He’d unhooked one the first day--that’s what made me hesitate to trust him.”

“Then the papers,” said Sir James slowly, “are still at the back of the picture in that room.”

“Yes.” The girl sank back on the sofa exhausted.

Sir James rose and looked at his watch.

“Come,” he said, “we must go at once.”

“To-night?” queried Tuppence, surprised.

“To-morrow may be too late,” said Sir James gravely. “Besides, by going to-night we have the chance of capturing that great man and super-criminal--Mr. Brown! You have been followed here--not a doubt of it. When we leave the house we shall be followed again, but not molested, _for it is Mr. Brown’s plan that we are to lead him_. But the Soho house is under police supervision night and day. When we enter that house, Mr. Brown will not draw back--he will risk all, on the chance of obtaining the spark to fire his mine. And he fancies the risk not great--since he will enter in the guise of a friend!”

Tuppence flushed, then opened her mouth impulsively.

“But there’s something you don’t know--that we haven’t told you.” Her eyes dwelt on Jane in perplexity. “It’s so difficult--if I’m wrong--oh, it would be dreadful.”

“You want me to help you out, eh?” Sir James asked.

“Yes, please. _You_ know who Mr. Brown is, don’t you?”

“Yes,” said Sir James gravely. “At last I do. I have been morally certain of his identity ever since the night of Mrs. Vandemeyer’s mysterious death. Either the chloral was administered by her own hand, which theory I reject utterly, or else it was administered in the brandy you gave her. Only three people touched that brandy--you, Miss Tuppence, I myself, and one other--Mr. Julius Hersheimmer!”

Jane Finn stirred and sat up, regarding the speaker with wide astonished eyes.

“At first, the thing seemed utterly impossible,” Sir James went on. “But you cannot escape from the logic of facts. I took an early opportunity of giving you a hint. Then Mr. Beresford rang me up and told me, what I had already suspected, that the photograph of Miss Jane Finn had never really been out of Mr. Hersheimmer’s possession----”

But the girl interrupted. Springing to her feet, she cried out angrily:

“What do you mean? That Mr. Brown is _Julius?_ Julius--my own cousin!”

“No, Miss Finn,” said Sir James unexpectedly. “Not your cousin. The man who calls himself Julius Hersheimmer is no relation to you whatsoever.”

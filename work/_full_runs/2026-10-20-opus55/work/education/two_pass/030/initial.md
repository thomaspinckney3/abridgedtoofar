AMERICA has always taken tragedy lightly. Too busy to stop the activity of their twenty-million-horse-power society, Americans ignore tragic motives that would have overshadowed the Middle Ages. The world learns to regard assassination as a form of hysteria, and death as neurosis, to be treated by a rest-cure. Three hideous political murders, that would have fattened the Eumenides with horror, have thrown scarcely a shadow on the White House.

The year 1901 was a year of tragedy that seemed to Hay to centre on himself. First came, in summer, the accidental death of his son, Del Hay. Close on the tragedy of his son followed the death of his chief, McKinley, "all the more hideous that we were so sure of his recovery." The world turned suddenly into a graveyard. "I have acquired the funeral habit." "Nicolay is dying. I went to see him yesterday, and he did not know me." Among the letters of condolence was one from Clarence King at Pasadena, "heart-breaking in grace and tenderness--the old King manner." King himself was "simply waiting till nature and the foe have done their struggle." The tragedy of King impressed him intensely. "There you have it in the face!" he said--"the best and brightest man of his generation, with talents immeasurably beyond any of his contemporaries; with industry that has often sickened me to witness it; with everything in his favor but blind luck; hounded by disaster from his cradle, with none of the joy of life to which he was entitled, dying at last, with nameless suffering alone and uncared-for, in a California tavern. Ca vous amuse, la vie?"

The first summons that met Adams, before he had even landed on the pier at New York, December 29, was to Clarence King's funeral. From the funeral he had no gayer road to travel than the one that led to Washington. There a revolution had occurred that must in any case have made the men of his age instantly old. It hurried to the front the generation he had regarded as boys, and it could not fail to break the social ties that had till then held them all together.

Ca vous amuse, la vie? Honestly, the lessons of education were becoming too trite. Hay himself, probably for the first time, felt half glad that Roosevelt should want him to stay in office, if only to save himself the trouble of quitting; but to Adams all was pure loss. On that side, his education had been finished at school. His friends in power were lost, and he knew life too well to risk total wreck by trying to save them.

As far as concerned Roosevelt, the chance was hopeless. To them at sixty-three, Roosevelt at forty-three could not be taken seriously in his old character, and could not be recovered in his new one. Roosevelt, more than any other man living within the range of notoriety, showed the singular primitive quality that belongs to ultimate matter--the quality that mediaeval theology assigned to God--he was pure act. With him wielding unmeasured power with immeasurable energy in the White House, the relation of age to youth--of teacher to pupil--was altogether out of place; and no other was possible. Even Hay's relation was a false one, while Adams's ceased of itself. The wisest courtier that ever lived, Lucius Seneca himself, must have remained in some shade of doubt what advantage he should get from the power of his friend and pupil Nero, until, as a gentleman past sixty, he received Nero's filial invitation to kill himself. Seneca closed the vast circle of his knowledge by learning that a friend in power was a friend lost--a fact very much worth insisting upon. Meanwhile the gray-headed moth that had fluttered through many moth-administrations, and had singed his wings in them all, acquired an instinct of self-preservation. It kept him to the north side of La Fayette Square, and deterred him from hovering between Presidents and Senators.

Those who seek education in the paths of duty are always deceived by the illusion that power in the hands of friends is an advantage to them. Adams had found it an invariable disaster. Power is poison. Its effect on Presidents had always been tragic: an almost insane excitement at first, and a worse reaction afterwards. No mind is so well balanced as to bear the strain of seizing unlimited force without habit or knowledge of it, and of finding it disputed by hungry packs of wolves and hounds whose lives depend on snatching the carrion. Roosevelt enjoyed a singularly direct nature and honest intent, but his first year of Presidency showed chronic excitement that made a friend tremble. The effect of unlimited power on limited mind is worth noting in Presidents, because it must represent the same process in society. The power of self-control must have limit somewhere in face of the control of the infinite.

Roosevelt was lost, but this seemed no reason why Hay and Lodge should also be lost; yet the result was mathematically certain. With Hay, it was only the steady decline of strength. With Lodge it was law of politics. His position as the President's friend and as independent statesman at once was false, and he must be unsure in both relations.

Lodge's importance hung on his position in Massachusetts, and his standing there was highly insecure. Nowhere in America was society so complex or change so rapid. New England standards were various, scarcely reconcilable with each other, and constantly multiplying. State Street and the banks exacted one stamp; the old Congregational clergy another; Harvard College, poor in votes but rich in social influence, a third. The Irish held aloof, and seldom consented to approve any one; the new socialist class promised to become more exclusive than the Irish. No one could represent it faithfully as a whole.

As a type for study, Lodge was more interesting than the President. Roosevelts are born and never can be taught; but Lodge was a creature of teaching--Boston incarnate--the child of his local parentage. An excellent talker and accomplished orator, he could never feel perfectly at ease whatever leg he stood on. He shifted from one sensitive muscle to another, uncertain whether to pose as an uncompromising Yankee; or a pure American; or a patriot among Irish, Germans, or Jews; or a scholar of Harvard College. He was English to the last fibre of his thought, yet at home and happy among the vices and extravagances of Shakespeare; now worshipping, now banning; always intelligent. The usual statesmen flocked in swarms like crows, black and monotonous. Lodge's plumage was varied, and, like his flight, harked back to race.

Adams, too, was Bostonian, and the Bostonian's uncertainty of attitude was as natural to him as to Lodge. His theory and practice were also at variance. In theory only the Greek, the Italian or the French standards had claims to respect, and the barbarism of Shakespeare was as flagrant as to Voltaire. Yet he knew that English disorder approached nearer to truth, if truth existed, than French measure. He read his Shakespeare as the Evangel of conservative Christian anarchy, neither very conservative nor very Christian, but stupendously anarchistic. He made no scruple of defying sequence and denying consistency--but he was not a Senator.

Double standards are inspiration to men of letters, but they are apt to be fatal to politicians. Roosevelt and Lodge were always liable to find the shifty sands of American opinion yield suddenly under their feet. In domestic politics the larger study was lost in the ambitions of fifth-rate men. Foreign affairs, however, dealt only with large units, and made possible with Hay a personal relation that could not be maintained with Roosevelt or Lodge. The work of domestic progress is done by masses of mechanical power, controlled by a score or two of individuals. Most of them are forces as dumb as their dynamos, absorbed in the development or economy of power. Modern politics is, at bottom, a struggle not of men but of forces. The men become every year more and more creatures of force, massed about central power-houses, and they tend to succumb to their own motive forces.

In domestic politics everyone works for an immediate object, in a near horizon. In foreign affairs the outlook is far ahead, over a field as wide as the world. For this reason Adams had always insisted that international relation was the only sure base for a chart of history.

The Secretary of State has always stood as much alone as the historian. He measures forces unknown to party managers, and he has found Congress more or less hostile ever since Congress first sat. Since the first day the Senate existed, it has always intrigued against him whenever he has gone beyond appointing Consuls in Senators' service. As education, this made of Hay the best schoolmaster since 1865. Adams sought only instruction. He wanted to chart the international channel for fifty years to come, and to fix the acceleration of movement in politics since the year 1200.

With infinite effort Hay had induced the Senate, with only six negative votes, to permit Great Britain to renounce, without equivalent, treaty rights she had defended for fifty years. England was won. With France the Senate blocked advance, but England assumed the task and effected the object. The next and far more difficult step was to bring Germany into the combine. At the end of the vista, most unmanageable of all, Russia remained to be satisfied and disarmed. This was McKinleyism: the system of combinations, consolidations and trusts, realized at home and realizable abroad.

A student of the eighteenth century noticed with astonishment that this capitalistic scheme of combining governments, like railways or furnaces, was in effect precisely the socialist scheme of Jaures and Bebel. That John Hay, of all men, should adopt a socialist policy seemed more absurd than conservative Christian anarchy. Yet paradox had become the only orthodoxy in politics as in science. Hay's and McKinley's statesmanship turned on persuading the Kaiser to join what might be called the Coal-power combination. The only possible alternative was a Gun-power combination formed by merging Germany in Russia. Thus Bebel and Jaures, McKinley and Hay, were partners.

The problem was as rigorous as a geometrical demonstration. Hay, master of both theory and practice, knew not only the forces but also the men.

Probably this was the moment of highest knowledge that a scholar could ever reach. Since 1860 education had done its worst to train these two minds to catch every spring of international action. The machinery of politics in several great countries had little to do but supply the last and best information. Education could be carried no further.

Its effects on Adams were grotesque. Never had the proportions of his ignorance looked so appalling. He seemed to know nothing--to be groping in darkness--to be falling forever in space. The worst depth consisted in the assurance that no one knew more. He had the mechanical assurance of certain values, such as the relative intensities of his Coal-powers and the relative inertia of his Gun-powers. Even so, he conceived that had the Czar and the Kaiser and the Mikado turned schoolmasters, like Hay, and taught him all they knew, he would still have known nothing. They knew nothing themselves. Only by comparison of their ignorance could the student measure his own.

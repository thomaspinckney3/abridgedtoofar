OF the year 1862 Henry Adams could never think without a shudder. The war alone did not greatly distress him. He was used to seeing people wade in blood, and he could plainly discern in history that man from the beginning had found his chief amusement in bloodshed. But the ferocious joy of destruction at its best requires that one should kill what one hates, and young Adams neither hated nor wanted to kill his friends the rebels, while he wanted nothing so much as to wipe England off the earth. Never could any good come from that besotted race! Every day the British Government deliberately crowded him one step further into the grave. The Trent Affair showed where Palmerston and Russell stood. The escape of the rebel cruisers from Liverpool was not, in a young man's eyes, the sign of hesitation, but the proof of their fixed intention to intervene. In private no political opponent in England, and few political friends, hesitated to say brutally of Lord John Russell that he lied. This was no great reproach, for, more or less, every statesman lied. The intensity of the private secretary's rage sprang from his belief that Russell's form of defence covered intent to kill. Not for an instant did the Legation draw a free breath. The suspense was hideous and unendurable.

The Minister, no doubt, endured it, but he had support and consideration. His son had nothing to think about but his friends, who were mostly dying under McClellan in the swamps about Richmond, or his enemies, who were exulting in Pall Mall. When the story of the second Bull Run appeared, he could bear it no longer. After a sleepless night, walking up and down his room without reflecting that his father was beneath him, he announced at breakfast his intention to go home into the army. His mother seemed to be less impressed by the announcement than by the walking over her head, which was so unlike her as to surprise her son. Mr. Adams took his son's defection as quietly as he took Bull Run, but his son never got the chance to go. The remonstrances of his brother Charles had much to do with delaying action. Henry also felt, of his own accord, that if he deserted his post in London and found the Capuan comforts he expected in Virginia, where he would have only bullets to wound him, he would never forgive himself. He would be leaving his father and mother alone to be devoured by the wild beasts of the British amphitheatre. This reflection might not have stopped him, but his father's suggestion was decisive. The Minister pointed out that it was too late for him to take part in the actual campaign, and that long before next spring they would all go home together.

The young man had copied too many affidavits about rebel cruisers to miss the point of this argument, so he sat down again to copy some more. Consul Dudley at Liverpool provided a continuous supply. The work was nothing. Not the work, but the play exhausted. The effort of facing a hostile society was bad enough, but that of facing friends was worse. After terrific disasters like the seven days before Richmond and the second Bull Run, friends needed support. A tone of bluff would have been fatal; nothing answers but candor. Yet private secretaries never feel candid, however much they feel the reverse, and therefore they must affect candor. One must read one's Times every morning over one's muffin without reading aloud--"Another disastrous Federal Defeat"; and one might not even indulge in harmless profanity. Self-restraint among friends required much more effort than keeping a quiet face before enemies. Great men were the worst blunderers. One day the private secretary was standing with the crowd in the throne-room, and he smiled at hearing, behind his shoulder, one Cabinet Minister remark gaily to another: "So the Federals have got another licking!" The point of the remark was its truth. Even a private secretary had learned to betray no joy over the "lickings" of an enemy--in the enemy's presence.

London was altogether beside itself on one point. It created a nightmare of its own, and gave it the shape of Abraham Lincoln. Behind this it placed another demon, if possible more devilish, and called it Mr. Seward. Defence was useless; explanation was vain; one could only let the passion exhaust itself. One's best friends were as unreasonable as enemies. The last time Henry Adams saw Thackeray, before his sudden death at Christmas in 1863, was in entering the house of Sir Henry Holland for an evening reception. Thackeray was pulling on his coat downstairs, laughing because he had stumbled into the wrong house. Then his tone changed as he spoke of his--and Adams's--friend, Mrs. Frank Hampton, of South Carolina, whom he had loved as Sally Baxter and painted as Ethel Newcome. She had died of consumption at Columbia while her parents and sister were refused permission to pass through the lines to see her. In speaking of it, Thackeray's voice trembled and his eyes filled with tears. He never doubted that the Federals made a business of harrowing the tenderest feelings of women--particularly of women--in order to punish their opponents. On quite insufficient evidence he burst into violent reproach. At that moment Thackeray, and all London society with him, needed the nervous relief of expressing emotion; for if Mr. Lincoln was not what they said he--was what were they?

For like reason, the members of the Legation kept silence, even in private, under the boorish Scotch jibes of Carlyle. Demolition of one's idols is painful, and Carlyle had been an idol. Not merely the idols fell, but also the habit of faith. If Carlyle, too, was a fraud, what were his scholars and school?

Society as a rule was civil, but one's few friends in society were mere ornament. Henry Adams went his way peacefully enough. He went where he was asked; he was always courteously received; and he held his tongue.

For a thousand reasons, the best diplomatic house in London was Lord Palmerston's, while Lord John Russell's was one of the worst. The Queen herself, in her famous memorandum of August 12, 1850, gave her opinion of Palmerston in words that differed little from Lord John Russell's. Yet the diplomatic standard of trust seemed to be other than the parliamentarian. No professional diplomatists worried about falsehoods. The worst liars were the candid. What diplomatists wanted to know was the motive that lay beyond the expression. In the case of Palmerston, they were unanimous in warning new colleagues that they might expect to be sacrificed by him to any momentary personal object. If he had an object to gain, he would go down to the House of Commons and betray or misrepresent a foreign Minister, without concern for his victim. No one got back on him with a blow equally mischievous--not even the Queen--for, as old Baron Brunnow described him: "C'est une peau de rhinocere!"

Diplomatists have no right to complain of mere lies, but they complain bitterly of traps. Palmerston was believed to lay traps. On the other hand, Lady Palmerston was believed to be good and loyal, and the diplomats and their wives took their troubles to her. Her evenings at home had great vogue. Lady Palmerston was no longer young or handsome. Yet, beyond a doubt, Cambridge House was the best, and perhaps the only political house in London. Its success was due to Lady Palmerston, who never seemed to make an effort beyond a friendly recognition. The world never explains such riddles. The foreigners said only that Lady Palmerston was "sympathique."

The small fry of the Legations were tolerated there, and could at least stand in a corner and look at a bishop or even a duke. No one knew young Adams--not even the lackeys. On the last Saturday evening he ever attended, he gave his name as usual at the foot of the staircase. He was rather disturbed to hear it shouted up as "Mr. Handrew Hadams!" He tried to correct it, and the footman shouted more loudly: "Mr. Hanthony Hadams!" With some temper he repeated the correction, and was finally announced as "Mr. Halexander Hadams," and under this name made his bow for the last time to Lord Palmerston who certainly knew no better.

Far down the staircase one heard Lord Palmerston's laugh as he stood at the door receiving his guests. The laugh was singular, mechanical, wooden, and did not seem to disturb his features. "Ha! ... Ha! ... Ha!" Each was a slow, deliberate ejaculation, as though he meant to say: "Yes! ... Yes! ... Yes!" by way of assurance. It was a laugh of 1810 and the Congress of Vienna. One made the usual bow and received the usual glance of civility. Then one passed on to Lady Palmerston, who was always kind in manner but wasted no remarks.

By some process of personal affiliation, Minister Adams succeeded in persuading himself that he could trust Lord Russell more safely than Lord Palmerston. His son, being young and ill-balanced in temper, thought there was nothing to choose. Minister Adams had much to learn, although with him as well as with his son, the months of education began to count as aeons.

Just as Brunnow predicted, Lord Palmerston made his rush at last, as unexpected as always. It was late one afternoon in June, 1862. The private secretary had returned with the Minister from some social function, and he saw his father pick up a note from his desk and read it in silence. Then he said curtly: "Palmerston wants a quarrel!" Palmerston wanted a quarrel; he must not be gratified; he must be stopped. The matter of quarrel was General Butler's famous woman-order at New Orleans. The motive was the belief in President Lincoln's brutality that had taken such deep root in the British mind. The Minister took for granted that Palmerston meant to score a diplomatic point by producing this note in the House of Commons. If he did this at once, the Minister was lost.

The moment was nervous--as far as the private secretary knew, quite the most critical moment in the records of American diplomacy--but the story belongs to history, not to education. His father succeeded in muzzling Palmerston without a public scandal. That was not enough for a private secretary who liked going to Cambridge House, and was puzzled to reconcile contradictions. The correspondence was conducted feebly on Palmerston's side. He allowed the United States Minister to close it by a refusal to receive further communications from him except through Lord Russell. The step was excessively strong, for it broke off private relations as well as public, and cost even the private secretary his invitations to Cambridge House. Mr. Adams was never more cool. He could conceive no other way of protecting his Government than to force Lord Russell to interpose. He believed that Palmerston's submission and silence were due to Russell. Perhaps he was right; at the time, his son had no doubt of it, though afterwards he felt less sure. Palmerston wanted a quarrel; the motive seemed evident; yet when the quarrel was made, he backed out of it; for some reason it seemed that he did not want it--at least, not then. Incredible as it seemed, he behaved like a well-bred gentleman who felt himself in the wrong. Still, the private secretary would have felt his education in politics more complete had he ever finally made up his mind on one point. Was Palmerston more angry with General Butler, or more annoyed at himself, for committing what was in both cases an unpardonable betise?

The year 1862 was a dark spot in Henry Adams's life, and the education it gave was mostly one that he gladly forgot. As far as he was aware, he made no friends. Yet towards the close of the year he was flattered by an invitation from Monckton Milnes to Fryston. It was one of many acts of charity towards the young that gave Milnes immortality. Milnes made it his business to be kind. A dispirited, disheartened private secretary was exceedingly grateful, and never forgot the kindness. Milnes was never like anybody, and his country parties served his purpose of mixing strange elements. In the winter mists of Yorkshire, his singular guests had nothing to do but astonish each other, if anything could astonish such men. Of the five, Adams alone was tame; he alone added nothing to the wit or humor, except as a listener; but they needed a listener and he was useful. Milnes was the oldest, and perhaps the sanest. He had sought contact with everybody and everything that Europe could offer, and knew it all chiefly as humorous.

The second guest was a quiet, singularly agreeable gentleman of the literary class, whom Milnes called Stirling of Keir. Milnes gave the hint that Stirling was violent only on one point--hatred of Napoleon III. The third guest Adams had already met at Lady Palmerston's, carrying his arm in a sling. He was Lawrence Oliphant, just from Japan, where he had been wounded in the fanatics' attack on the British Legation.

The fourth was a boy, or had the look of one, though in fact he was a year older than Adams himself. He resembled in action a tropical bird, high-crested, long-beaked, quick-moving, with rapid utterance and screams of humor, quite unlike any English lark or nightingale. Milnes introduced him as Mr. Algernon Swinburne. Milnes lingered in Adams's room to add that Swinburne had written some poetry, not yet published, of really extraordinary merit. Adams only wondered what more Milnes would discover, and whether by chance he could discover merit in a private secretary. He was capable of it.

At dinner, conversation ran first to Oliphant, who told his dramatic story simply, until Milnes thought it time to bring Swinburne out. Then, at last, if never before, Adams acquired education. What he had sought so long, he found; but he was none the wiser; only the more astonished. In 1862 smoking was forbidden indoors. But Monckton Milnes was a licensed libertine, and he let his guests smoke in Adams's bedroom, since Adams was an American-German barbarian ignorant of manners. There, after dinner, all sat--or lay--till far into the night, listening to the rush of Swinburne's talk. In a long experience, before or after, no one ever approached it.

The others could not believe his incredible memory and knowledge of literature, classic, mediaeval, and modern. He could recite a play of Sophocles or a play of Shakespeare, forward or backward, from end to beginning; or Dante, or Villon, or Victor Hugo. They knew not what to make of his rhetorical recitation of his own unpublished ballads, which he declaimed as though they were books of the Iliad. Swinburne was millions of ages far from them, yet he united them by his humor even more than by his poetry.

Late at night, Stirling of Keir wanted to take with him to his chamber a copy of "Queen Rosamund," the only volume Swinburne had then published. Adams offered to light him down with his solitary bedroom candle. All the way, Stirling was ejaculating explosions of wonder. At the foot of the stairs he paused, and burst out: "He's a cross between the devil and the Duke of Argyll!"

To appreciate the full merit of this description, a judicious critic should have known both, and Henry Adams knew only one. But he understood that to a Scotchman the likeness meant something quite portentous, supernatural, and what the French call moyenageux. The idea that one has actually met a real genius dawns slowly on a Boston mind, but it made entry at last.

Then came the sad reaction, not from Swinburne, whose genius never was in doubt, but from the Boston mind, which in its uttermost flights was never moyenageux. To Swinburne, Adams could be no more than a worm. The quality of genius was an education almost ultimate, for one touched there the limits of the human mind on that side; but one could only receive; one had nothing to give--nothing even to offer.

Swinburne tested him then and there by one of his favorite tests--Victor Hugo. Adams tried to evade Swinburne's vehement insistence by parading an affection for Alfred de Musset. Swinburne would have none of it; de Musset was unequal; he did not sustain himself on the wing. Swinburne tried him again on Walter Savage Landor, and Adams's failure was equally gross. When forced to despair, he had to admit that both Hugo and Landor bored him. Nothing more was needed. One who could feel neither Hugo nor Landor was lost.

The sentence was just and Adams never appealed from it. He knew he was no companion for Swinburne. Even Monckton Milnes, who felt the splendors of Hugo and Landor, was almost as helpless as an American private secretary in personal contact with them. Ten years afterwards Adams met him at the Geneva Conference, fresh from Paris, bubbling with delight at a call he had made on Hugo.

"I was shown into a large room," Milnes said, "with women and men seated in chairs against the walls, and Hugo at one end throned. No one spoke. At last Hugo raised his voice solemnly, and uttered the words: 'Quant a moi, je crois en Dieu!' Silence followed. Then a woman responded as if in deep meditation: 'Chose sublime! un Dieu qui croit en Dieu!'"

With the best of will, one could not do this in London. Yet even a private secretary was not wholly wanting in instinct. As soon as he reached town he hurried to Pickering's for a copy of "Queen Rosamund." He would have offered Swinburne a solemn worship as Milnes's female offered Hugo, if it would have pleased the poet. Unfortunately it was worthless.

The three young men returned to London, and each went his own way. "The London season," Milnes used to say, "is a season for making acquaintances and losing friends." Adams sat next to Swinburne once more, at a dinner of the Authors' Fund over which Milnes presided. Swinburne was famous then, but no nearer, and they never met again. The only record of the visit may perhaps exist still in the registers of the St. James's Club. Immediately afterwards Milnes proposed Henry Adams for membership, with seconders a little singular for variety. On the whole, the list suggested that the private secretary was getting on.

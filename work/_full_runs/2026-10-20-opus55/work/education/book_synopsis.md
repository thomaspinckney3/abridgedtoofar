Henry Brooks Adams, great-grandson of John Adams and grandson of John Quincy Adams, is born in 1838 on Beacon Hill in Boston. Writing of himself in the third person as a "manikin" on which the garments of education are draped, he recounts a childhood divided between winter Boston, with its discipline and law, and summer Quincy, with its liberty and the eighteenth-century world of his grandfather. That world ends in 1848 with the President's death on the floor of the House. His father, Charles Francis Adams, becomes the dominant influence on him: a man of perfectly balanced mind who leads the antislavery Free Soil movement alongside Charles Sumner, whom the boy worships. A trip to slaveholding Washington and Mount Vernon in 1850 leaves him with an early sense of moral contradiction. So does the coalition bargain that makes Sumner a senator, a corrupt arrangement from which his own side profits.

Harvard leaves him "an autobiographical blank," though he befriends Roony Lee and becomes Class Orator. In 1858 he goes to Berlin to study Civil Law, abandons it after a single lecture, and drifts through Dresden, Italy, and Paris as an accidental tourist. Along the way he discovers Beethoven in a beer garden, sits brooding over Gibbon on the steps of Ara Coeli in Rome, and meets Garibaldi in revolutionary Palermo. He returns home in 1860 and serves as his father's private secretary in Washington during the secession winter. There he meets John Hay and watches Sumner break with his family, which teaches him that "a friend in power is a friend lost."

When Lincoln appoints his father Minister to England in 1861, Henry follows as private secretary and spends seven years in London. He arrives to find Britain recognizing Confederate belligerency, and he endures the Trent Affair, the escape of the Alabama, and Gladstone's declaration that Jefferson Davis had "made a nation." He watches his father hold firm against Palmerston and Russell, and the crisis culminates in 1863 with Minister Adams's warning, "this is war," which stops the Laird rams. Decades later, published memoirs reveal how wrongly he had judged the British statesmen's motives. To Adams, that discovery proves his diplomatic education was a long mistake. Meanwhile he gains a footing in English society through Monckton Milnes, hears Swinburne at Fryston, makes lifelong friends with the Milnes Gaskells at Wenlock Abbey, dabbles in art and geology under Sir Charles Lyell, and finds in the ancient fish Pteraspis a challenge to Darwinian evolution.

Back in America in 1868, he tries journalism in Washington and hopes to champion reform under President Grant. Grant's Cabinet appointments and Jay Gould's gold conspiracy crush that hope, and Adams is driven into opposition. In 1870 his sister dies horribly of tetanus in Italy, and for the first time he sees Nature as a chaos of purposeless force. At his family's urging he accepts a Harvard professorship in medieval history and the editorship of the North American Review, and he judges both failures. In 1871 he meets the brilliant geologist Clarence King in the Rockies, and with that friendship his formal education ends.

The narrative then skips twenty years, passing silently over his marriage and his wife's death. It resumes in 1892, when Adams, weary, returns from the South Seas to his house on Lafayette Square beside Hay's. The Saint-Gaudens memorial figure at Rock Creek stands as an unspoken monument to his grief. The panic of 1893 nearly bankrupts him, and the Chicago Exposition shows him the dynamo. The final victory of the gold standard convinces him that America has chosen capitalism and mechanical concentration. He travels restlessly, watches Hay become Secretary of State and bring England and Germany into an "Atlantic combine," and begins studying twelfth-century Normandy with the Lodges.

At the Paris Exposition of 1900, guided by Samuel Langley, Adams confronts the dynamo as a modern symbol of infinite force. He sets it against the Virgin of Chartres, the energy that once built cathedrals and unified medieval thought. Radium and the new physics of Karl Pearson and Poincaré persuade him that science has abandoned unity for multiplicity and chaos. He resolves to measure history as motion between two fixed points: thirteenth-century unity, recorded in *Mont-Saint-Michel and Chartres*, and twentieth-century multiplicity, recorded in this book. That project yields his "Dynamic Theory of History," in which man is pulled by natural forces, and a "Law of Acceleration," modeled on the doubling of coal power, which predicts that the human mind must soon leap into a new phase or be overwhelmed.

King dies in 1901, and Hay, worn out by office and the Russo-Japanese crisis, dies in 1905 before he can make peace. With all three friends gone, Adams accepts that his education is over. He imagines that they might return together in 1938, their centenary, to find a world "that sensitive and timid natures could regard without a shudder."

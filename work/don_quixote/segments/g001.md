## Chapter 1

Cide Hamete Benengeli records that, after carrying Don Quixote home enchanted on the ox-cart, the curate and the barber stayed away near a month, visiting only the niece and the housekeeper and charging them to ply their master with comforting meats “good for the heart and the brain, whence, it was plain to see, all his misfortune proceeded.” The women said they did so, and that at whiles he even seemed in his right mind; at which the pair rejoiced, and resolved to visit him, agreeing, however, to avoid all mention of knight-errantry.

They found him propped in bed in a green baize waistcoat and a red Toledo cap, so withered that he looked a mummy; yet he spoke naturally and in well-chosen language. They fell to “State-craft and systems of government,” correcting and reforming at their ease, until they had, as it were, thrust the State into a furnace and drawn it out quite other than it went in; and in all this Don Quixote showed such good sense that they were convinced he was whole in his wits.

But the curate, changing his plan to try him thoroughly, spoke of news from court: the Turk was descending with a powerful fleet, all Christendom in fear, and his Majesty had provided for Naples, Sicily, and Malta. “His Majesty,” said Don Quixote, “has acted like a prudent warrior; but if my advice were taken I would recommend him to adopt a measure which at present, no doubt, his Majesty is very far from thinking of.” Pressed for it, he fenced, fearing others might run away with the thanks; but, upon oath of secrecy, he said: “What more has his Majesty to do but to command, by public proclamation, all the knights-errant that are scattered over Spain to assemble on a fixed day in the capital; for even if no more than half a dozen come, there may be one among them who alone will suffice to destroy the entire might of the Turk. Is it any new thing for a single knight-errant to demolish an army of two hundred thousand men, as if they all had but one throat? If only the famous Don Belianis were alive now, or any of the innumerable progeny of Amadis of Gaul! But God will provide someone; and I say no more.”

“Alas!” cried the niece, “may I die if my master does not want to turn knight-errant again.” “A knight-errant I shall die,” he replied; “let the Turk come when he likes; once more I say, God knows what I mean.”

Here the barber begged leave to tell a Seville tale. In the madhouse there, a licentiate, kept as mad by covetous kinsfolk, wrote so wisely to the Archbishop that a chaplain was sent to examine him. He spoke an hour with coherence so great the chaplain resolved to free him. Dressed in his own decent clothes, the licentiate begged to take leave of his “comrades the madmen.” At a cage he exhorted one to hope; whereon another madman, stark naked on a mat, cried out that he, Jupiter, for the crime Seville was committing, would punish it with three years’ drought. “Attach no importance to what this madman has said,” said our licentiate; “for if he is Jupiter and will not send rain, I, who am Neptune, the father and god of the waters, will rain as often as it pleases me.” “For all that, Señor Neptune,” said the chaplain, half-ashamed amid the laughter, “it will not do to vex Señor Jupiter; remain where you are.” And they stripped him again; and that was the end.

“So that’s the story, master barber,” said Don Quixote, “that came in so pat? Master shaver, master shaver! how blind is he who cannot see through a sieve. Comparisons of wit with wit, valour with valour, beauty with beauty, birth with birth, are always odious. I am not Neptune, the god of the waters, nor an astute man; my only endeavour is to convince the world of the mistake it makes in not reviving the happy time when the order of knight-errantry was in the field. With the knights of these days it is the damask and brocade they wear that rustle, not the chain mail of their armour; no knight now-a-days sleeps in the open field, in full panoply; no one, issuing from the wood, treads the barren shore, and finding on the beach a little bark without oars, sail, mast, or tackling, in the intrepidity of his heart flings himself into it and commits himself to the wrathful billows, that one moment lift him to heaven and the next plunge him into the depths; and leaping ashore in a remote land has adventures to be written, not on parchment, but on brass. Now sloth triumphs over energy, indolence over exertion, vice over virtue, arrogance over courage, and theory over practice in arms, which flourished only in the golden ages and in knights-errant. For tell me, who was more virtuous and more valiant than Amadis of Gaul? Who more discreet than Palmerin of England? Who more gracious and easy than Tirante el Blanco? Who more courtly than Lisuarte of Greece? Who more slashed or slashing than Don Belianis? Who more intrepid than Perion of Gaul? Who more ready to face danger than Felixmarte of Hircania? Who more sincere than Esplandian? Who more impetuous than Don Cirongilio of Thrace? Who more bold than Rodamonte? Who more prudent than King Sobrino? Who more daring than Reinaldos? Who more invincible than Roland? and who more gallant and courteous than Ruggiero, from whom the dukes of Ferrara are descended, according to Turpin in his Cosmography? These, or such as these, would carry out my plan, and his Majesty would be well served and save great expense, and the Turk would be left tearing his beard. And so I will stay where I am; and if Jupiter will not send rain, here am I, and I will rain when I please. I say this that Master Basin may know that I understand him.”

The curate then said he had a doubt: could he persuade himself that the whole pack of knights-errant named were truly persons of flesh and blood? “That,” said Don Quixote, “is another mistake. I can almost say I have with my own eyes seen Amadis of Gaul; lofty, fair, with a handsome though black beard; gentle and stern in countenance, sparing of words, slow to anger, quick to put it away. By their deeds and dispositions, with the aid of sound philosophy, one may deduce their features.”

“How big was Morgante?” asked the barber. “Scripture shows there were giants: Goliath seven cubits and a half; bones in Sicily prove men as tall as towers. Yet Morgante I suspect not over-tall; he often slept under a roof.”

“What of Reinaldos and Roland?” “Reinaldos was broad-faced, ruddy, with roguish prominent eyes, punctilious and touchy, friend of scapegraces. Roland, or Rotolando, of middle height, broad-shouldered, rather bow-legged, swarthy, red-bearded, hairy, severe of countenance; a man of few words, very polite and well-bred.”

“No wonder,” said the curate, “the fair Angelica left him for the gentle Medoro.” “That Angelica,” returned Don Quixote, “was a giddy damsel; she scorned a thousand valiant gentlemen and took up with a smooth-faced sprig. The famous Ariosto, not caring to sing her adventures after her contemptible surrender, dropped her where he says:

How she received the sceptre of Cathay,
Some bard of defter quill may sing some day;

and poets are vates, diviners; for since then an Andalusian poet has lamented her tears, and a rare Castilian has sung her beauty.” Asked if none had satirized her, he said poets scorned might have, “but up to the present I have not heard of any defamatory verse against the Lady Angelica, who turned the world upside down.”

At this the housekeeper and the niece cried out in the courtyard, and all ran to see what the matter was.

## Chapter 2

The outcry came from the niece and the housekeeper, who held the door against Sancho Panza. “What does the vagabond want here? Be off to thy own house, brother; it is thou, and no one else, that deludest my master and leadest him astray, and takest him tramping about the country.” “Devil’s own housekeeper!” cried Sancho; “it is I who am deluded, and led astray, and taken tramping, and not thy master. He enticed me by a trick, promising me an island, which I am still waiting for.” “May evil islands choke thee,” said the niece. “What are islands? Is it something to eat, glutton that thou art?” “Not to eat,” said Sancho, “but to govern and rule, better than four cities or four judgeships at court.” “Go govern thy house and dig thy seed-patch,” said the housekeeper, “and give over looking for islands or shylands.”

Don Quixote, fearing Sancho would blurt out mischief not to his credit, called him in, making the women hold their tongues. The curate and the barber took their leave, despairing of his recovery when they saw how wedded he was to his chivalries. “You will see,” said the curate, “our gentleman will be off when we least think of it.” “I do not so much wonder at the knight’s madness,” said the barber, “as at the squire’s simplicity about the island.”

Shut in with Sancho, Don Quixote said, “It grieves me thou shouldst say I took thee from thy cottage, when thou knowest I did not remain in mine. We sallied forth together, and have had the same fortune; if they blanketed thee once, they belaboured me a hundred times—that is the only advantage I have of thee.” “Reasonable,” said Sancho; “for by what your worship says, misfortunes belong more properly to knights-errant than to their squires.” “Thou art mistaken,” said Don Quixote; “quando caput dolet—” “I don’t understand any language but my own,” said Sancho. “When the head suffers, all the members suffer; I am thy head, thou a part of me.” “It should be so,” said Sancho; “but when I was blanketed as a member, my head was on the other side of the wall, looking on, and did not feel any pain. If the members are obliged to feel the sufferings of the head, it should be obliged to feel theirs.”

Putting that aside, Don Quixote begged to know what the village said of him: common folk, hidalgos, caballeros—his valour, achievements, courtesy, and his task of reviving chivalry. “Tell me the naked truth.” “With all my heart,” said Sancho, “provided your worship be not vexed. The common people count you a mighty great madman, and me no less a fool. The hidalgos say that, not keeping within the bounds of your quality, you have assumed the Don, and made a knight of yourself at a jump, with four vine-stocks and a couple of acres, and never a shirt to your back. The caballeros say they will not have hidalgos setting up in opposition, particularly squire hidalgos who polish their own shoes and darn their black stockings with green silk. As to your valour, courtesy, and task, some say, ‘mad but droll;’ others, ‘valiant but unlucky;’ others, ‘courteous but meddling.’”

“Wherever virtue exists eminently it is persecuted,” said Don Quixote, citing Julius Cæsar ambitious, Alexander a drunkard, Hercules luxurious, Don Galaor over-quarrelsome, and of his brother that he was lachrymose. “Among such calumnies, mine may pass.”

“There’s the tail to be skinned yet,” said Sancho. “Last night the son of Bartholomew Carrasco came home bachelor from Salamanca, and he told me that your worship’s history is already abroad in books, with the title THE INGENIOUS GENTLEMAN DON QUIXOTE OF LA MANCHA; he says they mention me by my own name of Sancho Panza, and the lady Dulcinea del Toboso too, and divers things that happened to us alone; I crossed myself to think how the historian knew them.” “No doubt some sage enchanter,” said Don Quixote. “What! a sage and an enchanter? Why, the bachelor Samson Carrasco says the author is called Cide Hamete Berengena.” “That is a Moorish name,” said Don Quixote. “May be so,” said Sancho; “for I have heard say the Moors are great lovers of berengenas.” “Thou must have mistaken it,” said Don Quixote; “Cide means ‘Lord’ in Arabic.” “Very like,” said Sancho; “shall I fetch the bachelor?” “Thou wilt do me a pleasure,” said Don Quixote; and Sancho went to bring him, and they returned for a very droll colloquy.

## Chapter 3

While waiting for the bachelor, Don Quixote pondered how his deeds could already be in print, the blood of the enemies he had slain not yet dry. A sage, friend or foe, by magic might have given them to the press: a friend to exalt them, an enemy to bring them to naught. If the history existed, being of a knight-errant it must be “grandiloquent, lofty, imposing, grand and true.” Yet the title showed “Cide,” and “no truth is to be looked for from Moors,” cheats and schemers; he feared they might have handled his love affairs indecorously, to the prejudice of Dulcinea’s purity. Wrapped thus in thought he was found by Sancho and Samson Carrasco, a sallow, merry wag of four-and-twenty, round-faced, flat-nosed, wide-mouthed, who fell on his knees: “Let me kiss your mightiness’s hand, Señor Don Quixote of La Mancha; by the habit of St. Peter that I wear, though I have no more than the first four orders, your worship is one of the most famous knights-errant there have ever been. A blessing on Cide Hamete Benengeli, who has written the history of your great deeds, and a double blessing on him who translated it into our Castilian vulgar tongue for the universal entertainment of the people!”

“So, then, it is true,” said Don Quixote, “that there is a history of me, and that it was a Moor and a sage who wrote it?” “So true,” said Samson, “that my belief is there are more than twelve thousand volumes in print this very day. Ask Portugal, Barcelona, and Valencia, where it has been printed; they say it is being printed at Antwerp; there will not be a country or language without a translation.”

“One of the things that ought to give most pleasure to a virtuous and eminent man,” said Don Quixote, “is to find himself in his lifetime in print and in type, familiar in people’s mouths with a good name.” “If it goes by good name,” said the bachelor, “your worship bears away the palm; the Moor in his tongue and the Christian in his have set before us your gallantry, high courage, fortitude, patience, and the purity and continence of the platonic loves of your worship and my lady Doña Dulcinea del Toboso—” “I never heard my lady Dulcinea called Doña,” put in Sancho; “nothing more than the lady Dulcinea del Toboso; so here already the history is wrong.” “No matter,” said Samson. “Certainly not,” said Don Quixote; “but tell me, señor bachelor, what deeds of mine are made most of?” “Tastes differ,” said he; “some swear by the windmills you took for Briareuses; others by the fulling mills; one cries up the two armies that proved two droves of sheep; another that of the dead body to Segovia; a third says the liberation of the galley slaves is best; a fourth that nothing comes up to the Benedictine giants, and the battle with the valiant Biscayan.” “Does the adventure with the Yanguesans come in,” asked Sancho, “when Rocinante went hankering after dainties?” “The sage has left nothing in the ink-bottle,” said Samson; “he tells all, even the capers that worthy Sancho cut in the blanket.” “I cut no capers in the blanket,” said Sancho; “in the air I did, and more than I liked.”

“There is no human history,” said Don Quixote, “without its ups and downs.” “For all that,” said Samson, “some wish the author had left out a few cudgellings inflicted on Señor Don Quixote.” “That’s where the truth of the history comes in,” said Sancho. “Yet,” said Don Quixote, “events that do not affect the truth, if they tend to bring the hero into contempt, might have been passed over. It is one thing to write as a poet, another as a historian; the poet may sing things not as they were but as they ought to have been; the historian must write them as they were.” “If this Moor tells the truth,” said Sancho, “mine are to be found; for they never took the measure of his worship’s shoulders without doing the same for my whole body.”

“Some,” said Samson, smiling, “would rather hear you talk than the cleverest in the book; though there are those who say you showed yourself over-credulous in believing in the government of that island offered by your master.” “There is still sunshine on the wall,” said Don Quixote; “with years, Sancho will be fitter to govern.” “By God, master,” said Sancho, “the island I cannot govern with the years I have, I’ll not govern with Methuselah’s; the difficulty is the said island keeps its distance; not that there is any want of head in me.” “Leave it to God,” said Don Quixote. “If it be God’s will,” said Samson, “there will not be want of a thousand islands, much less one. Only, those that govern islands must at least know grammar.” “I could manage the gram well enough,” said Sancho; “but for the mar I have neither leaning nor liking.”

“One fault they find,” said the bachelor, “is that the author inserted a novel, ‘The Ill-advised Curiosity;’ good in itself, but out of place.” “Then,” said Don Quixote, “the author of my history was no sage, but some ignorant chatterer, who, in a haphazard way, set about writing it, just as Orbaneja of Úbeda painted; when asked what he was painting, he answered, ‘What it may turn out.’ Sometimes he would paint a cock so unlike he had to write beside it in Gothic letters, ‘This is a cock;’ and so it will be with my history, which will require a commentary.” “No fear,” said Samson; “it is so plain the children turn its leaves, the young read it, the grown understand it, the old praise it; there is not a lord’s ante-chamber without a Don Quixote; in short, it is the most delightful and least injurious entertainment yet seen, for not a semblance of an immodest word is in it.” “To write otherwise,” said Don Quixote, “would not be to write truth, but falsehood; historians who have recourse to falsehood ought to be burned, like those who coin false money. I know not why he had recourse to novels and irrelevant stories, when he had so much to write in mine; no doubt he went by the proverb ‘with straw or with hay;’ for by merely setting forth my thoughts, sighs, tears, lofty purposes and enterprises, he might have made a volume larger than all the works of El Tostado. To write histories or any books there is need of great judgment; to give graceful pleasantry is the gift of great geniuses; the cleverest in comedy is the clown, for he who would make people take him for a fool, must not be one. History is in a measure sacred, for it should be true; where the truth is, there God is.” “There is no book so bad but it has something good in it,” said the bachelor. They spoke of how printed works, examined at leisure, show faults; fame brings envy; fault-finders should be more lenient, remembering aliquando bonus dormitat Homerus; it is impossible to please all—stultorum infinitum est numerus.

“Some,” added Samson, “charge the author with forgetting to say who stole Sancho’s Dapple; it is only to be inferred he was stolen, and a little farther on Sancho is on the same ass with no reappearance told; and they say he forgot what Sancho did with the hundred crowns he found in the Sierra Morena; many would be glad to know.” “I am not in humour now for accounts,” said Sancho; “there’s a sinking of the stomach on me, and unless I doctor it with a couple of sups of the old stuff it will put me on the thorn of Santa Lucia. After dinner I’ll answer you and the world.” And without more words he made off home.

Don Quixote begged the bachelor to do penance with him; he stayed; a couple of young pigeons were added to the fare; they talked chivalry, slept the siesta; Sancho returned, and their conversation was resumed.

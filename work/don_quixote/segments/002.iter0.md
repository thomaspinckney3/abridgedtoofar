OF THE INTERVIEW THE CURATE AND THE BARBER HAD WITH DON QUIXOTE ABOUT HIS MALADY

Cide Hamete Benengeli, in the Second Part and third sally of Don Quixote, says that the curate and the barber kept away from him nearly a month, lest they should revive what had passed; they did, however, visit his niece and housekeeper, charging them to tend him carefully with such comforting things as were good for the heart and the brain, whence, it was plain, all his misfortune proceeded. The women declared they did so, and that now and then their master showed signs of being in his right mind; whereat the curate and the barber were satisfied, concluding they had acted wisely in carrying him off enchanted on the ox-cart, as described at the end of the First Part. They resolved to visit him, to test the improvement, yet to avoid all touch of knight-errantry.

They found him sitting up in bed, in a green baize waistcoat and a red Toledo cap, so withered and dried he seemed a mummy; but he received them kindly, answered for his health, and spoke of himself very naturally and in well-chosen language. They fell to discussing what they call State-craft and systems of government, correcting this abuse and condemning that, reforming one practice and abolishing another; so completely did they remodel the State that it seemed thrust into a furnace and taken out another thing; and on all they dealt with, Don Quixote spoke with such good sense that they were convinced he was quite recovered.

The niece and housekeeper, present, thanked God to see their master so clear in his mind; the curate, however, changing his plan, resolved to test him thoroughly, and came at last to news from the capital: it was certain, he said, that the Turk was coming down with a powerful fleet—none knew his purpose—and all Christendom was in apprehension; his Majesty had provided for the safety of Naples, Sicily, and Malta. Don Quixote replied, “His Majesty has acted like a prudent warrior; but if my advice were taken I would recommend him to adopt a measure which at present, no doubt, his Majesty is very far from thinking of.” He would not, he said, tell it there and then, lest it reach the lords of the council and another carry off the thanks; the barber swore not to repeat it “to King, Rook or earthly man,” and the curate went security with “my profession, which is to keep secrets.” “Ods body!” said Don Quixote; “what more has his Majesty to do but to command, by public proclamation, all the knights-errant that are scattered over Spain to assemble on a fixed day in the capital; for even if no more than half a dozen come, there may be one among them who alone will suffice to destroy the entire might of the Turk. If only (in an evil hour for me) the famous Don Belianis were alive now, or anyone of the innumerable progeny of Amadis of Gaul! But God will have regard for his people, and will provide someone; God knows what I mean, and I say no more.” “Alas!” cried the niece, “may I die if my master does not want to turn knight-errant again.” “A knight-errant I shall die,” answered Don Quixote; “and let the Turk come down or go up when he likes—once more I say, God knows what I mean.”

Here the barber begged leave to tell a short story that came pat to the purpose. It was of a graduate of Osuna, a licentiate confined for madness in the Seville madhouse, who wrote so sensibly to the Archbishop for release that a chaplain was sent to inquire. The governor warned that though the man often spoke like one highly intelligent, he at last broke into nonsense that outweighed the rest; but the chaplain, after long discourse, believed him sane, procured his own clothes for him, and agreed to take him before the Archbishop. The licentiate asked leave, in charity, to take farewell of his comrades the madmen, and, going with the chaplain and others, addressed a furious one with pious counsel—“Have strong hope and trust in God… all this madness of ours comes of having the stomach empty and the brains full of wind… despondency breaks down health and brings on death.” Another madman, naked on a mat, cried out, asked who was going away cured, and, on hearing it, swore “by Jupiter, whose majesty I represent on earth,” that for this crime Seville should have no rain for three whole years. “Attach no importance,” said our licentiate to the chaplain; “if he is Jupiter and will not send rain, I, who am Neptune, the father and god of the waters, will rain as often as it pleases me.” At this they laughed; the chaplain, half-ashamed, said, “For all that, Señor Neptune, it will not do to vex Señor Jupiter; remain where you are, and some other day we will come back for you.” So they stripped the licentiate again, and left him where he was; and that was the end.

“So that’s the story, master barber,” said Don Quixote, “which came in so pat to the purpose that you could not help telling it? Master shaver, master shaver! how blind is he who cannot see through a sieve. I am not Neptune, nor do I try to pass for an astute man; my endeavour is to convince the world of the mistake it makes in not reviving the happy time when the order of knight-errantry was in the field. But our depraved age does not deserve such a blessing as those ages enjoyed, when knights-errant took upon their shoulders the defence of kingdoms, the protection of damsels, the succour of orphans and minors, the chastisement of the proud, and the recompense of the humble. With the knights of these days it is the damask, brocade, and rich stuffs they wear, that rustle as they go, not the chain mail of their armour; no knight now-a-days sleeps in the open field, and in full panoply; no one now, issuing from the wood, treads the barren, lonely shore of the sea—mostly a tempestuous and stormy one—and, finding on the beach a little bark without oars, sail, mast, or tackling of any kind, in the intrepidity of his heart flings himself into it and commits himself to the wrathful billows of the deep sea, that one moment lift him up to heaven and the next plunge him into the depths; and opposing his breast to the irresistible gale, finds himself, when he least expects it, three thousand leagues and more away, and leaping ashore in a remote and unknown land has adventures that deserve to be written, not on parchment, but on brass. But now sloth triumphs over energy, indolence over exertion, vice over virtue, arrogance over courage, and theory over practice in arms, which flourished and shone only in the golden ages and in knights-errant. For tell me, who was more virtuous and more valiant than the famous Amadis of Gaul? who more discreet than Palmerin of England? who more gracious than Tirante el Blanco? who more courtly than Lisuarte of Greece? who more slashed than Don Belianis? who more intrepid than Perion of Gaul? who readier to face danger than Felixmarte of Hircania? who more sincere than Esplandian? who more impetuous than Don Cirongilio of Thrace? who more bold than Rodamonte? who more prudent than King Sobrino? who more daring than Reinaldos? who more invincible than Roland? and who more gallant and courteous than Ruggiero, from whom the dukes of Ferrara descend, according to Turpin’s ‘Cosmography’? These, and many more, were knights-errant, the light and glory of chivalry. These, or such as these, I would have to carry out my plan; his Majesty would be well served and save great expense, and the Turk would be left tearing his beard. And so I will stay where I am, as the chaplain does not take me away; and if Jupiter, as the barber has told us, will not send rain, here am I, and I will rain when I please. I say this that Master Basin may know that I understand him.”

The barber protested he meant no offence; “As to whether I ought to be vexed or not,” returned Don Quixote, “I myself am the best judge.”

“I would gladly be relieved of a doubt,” said the curate, “that worries my conscience: all I can do, I cannot persuade myself that the whole pack of knights-errant you have mentioned were really persons of flesh and blood; I suspect fiction and fable.” “That is another mistake,” replied Don Quixote; “the truth is so clear that I can almost say I have with my own eyes seen Amadis of Gaul, a man of lofty stature, fair complexion, with a handsome though black beard, of a countenance between gentle and stern, sparing of words, slow to anger, and quick to put it away; and as I have depicted Amadis, so could I portray the rest, for by their deeds and dispositions it is possible, with sound philosophy, to deduce their features, complexion, and stature.”

“How big, in your worship’s opinion, may the giant Morgante have been?” asked the barber. “With regard to giants,” said Don Quixote, “opinions differ; but Holy Scripture shows there were such—Goliath, seven cubits and a half in height. Likewise, in Sicily leg-bones and arm-bones have been found so large that their owners were giants, as tall as towers; geometry puts it beyond doubt. For all that, I suspect Morgante cannot have been very tall, because the history in which his deeds are mentioned says he frequently slept under a roof; and he found houses to contain him.”

The curate, enjoying such nonsense, asked his notion of Reinaldos of Montalban, and of Don Roland and the rest of the Twelve Peers. “As for Reinaldos,” said Don Quixote, “he was broad-faced, of ruddy complexion, with roguish and somewhat prominent eyes, excessively punctilious and touchy, and given to the society of thieves and scapegraces. Roland, or Rotolando, or Orlando, was of middle height, broad-shouldered, rather bow-legged, swarthy-complexioned, red-bearded, with a hairy body and a severe expression, a man of few words, but very polite and well-bred.” “If Roland was not a more graceful person,” said the curate, “no wonder the fair Lady Angelica rejected him for that budding-bearded little Moor, Medoro.” “That Angelica,” returned Don Quixote, “was a giddy damsel, flighty and somewhat wanton; she scorned a thousand gentlemen of valour and wisdom, and took up with a smooth-faced sprig of a page, without fortune or fame, save such reputation for gratitude as his affection got him. The great poet who sang her, the famous Ariosto, not caring to sing her adventures after her contemptible surrender, dropped her where he says:

How she received the sceptre of Cathay,
Some bard of defter quill may sing some day;

and this was a kind of prophecy, for poets are also called vates; and its truth was made plain, for since then a famous Andalusian poet has lamented and sung her tears, and another rare Castilian has sung her beauty.”

“Tell me,” said the barber, “has no poet written a satire on this Lady Angelica?” “I can well believe,” replied Don Quixote, “that if Sacripante or Roland had been poets they would have given her a trimming; for poets scorned by their ladies avenge themselves in satires and libels—a vengeance unworthy of generous hearts; but up to the present I have not heard of any defamatory verse against the Lady Angelica, who turned the world upside down.”

At this moment they heard the housekeeper and the niece exclaiming aloud in the courtyard, and at the noise they all ran out.

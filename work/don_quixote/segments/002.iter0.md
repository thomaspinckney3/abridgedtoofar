Cide Hamete Benengeli records that, after carrying Don Quixote home enchanted on the ox-cart, the curate and the barber stayed away near a month, visiting only the niece and the housekeeper and charging them to ply their master with comforting meats “good for the heart and the brain, whence, it was plain to see, all his misfortune proceeded.” The women said they did so, and that at whiles he even seemed in his right mind; at which the pair rejoiced, and resolved to visit him, agreeing, however, to avoid all mention of knight-errantry.

They found him propped in bed in a green baize waistcoat and a red Toledo cap, so withered that he looked a mummy; yet he spoke naturally and in well-chosen language. They fell to “State-craft and systems of government,” correcting and reforming at their ease, until they had, as it were, thrust the State into a furnace and drawn it out quite other than it went in; and in all this Don Quixote showed such good sense that they were convinced he was whole in his wits.

But the curate, changing his plan to try him thoroughly, spoke of news from court: the Turk was descending with a powerful fleet, all Christendom in fear, and his Majesty had provided for Naples, Sicily, and Malta. “His Majesty,” said Don Quixote, “has acted like a prudent warrior; but if my advice were taken I would recommend him to adopt a measure which at present, no doubt, his Majesty is very far from thinking of.” Pressed for it, he fenced, fearing others might run away with the thanks; but, upon oath of secrecy, he said: “What more has his Majesty to do but to command, by public proclamation, all the knights-errant that are scattered over Spain to assemble on a fixed day in the capital; for even if no more than half a dozen come, there may be one among them who alone will suffice to destroy the entire might of the Turk. Is it any new thing for a single knight-errant to demolish an army of two hundred thousand men, as if they all had but one throat? If only the famous Don Belianis were alive now, or any of the innumerable progeny of Amadis of Gaul! But God will provide someone; and I say no more.”

“Alas!” cried the niece, “may I die if my master does not want to turn knight-errant again.” “A knight-errant I shall die,” he replied; “let the Turk come when he likes; once more I say, God knows what I mean.”

Here the barber begged leave to tell a Seville tale. In the madhouse there, a licentiate, kept as mad by covetous kinsfolk, wrote so wisely to the Archbishop that a chaplain was sent to examine him. He spoke an hour with coherence so great the chaplain resolved to free him. Dressed in his own decent clothes, the licentiate begged to take leave of his “comrades the madmen.” At a cage he exhorted one to hope; whereon another madman, stark naked on a mat, cried out that he, Jupiter, for the crime Seville was committing, would punish it with three years’ drought. “Attach no importance to what this madman has said,” said our licentiate; “for if he is Jupiter and will not send rain, I, who am Neptune, the father and god of the waters, will rain as often as it pleases me.” “For all that, Señor Neptune,” said the chaplain, half-ashamed amid the laughter, “it will not do to vex Señor Jupiter; remain where you are.” And they stripped him again; and that was the end.

“So that’s the story, master barber,” said Don Quixote, “that came in so pat? Master shaver, master shaver! how blind is he who cannot see through a sieve. Comparisons of wit with wit, valour with valour, beauty with beauty, birth with birth, are always odious. I am not Neptune, the god of the waters, nor an astute man; my only endeavour is to convince the world of the mistake it makes in not reviving the happy time when the order of knight-errantry was in the field. With the knights of these days it is the damask and brocade they wear that rustle, not the chain mail of their armour; no knight now-a-days sleeps in the open field, in full panoply; no one, issuing from the wood, treads the barren shore, and finding on the beach a little bark without oars, sail, mast, or tackling, in the intrepidity of his heart flings himself into it and commits himself to the wrathful billows, that one moment lift him to heaven and the next plunge him into the depths; and leaping ashore in a remote land has adventures to be written, not on parchment, but on brass. Now sloth triumphs over energy, indolence over exertion, vice over virtue, arrogance over courage, and theory over practice in arms, which flourished only in the golden ages and in knights-errant. For tell me, who was more virtuous and more valiant than Amadis of Gaul? Who more discreet than Palmerin of England? Who more gracious and easy than Tirante el Blanco? Who more courtly than Lisuarte of Greece? Who more slashed or slashing than Don Belianis? Who more intrepid than Perion of Gaul? Who more ready to face danger than Felixmarte of Hircania? Who more sincere than Esplandian? Who more impetuous than Don Cirongilio of Thrace? Who more bold than Rodamonte? Who more prudent than King Sobrino? Who more daring than Reinaldos? Who more invincible than Roland? and who more gallant and courteous than Ruggiero, from whom the dukes of Ferrara are descended, according to Turpin in his Cosmography? These, or such as these, would carry out my plan, and his Majesty would be well served and save great expense, and the Turk would be left tearing his beard. And so I will stay where I am; and if Jupiter will not send rain, here am I, and I will rain when I please. I say this that Master Basin may know that I understand him.”

The curate then said he had a doubt: could he persuade himself that the whole pack of knights-errant named were truly persons of flesh and blood? “That,” said Don Quixote, “is another mistake. I can almost say I have with my own eyes seen Amadis of Gaul; lofty, fair, with a handsome though black beard; gentle and stern in countenance, sparing of words, slow to anger, quick to put it away. By their deeds and dispositions, with the aid of sound philosophy, one may deduce their features.”

“How big was Morgante?” asked the barber. “Scripture shows there were giants: Goliath seven cubits and a half; bones in Sicily prove men as tall as towers. Yet Morgante I suspect not over-tall; he often slept under a roof.”

“What of Reinaldos and Roland?” “Reinaldos was broad-faced, ruddy, with roguish prominent eyes, punctilious and touchy, friend of scapegraces. Roland, or Rotolando, of middle height, broad-shouldered, rather bow-legged, swarthy, red-bearded, hairy, severe of countenance; a man of few words, very polite and well-bred.”

“No wonder,” said the curate, “the fair Angelica left him for the gentle Medoro.” “That Angelica,” returned Don Quixote, “was a giddy damsel; she scorned a thousand valiant gentlemen and took up with a smooth-faced sprig. The famous Ariosto, not caring to sing her adventures after her contemptible surrender, dropped her where he says:

How she received the sceptre of Cathay,
Some bard of defter quill may sing some day;

and poets are vates, diviners; for since then an Andalusian poet has lamented her tears, and a rare Castilian has sung her beauty.” Asked if none had satirized her, he said poets scorned might have, “but up to the present I have not heard of any defamatory verse against the Lady Angelica, who turned the world upside down.”

At this the housekeeper and the niece cried out in the courtyard, and all ran to see what the matter was.

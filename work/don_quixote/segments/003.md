The outcry came from the niece and the housekeeper, who held the door against Sancho Panza. “What does the vagabond want here? Be off to thy own house, brother; it is thou, and no one else, that deludest my master and leadest him astray, and takest him tramping about the country.” “Devil’s own housekeeper!” cried Sancho; “it is I who am deluded, and led astray, and taken tramping, and not thy master. He enticed me by a trick, promising me an island, which I am still waiting for.” “May evil islands choke thee,” said the niece. “What are islands? Is it something to eat, glutton that thou art?” “Not to eat,” said Sancho, “but to govern and rule, better than four cities or four judgeships at court.” “For all that,” said the housekeeper, “you don’t enter here, you bag of mischief and sack of knavery; go govern your house and dig your seed-patch, and give over looking for islands or shylands.”

Don Quixote, fearing Sancho would blurt out mischief not to his credit, called him in and made the women hold their tongues. The curate and the barber took their leave, despairing of his recovery when they saw how wedded he was to his chivalries; and said the curate to the barber, “You will see, gossip, that when we are least thinking of it, our gentleman will be off once more for another flight.” “I have no doubt of it,” returned the barber; “but I do not wonder so much at the madness of the knight as at the simplicity of the squire, who has such a firm belief in all that about the island.” “God help them,” said the curate; “and let us be on the look-out to see what comes of all these absurdities of the knight and squire, for it seems as if they had both been cast in the same mould, and the madness of the master without the simplicity of the man would not be worth a farthing.” “That is true,” said the barber, “and I should like very much to know what the pair are talking about at this moment.” “I promise you,” said the curate, “the niece or the housekeeper will tell us by-and-by, for they are not the ones to forget to listen.”

Shut in with Sancho, Don Quixote said, “It grieves me thou shouldst say I took thee from thy cottage, when thou knowest I did not remain in mine. We sallied forth together, and have had the same fortune; if they blanketed thee once, they belaboured me a hundred times—that is the only advantage I have of thee.” “Reasonable,” said Sancho; “for by what your worship says, misfortunes belong more properly to knights-errant than to their squires.” “Thou art mistaken,” said Don Quixote; “quando caput dolet—” “I don’t understand any language but my own,” said Sancho. “I mean that when the head suffers, all the members suffer; I am thy head, thou a part of me.” “It should be so,” said Sancho; “but when I was blanketed as a member, my head was on the other side of the wall, looking on, and did not feel any pain. If the members are obliged to feel the sufferings of the head, it should be obliged to feel theirs.”

“Let us put that aside,” said Don Quixote; “tell me, Sancho my friend, what do they say of me in the village? What do the common people, the hidalgos, the caballeros? What do they say of my valour; of my achievements; of my courtesy? How do they take the task I have undertaken in reviving the forgotten order of chivalry? In short, tell me all, and tell it truly; for it is the duty of loyal vassals to tell the truth to their lords in its proper shape, not allowing flattery to add to it or idle deference to lessen it. And I would have thee know, Sancho, that if the naked truth, undisguised by flattery, came to the ears of princes, times would be different, and other ages would be reckoned iron ages more than ours, which I hold to be the golden of these latter days. Profit by this, and report clearly and faithfully what thou knowest.”

“That I will do with all my heart, master,” replied Sancho, “provided your worship be not vexed at what I say, as you wish me to say it out in all its nakedness.” “I will not be vexed at all,” returned Don Quixote; “speak freely, and without beating about the bush.” “Well then,” said he, “first of all, the common people count your worship a mighty great madman, and me no less a fool. The hidalgos say that, not keeping within the bounds of your quality, you have assumed the Don, and made a knight of yourself at a jump, with four vine-stocks and a couple of acres, and never a shirt to your back. The caballeros say they will not have hidalgos setting up in opposition to them, particularly squire hidalgos who polish their own shoes and darn their black stockings with green silk.”

“That,” said Don Quixote, “does not apply to me, for I always go well dressed and never patched; ragged I may be, but ragged more from the wear and tear of arms than of time.” “As to your worship’s valour, courtesy, accomplishments, and task, there is a variety of opinions. Some say, ‘mad but droll;’ others, ‘valiant but unlucky;’ others, ‘courteous but meddling;’ and then they go into such a number of things that they don’t leave a whole bone either in your worship or in myself.” “Recollect, Sancho,” said Don Quixote, “that wherever virtue exists in an eminent degree it is persecuted. Few of the famous have escaped calumny. Julius Cæsar, boldest and wisest of captains, was charged with ambition and not being cleanly in his dress or pure in his morals; Alexander, that he was somewhat of a drunkard; Hercules, lewd and luxurious; Don Galaor over-quarrelsome, and of his brother that he was lachrymose. Among such calumnies, mine may pass, since they are no more than thou hast said.”

“That’s just where it is, body of my father!” “Is there more, then?” asked Don Quixote. “There’s the tail to be skinned yet,” said Sancho; “all so far is cakes and fancy bread; but if your worship wants to know all about the calumnies, I will fetch you one this instant who can tell the whole without missing an atom; for last night the son of Bartholomew Carrasco came home bachelor from Salamanca, and he told me that your worship’s history is already abroad in books, with the title THE INGENIOUS GENTLEMAN DON QUIXOTE OF LA MANCHA; he says they mention me by my own name of Sancho Panza, and the lady Dulcinea del Toboso too, and divers things that happened to us alone; I crossed myself in my wonder how the historian knew them.”

“I promise thee, Sancho,” said Don Quixote, “the author of our history will be some sage enchanter; for to such nothing they choose to write is hidden.” “What! a sage and an enchanter? Why, the bachelor Samson Carrasco says the author is called Cide Hamete Berengena.” “That is a Moorish name,” said Don Quixote. “May be so,” said Sancho; “for I have heard say the Moors are great lovers of berengenas.” “Thou must have mistaken the surname of this ‘Cide’—which means in Arabic ‘Lord’—Sancho,” observed Don Quixote. “Very like,” said Sancho; “but if your worship wishes, I’ll fetch the bachelor in a twinkling.” “Thou wilt do me a pleasure,” said Don Quixote, “for what thou hast told me has amazed me, and I shall not eat a morsel that will agree with me until I have heard all about it.” “Then I am off for him,” said Sancho; and he returned shortly with the bachelor, and all three together had a very droll colloquy.

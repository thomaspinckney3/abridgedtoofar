Paris after midsummer suited Adams: history was nowhere better off, and the calm of the Champs Élysées was so deep that when M. de Witte was promoted to a powerless dignity no one whispered disgrace, while one might have supposed, from the silence, that Alexeieff had reoccupied Manchuria as fulfilment of treaty. For once the conspiracy of silence became crime. Never had so vital a riddle been put before Western society, but society shut its eyes. Manchuria knew every step into war; Japan had completed every preparation; from Yokohama to Irkutsk the East was under war conditions; but Europe knew nothing. The banks would allow no disturbance; the press said not a word; even the embassies were silent.

Adams felt as ignorant as the best-informed statesman, and for once could see that the ignorance was assumed. He could make nothing of it. He found himself in face of his new problem — the workings of Russian inertia — and could conceive no way of forming an opinion how much was real and how much comedy had he been in the Winter Palace himself.

On January 6, 1904, he reached Washington, where the contrast of atmosphere astonished him, for he had never before seen his country think as a world-power. Russian diplomacy had altogether disappeared. Cassini resorted to transparent blague: "Japan seemed infatuated even to the point of war! But what can the Japanese do? As usual, sit on their heels and pray to Buddha!" One of the most accomplished diplomatists in the service could never show his hand so empty as this if he held a card to play. The inertia of Cassini, naturally the most energetic of diplomatists, deeply interested a student of race-inertia, whose mind had lost itself in the attempt to invent scales of force.

The air of the White House was most dramatic by contrast with the outspoken candor of the President. Reticence had no place there. Obliged to take his brother Brooks's place at the Diplomatic Reception, Adams found himself supping at the President's table. Roosevelt spared none. With the usual abuse of the *quos ego*, common to vigorous statesmen, he said all that he thought about Russians and Japanese, Boers and British, in full hearing of twenty people, and concluded by declaring that war was imminent; that it ought to be stopped; that it could be stopped: "I could do it myself; I could stop it to-morrow!" That he was right made no shadow of doubt in the mind of his hearer. The truth was ordinary, even commonplace; but it was not a truth of inertia, nor was the method to be mistaken for inert.

The forces thus launched were bound to reach some new equilibrium, and the war had no personal value for Adams except that it gave Hay his last great triumph. He had carried on his long contest with Cassini so skillfully that no one knew enough to understand the diplomatic perfection of his work, which contained no error; but such success is complete only when invisible. Japan and England saved his "open door" and fought his battle. All that remained was to make the peace; but the race was close, and while the war grew every day in proportions, Hay's strength every day declined.

When the President ordered Hay to St. Louis to address the Exposition, Mrs. Hay bade Adams go with them. He professed the religion of World's Fairs, without which he held education a blind impossibility, and obeyed her bidding the more readily because it united his two educations in one. Ten years had passed since he last crossed the Mississippi, and he found everything new. Through Pittsburgh, Ohio, Indiana, agriculture had made way for steam; tall chimneys reeked smoke on every horizon, and dirty suburbs of scrap-iron and cinders formed the setting of every town. The American of the prime seemed extinct with the Shawnee and the buffalo. The new American showed his parentage proudly; he was the child of steam and the brother of the dynamo, and within less than thirty years this mass of mixed humanities, brought together by steam, was squeezed and welded into approach to shape; a product of so much mechanical power, and bearing no distinctive marks but that of its pressure. The new American, like the new European, was the servant of the powerhouse, as the European of the twelfth century was the servant of the Church.

The St. Louis Exposition was its first creation in the twentieth century, and for that reason acutely interesting. One saw a third-rate town of half-a-million people without history, education, unity, or art, doing what London or Paris would have shrunk from attempting. This new social conglomerate threw away thirty or forty million dollars on a pageant as ephemeral as a stage flat. The world had never witnessed so marvellous a phantasm by night: long lines of white palaces, exquisitely lighted by thousands on thousands of electric candles, soft, rich, shadowy, palpable in their sensuous depths; all in deep silence, profound solitude, as though the Emir Mirza were displaying the beauties of this City of Brass, bathed in the pure light of setting suns. One enjoyed it with iniquitous rapture, not because of exhibits but rather because of their want. Here education found new forage. That the power was wasted, the art indifferent, the economic failure complete, added just so much to the interest. A pilgrim of power, without constituency to flatter, might allow himself to hope: in another half-century, the people of the central valleys might know what they wanted. Possibly they might even have learned how to reach it.

He left St. Louis May 22, 1904, and on Sunday, June 5, found himself again at Coutances, where the people of Normandy had built, towards the year 1250, an Exposition which architects still admired and tourists visited, for it was thought singularly expressive of force as well as of grace in the Virgin. The Norman world was celebrating the Fête Dieu; streets filled with altars to the Virgin, pavements strewn with paths of leaves, the cathedral densely thronged at mass. The Virgin did not shut her costly Exposition on Sunday, or any other day, even to American senators who had shut the St. Louis Exposition to her — or for her. The power of the Virgin had been plainly One, embracing all human activity; while the power of the Senate, or its deity, seemed more or less ashamed of man and his work. Apparently the Virgin had no longer the force to build expositions one cared to visit, but had the force to close them. The force was still real, serious, and at St. Louis had been anxiously measured in actual money-value.

That it was actual proved itself at once by forcing Adams to buy an automobile, which was the form of force he most abominated. He had set aside the summer for study of the Virgin, not as a sentiment but as a motive power, which had left monuments widely scattered and not easily reached. The automobile alone could unite them in any reasonable sequence. In his mind the problem offered itself as to Newton; it was a matter of mutual attraction, a formula as precise as *s = gt²/2*, if he could but experimentally prove it. For him the Virgin was an adorable mistress, who led the automobile and its owner where she would, from Chartres to Rouen, thence to Amiens and Laon, kindly receiving, charming and dazzling her lover, as though she were Aphrodite herself. He never doubted her force, since he felt it to the last fibre of his being, and could not more dispute its mastery than he could dispute the force of gravitation of which he knew nothing but the formula.

If the automobile had one *vitesse* more useful than another, it was that of a century a minute; that of passing from one century to another without break. The centuries dropped like autumn leaves in one's road, and one was not fined for running over them too fast. The hunt for the Virgin's glass opened rich preserves. Then the ocean of religion, which had flooded France, broke into Shelley's light dissolved in star-showers thrown, which had left every remote village strewn with fragments that flashed like jewels. France abounds in sixteenth-century glass; the neighborhood within fifty miles of Paris contains scores of churches where the student may still imagine himself three hundred years old, kneeling before the Virgin's window in the silent solitude of an empty faith, crying his culp, beating his breast, confessing his historical sins, weighed down by the rubbish of sixty-six years' education, and still desperately hoping to understand.

He understood a little, though not much. At Beauvais a superb tree of Jesse, about 1570, bore among the fourteen ancestors of the Virgin features of the Kings of France — Francis I and Henry II — hardly more edifying than Kings of Israel. Compared with the still more famous Tree of Jesse at Chartres, dating from 1150 or thereabouts, must one declare that Engrand le Prince proved progress? and in what direction? Complexity, Multiplicity, even a step towards Anarchy, it might suggest, but what step towards perfection?

One late afternoon at midsummer, the Virgin's pilgrim was wandering through the streets of Troyes in close conversation with Thibaut of Champagne and his seneschal Joinville, when he noticed men looking at a bit of paper stuck in a window. M. de Plehve had been assassinated at St. Petersburg. The mad mixture of Russia and the Crusades, of the Hippodrome and the Renaissance, drove him for refuge into the fascinating Church of St. Pantaleon near by. Martyrs, murderers, Cæsars, saints and assassins — half in glass and half in telegram; chaos of time, place, morals, forces and motive — gave him vertigo. Had one sat all one's life on the steps of Ara Cœli for this? Was assassination forever to be the last word of Progress? No one in the street had shown a sign of protest; he himself felt none; the conservative Christian anarchist had come to his own, but which was he — the murderer or the murdered?

The Virgin herself never looked so winning — so One — as in this scandalous failure of her Grace. To what purpose had she existed, if, after nineteen hundred years, the world was bloodier than when she was born? The stupendous failure of Christianity tortured history. The effort for Unity could not be a partial success; even alternating Unity resolved itself into meaningless motion at last. To the tired student, the idea that he must give it up seemed sheer senility. As long as he could whisper, he would go on as he had begun, bluntly refusing to meet his creator with the admission that the creation had taught him nothing.

The effort must begin at once, for time pressed. The old formulas had failed, and a new one had to be made; but the object was not extravagant. One sought no absolute truth. One sought only a spool on which to wind the thread of history without breaking it. Among indefinite possible orbits, one sought the orbit which would best satisfy the observed movement of the runaway star Groombridge, 1838, commonly called Henry Adams. Any schoolboy could work out the problem if he were given the right to state it in his own terms.

Therefore, when the fogs and frosts stopped his slaughter of the centuries, and shut him up again in his garret, he sat down as though he were again a boy at school to shape after his own needs the values of a Dynamic Theory of History.

From these soundings Adams shaped a Dynamic Theory of History: man as a feeble atom captured by the attractive forces of nature, his progress measurable as the assimilation of new energies — from fetish and Cross to compass and gunpowder — each accelerating his orbit toward an unknown perihelion.

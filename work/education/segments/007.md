## VI. Rome (1859–1860)

The tramp through Thuringen lasted four-and-twenty hours. By its end, Adams's three companions—John Bancroft, James Higginson, and B. W. Crowninshield, all Boston and Harvard like himself—had seen enough. When they sat down to rest on the spot where Goethe had written his famous lines of patient waiting, the profoundness of the thought affected them so strongly that they hired a wagon and drove to Weimar the same night. They stayed because none of them wanted to go home, and Dresden, when they reached it in the spring, seemed a better spot for general education than Berlin, and equally good for reading Civil Law. There was nothing to study in Dresden, and no education to be gained, but the Sistine Madonna and the Correggios were famous, the theatre excellent, and the Elbe prettier than the Spree. For the next eighteen months the young man pursued what he was pleased to call accidental education, since he could pursue no other.

Perhaps the chief obstacle to that education, now that he had come of age, was his honesty—his simple-minded faith in his own intentions. Even after Berlin had become a nightmare he still persuaded himself that his German education was a success. The Germany he loved was the eighteenth-century Germany the Germans themselves were destroying as fast as they could: the good-natured sentiment, the musical and metaphysical abstraction, the blundering incapacity for practical affairs. Military Germany was his abhorrence. He was in fair way to do himself lasting harm, floundering between worlds passed and worlds coming, when the Emperor Napoleon declared war on Austria and raised a confused point of morals in the mind of Europe.

He was saved by his sister. She had married Charles Kuhn of Philadelphia and come to Italy, and like all good Americans was hotly Italian. In July, 1859, at Thun in Switzerland, Henry Adams joined them. Women have, commonly, a very positive moral sense; that which they will is right; that which they reject is wrong; and their will, in most cases, ends by settling the moral. Mrs. Kuhn wanted her brother civilized rather than Germanized, and he was delighted to give her the reins. It was his first experiment in giving the reins to a woman, and he was so much pleased with the results that he never wanted to take them back. In after life he made a general law of experience—no woman had ever driven him wrong; no man had ever driven him right.

Nothing would satisfy Mrs. Kuhn but to go to the seat of war as soon as the armistice was declared. The party crossed the St. Gothard and reached Milan, picturesque with every sort of uniform and every sign of war. To young Adams this first plunge into Italy passed Beethoven as a piece of accidental education. Like music, it differed from other education in being not a means of pursuing life but one of the ends attained. Mrs. Kuhn then insisted on invading the enemy's country by way of the Stelvio Pass. At the highest shoulder, where the glacier of the Ortler Spitze tumbled its huge mass down upon the road, even she gasped when the carriage was driven directly up to the barricade and stopped by a double line of sentries stretching up the mountains until the flash of gun barrels was lost in the flash of snow. The handsome blond officers of the Austrian Jagers were not to be beaten in courtesy by the Garibaldini below; the eternal young woman had her way, and in fifteen minutes the carriage was rolling down to Mals, swarming with German soldiers and German fleas. Young Adams never again felt quite the old confident charm of German atmosphere.

He closed and locked the German door with a long breath of relief in the spring of 1860, and took the road to Italy. He had pursued his education, as it pleased him, for eighteen months, and in spite of the infinite variety of new impressions packed into his mind, he knew no more for his practical purposes than the day he graduated. He had made no step towards a profession. He was as ignorant as a schoolboy of society. He had not natural intelligence enough to see what a mess he had made of his education. Supposing his father asked him what equivalent he had brought back for the time and money put into the experiment, the only possible answer would be: *"Sir, I am a tourist!"*

Meanwhile his father had perplexities of his own. His Quincy district had sent him to Congress, and in the spring of 1860 he was in the full confusion of the nominating season. He supported Mr. Seward. The Republican Party was an unknown force. No one could see far into the future, and fathers could blunder as well as sons. For the time being, young Adams was safe from interference, and went his way to take whatever chance fragments of education God or the devil was pleased to give him.

He joined his sister in Florence, then went on to Rome. For a young Bostonian fresh from Germany, Rome seemed a pure emotion, quite free from economic or actual values—yet it was mechanically piling up conundrum after conundrum in his educational path, which seemed unconnected but had to be connected, insoluble but somehow had to be solved. Rome could not be fitted into an orderly, middle-class, Bostonian, systematic scheme of evolution. No law of progress applied to it. Not even time-sequences—the last refuge of helpless historians—had value for it. The Forum no more led to the Vatican than the Vatican to the Forum. Rienzi, Garibaldi, Tiberius Gracchus, Aurelian might be mixed in any relation of time and never lead to a sequence. The great word Evolution had not yet, in 1860, made a new religion of history, but the old religion had preached the same doctrine for a thousand years without finding in the entire history of Rome anything but flat contradiction.

The problem was most likely more vital in May, 1860, than it had been in October, 1764, when the idea of writing the *Decline and Fall* first started to the mind of Gibbon, "in the close of the evening, as I sat musing in the Church of the Zoccolanti or Franciscan Friars, while they were singing Vespers in the Temple of Jupiter, on the ruins of the Capitol." Murray's Handbook had the grace to quote this passage from Gibbon's *Autobiography*, which led Adams more than once to sit at sunset on the steps of the Church of Santa Maria di Ara Coeli, curiously wondering that not an inch had been gained by Gibbon—or all the historians since—towards explaining the Fall. The mystery remained unsolved; the charm remained intact. Two great experiments of Western civilization had left there the chief monuments of their failure, and nothing proved that the city might not still survive to express the failure of a third.

The young man had no idea what he was doing. He was a tourist, even to the depths of his sub-consciousness, and it was well for him that he should be nothing else, for even the greatest of men cannot sit with dignity among the ruins of the Capitol unless they have something quite original to say about it. No one had ever answered the question to the satisfaction of anyone else; yet everyone who had either head or heart felt that sooner or later he must make up his mind what answer to accept. Substitute the word America for the word Rome, and the question became personal.

One morning Adams happened to be chatting in Hamilton Wilde's studio when a middle-aged Englishman came in, evidently excited, and described the shock of coming unexpectedly on the guillotine near the Circus Maximus, where some criminal had been put to death an hour before. Adams listened sympathetically—and only by slow degrees grappled the conviction that the victim of the shock was Robert Browning. On the background of the Circus Maximus, the Christian martyrs, and the morning's murderer on the block, Browning seemed rather in place, as a middle-aged gentlemanly English Pippa Passes; at Belgravia dinner-tables afterwards he never made part of his background except by effacement.

So the happiest month of May that life had yet offered faded behind the present. Adams explained to himself that he was absorbing knowledge. He would have put it better had he said that knowledge was absorbing him. He went on to Naples, and there heard rumors that Garibaldi and his thousand were about to attack Palermo. Calling on the American Minister, Chandler of Pennsylvania, he was kindly treated and Mr. Chandler amiably consented to send him to the seat of war as bearer of despatches to Captain Palmer of the sloop of war *Iroquois*. Young Adams seized the chance, and went to Palermo in a government transport filled with fleas, commanded by a charming Prince Caracciolo.

Captain Palmer took him with the officers of the ship to make an evening call on Garibaldi, whom they found in the Senate House towards sunset, at supper with his picturesque and piratic staff, in the full noise and color of the Palermo revolution. Garibaldi left the table, and sitting down at the window had a few words of talk with Captain Palmer and young Adams. At that moment Garibaldi was certainly the most serious of the doubtful energies in the world; the most essential to gauge rightly. Even then society was dividing between banker and anarchist.

Adams had the chance to look this sphinx in the eyes and, for five minutes, to watch him like a wild animal, at the moment of his greatest achievement and most splendid action. One saw a quiet-featured, quiet-voiced man in a red flannel shirt; absolutely impervious; a type of which Adams knew nothing. Sympathetic it was, and one felt that it was simple; one suspected even that it might be childlike, but could form no guess of its intelligence. The student was none the wiser.

The lesson of Garibaldi, as education, seemed to teach the extreme complexity of extreme simplicity. Adams left the problem as he found it, came north, squandered two or three months in Paris—disapproving of France in the lump while enjoying everything he disapproved—and finally, having stayed as long as he could and spent all the money he dared, started with mixed emotions but no education for home.

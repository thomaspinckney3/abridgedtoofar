When, forty years afterwards, Henry Adams looked back over his adventures in search of knowledge, he asked himself whether fortune or fate had ever dealt its cards so wildly to any of his known antecessors as when it led him to begin the study of law and to vote for Abraham Lincoln on the same day. He dropped back on Quincy like a lump of lead; he rebounded like a football, tossed into space by an unknown energy which played with all his generation as a cat plays with mice. Not one man in America wanted the Civil War, or expected or intended it. A small minority wanted secession. The vast majority wanted to go on with their occupations in peace. Not one, however clever or learned, guessed what happened.

Fresh from Europe and chaos of another sort, he plunged at once into a lurid atmosphere of politics. His past melted away. The prodigal was welcomed home; his father hinted only at some shade of prodigality by quietly inviting his son to act as private secretary during the winter in Washington, as though any young man who could afford to throw away two winters on the Civil Law could afford to read Blackstone for another winter without a master. November at Quincy had from earliest childhood been the least gay of seasons; yet a Quincy November seemed temperate before the chill of a Boston January. The November of 1860 stood apart: although no one believed in civil war, the air reeked of it; Republicans, as Wide-Awakes, paraded in ranks of torches, their air not that of innocence as they wound down the hillside to the Old House where Mr. Adams, their Member of Congress, received them.

Profoundly ignorant, anxious, and curious, the young man packed again and started for Washington with his family. Ten years had changed little. As in 1800 and 1850, so in 1860, the same rude colony camped in the same forest, with the same unfinished Greek temples for work rooms, and sloughs for roads. The Government had an air of social instability and incompleteness that seemed to support the right of secession in theory as in fact; right or wrong, secession was likely to be easy where there was so little to secede from. The Union was a sentiment, but not much more, and about the Capitol in December the sentiment was chiefly hostile. John Adams was better off in Philadelphia in 1776 than his great-grandson in Washington in 1860.

Patriotism threw a halo over the Continental Congress; over the close of the Thirty-sixth Congress none could be thrown by any one who saw it. Of all the crowd swarming in Washington that winter, young Adams was surely among the most ignorant and helpless, but the knowledge of everybody about him was hardly greater than his own. Mr. Sumner was fond of saying after Oxenstiern: “Quantula sapientia mundus regitur!” Oxenstiern talked of a world that wanted wisdom; Adams found himself seeking education in a world that seemed to him both unwise and ignorant. The Southern secessionists were unbalanced in mind—victims of hallucination—haunted by suspicion, idées fixes, morbid excitement; but this was not all. They were stupendously ignorant of the world. As a class, the cotton-planters were mentally one-sided, ill-balanced, provincial to a degree rarely known. On them the new fountains of power had poured wealth and slaves that acted like oil on flame. They gave a young student his first object-lesson of excess of power held by inadequate hands.

In 1860 this was paradox. Southern statesmen were regarded as standards of statesmanship, and such standards barred education. From such contradictions what was a young man to learn? He could learn nothing but cross-purpose. The planter-gentleman had nothing to teach, except warning. Even as example to be avoided he defied reason too glaringly to help a reasonable being. Thus, at one sweep, the whole field of instruction south of the Potomac was shut off; overshadowed by cotton-planters, from whom one could learn nothing but bad temper, bad manners, poker, and treason.

Perforce, the student was thrown back on Northern precept and example; first of all, on his New England surroundings. Republican houses were few in Washington; Mr. and Mrs. Adams aimed to create a social centre for New Englanders at the Markoe house on I Street. The private secretary began to learn his social duties; the political were confined to committee-rooms and lobbies. The Southern type was to be avoided; the New England type was one’s self—sane and steady men, well-balanced, educated, free from meanness or intrigue, bearing the stamp of Harvard College. Their strength was poise which almost amounted to a defect; they offered no target for love or hate; even as machines, their motion seemed never accelerated. One knew the character to the core; one was it.

There remained the Central and Western States: a few figures—Preston King, Henry Winter Davis, Owen Lovejoy, men born with social faculty; Henry J. Raymond, a man of the world. The average Congressman was civil, but had nothing to ask except offices, and nothing to offer but the views of his district. The average Senator was more reserved, and, excepting one or two genial natures, handicapped by his own importance. Study it as one might, the hope of education narrowed to the possible influence of only two men—Sumner and Seward.

Sumner was then fifty. After his Brooks injuries his nervous system never quite recovered its tone; perhaps ten years of solitary existence as Senator did more. No man can serve ten years as schoolmaster, priest, or Senator, and remain fit for anything else. Dogmatic stations fix a stiffness of attitude, as though they mesmerized the subject. Even among Senators there were degrees—from South Carolinian brutality to the massive Webster, Benton, Clay, or Sumner himself—until in extreme cases, like Conkling, it became Shakespearian and bouffe—Malvolio. Sumner had become dogmatic like the rest, but his qualities warranted dogmatism. He justly thought his services and sacrifices, education, oratorical power, political experience, representative character at the head of New England, and knowledge of the world, made him the most important member of the Senate; and no Senator had more thoroughly saturated himself with its spirit and temper. Senators seldom proclaim inferiority to each other, and like still less to be told of it. Even the greatest inspired little personal affection. Sumner had rivals who held his judgment in no high esteem; one was Senator Seward. The two men would have disliked each other by instinct had they lived in different planets. Each was created to exasperate the other; the virtues of one were the faults of his rival; that the public service must suffer was certain. What were its sufferings compared with the risks run by a young mosquito—a private secretary—trying to buzz admiration in the ears of each, unaware that each would slap at him for belonging to the other? Innocent and unsuspicious beyond nursery permission, the private secretary courted both.

Private secretaries are servants whose business is to serve sources of power. The first news of a professional kind, on reaching Washington, was that Mr. Lincoln had selected Mr. Seward for Secretary of State, and that Seward was to be the medium for communicating his wishes to his followers. Every young man naturally accepted Mr. Lincoln’s wishes as orders, the more because the new President was likely to need all the help that several million young men could give, if they counted on having any President at all to serve. One waited impatiently for the first meeting with the new Secretary.

Governor Seward had long been a family friend; since 1849 his leadership had separated him from the Free Soil remnant; the fierce heat that welded the Republican Party in 1856 melted such barriers, and in 1859 he renewed his intimacy, lost no chance of forcing Mr. Adams to the front. A few days after their arrival in December, he came to dinner alone, as one of the family, and the private secretary watched the man who disposed of his future: a slouching, slender figure; a head like a wise macaw; a beaked nose; shaggy eyebrows; unorderly hair and clothes; hoarse voice; offhand manner; free talk; a perpetual cigar. A new type—western New York—double, political and personal; complex, because the political had become nature, and none could tell which was mask and which features. Among friends he seemed to throw off restraint; in the world he threw it off for effect. He loathed pomposity and enjoyed a joke; how much was nature and how much mask, he was himself too simple to know. Underneath he was conventional by Albany’s conventions. Politicians called it unconventionality; Bostonians called it provincial; Henry thought it charming. From the first sight he loved the Governor, who, though sixty, had the youth of his sympathies. He noticed that Seward was never petty or personal; his talk was large; he generalized; he never posed for statesmanship; he did not require an attitude of prayer; and—almost singular—he had some means of producing the effect of unselfishness.

Superficially Seward and Mr. Adams were contrasts; essentially much alike. The Puritan character, thought rigid, could be supple enough; in Massachusetts all the Adamses had been attacked in succession as no better than political mercenaries. Hildreth echoed the charge that treachery was hereditary in the family. An Adams had to be thick-skinned, armed to return such attentions; but all must admit they subordinated local to national interests, and would continue to do so when forced to choose. C. F. Adams was sure to do what his father had done, as his father had followed John Adams, and no doubt earned his epithets.

The inevitable followed, as a child fresh from the nursery should have foreseen; but the young man never dreamed. He made no pretence of guessing motives; he knew only his own infantile ignorance and innocent good-faith. Rightly or wrongly the new President and his chief advisers decided that, before they could administer the Government, they must make sure of a government to administer, and that this chance depended on Virginia. The winter’s ascendancy wavered between the effort of the cotton States to drag Virginia out, and of the new President to keep Virginia in. Seward, representing the Administration in the Senate, took the lead; Mr. Adams took the lead in the House; the party united on its tactics. In offering concessions to the border States, they ran the risk—or incurred the certainty—of dividing their own party, and took it with open eyes. As Seward himself, in his gruff way, said at dinner, after he and Mr. Adams had made their speeches: “If there’s no secession now, you and I are ruined.”

They won their game. The sudden arrival of Mr. Lincoln on February 23, and the language of his inaugural, were the final term of the winter’s tactics, and closed the private secretary’s interest. Perhaps he felt more interest in another private secretary, of his own age, a young man named John Hay, who lighted on LaFayette Square at the same moment. Friends are born, not made. From the first slight meeting, he recognized Hay as a friend, and never lost sight of him; but, for the moment, his own task ended on March 4 when Hay’s began. The winter’s anxieties were shifted upon new shoulders, and Henry gladly turned back to Blackstone. He had tried to make himself useful, acting in secret as newspaper correspondent, cultivating a large acquaintance, even haunting ballrooms where the simple, old-fashioned Southern tone was pleasant in an atmosphere of conspiracy and treason. The sum was next to nothing for education, because no one could teach; all were as ignorant as himself; none knew what should be done or how; all were more bent on asking than answering questions. The mass of ignorance in Washington was lighted up by no ray of knowledge. Society, from top to bottom, broke down.

From this law there was no exception, unless perhaps old General Winfield Scott, the only military figure that looked equal to the crisis. No one else either looked it, or was it, by nature or training. Had young Adams’s life hung on the correctness of his estimate of the new President, he would have lost. He saw Mr. Lincoln but once, at the melancholy Inaugural Ball: a long, awkward figure; a plain, ploughed face; a mind, absent in part, and in part worried by white kid gloves; features that expressed neither self-satisfaction nor other familiar Americanism, but rather the same painful sense of needing education that tormented a private secretary; above all a lack of apparent force. Any private secretary fit for his business would have thought, as Adams did, that no man living needed so much education as the new President, but that all the education he could get would not be enough.

As far as one could see, no one in Washington was fitted for his duties; or rather, no duties in March were fitted for the duties in April. The few who thought they knew were more in error than those who knew nothing. Only one man in Adams’s reach seemed supremely fitted by knowledge and experience to be adviser and friend: Senator Sumner; and there, in fact, the young man’s education began; there it ended.

Going over the experience long after all the great actors were dead, he struggled to see where he had blundered. In the effort to make acquaintances, he lost friends; could he have helped it? He had necessarily followed Seward and his father; he took for granted that his business was obedience, discipline, and silence; he supposed the party to require it, and the crisis to overrule personal doubts. He was thunderstruck to learn that Senator Sumner privately denounced the course, regarded Mr. Adams as betraying the principles of his life, and broke off relations with his family.

Many a shock was he to meet in a long life near politics; but the profoundest lessons are not the lessons of reason; they are sudden strains that permanently warp the mind. He cared little for the point in discussion; was willing to admit that Sumner might be right, though in all great emergencies he commonly found every one more or less wrong; he liked lofty moral principle; he respected Sumner; but the shock opened a chasm that never closed, and as long as life lasted, he found himself invariably taking for granted, as a political instinct—without waiting further experiment—as he took for granted that arsenic poisoned—the rule that a friend in power is a friend lost.

On his own score, he never admitted the rupture, and never exchanged a word with Sumner on the subject, then or afterwards, but his education—for good or bad—made an enormous stride. He was looking at hundreds of Southern gentlemen who believed themselves honest, but who seemed engaged in the plainest breach of faith and the blackest secret conspiracy; yet they did not disturb his education; history told of little else; not one rebel defection—not even Lee’s—cost him a personal pang; but Sumner’s struck home.

This, then, was the result of the attempt at education down to March 4, 1861; and it was hardly what he wanted. Washington offered education, but not the kind that led to good. The process Matthew Arnold described as wandering between two worlds, one dead, the other powerless to be born, helps nothing. Even before the traitors had flown, the vultures descended in swarms that darkened the ground, and tore the carrion of political patronage into fragments and gobbets of fat and lean on the very steps of the White House. Not a man there knew what his task was to be, or was fitted for it; every one without exception was to learn his business at the cost of the public. Lincoln, Seward, Sumner, and the rest could give no help; they knew less than he; within six weeks they were all to be taught their duties by the uprising of such as he, and their education was to cost a million lives and ten thousand million dollars, more or less, before the country could recover its balance and movement. Henry was a helpless victim, and, like all the rest, he could only wait for he knew not what, to send him he knew not where.

With the close of the session, his own functions ended. Ceasing to be private secretary, he knew not what else to do but return with his father and mother to Boston in mid-March, and, with childlike docility, sit down at a desk in the law-office of Horace Gray in Court Street, to begin again: “My Lords and Gentlemen”; dozing after a two o’clock dinner, or waking to discuss politics with the future Justice. There, in ordinary times, he would have remained for life; his attempt at education in treason having, like all the rest, disastrously failed.

BOSTON (1848-1854)

Peter Chardon Brooks, the other grandfather, died January 1, 1849, bequeathing what was supposed to be the largest estate in Boston, about two million dollars, to his seven surviving children: four sons—Edward, Peter Chardon, Gorham, and Sydney; three daughters—Charlotte, married to Edward Everett; Ann, married to Nathaniel Frothingham, minister of the First Church; and Abigail Brown, born April 25, 1808, married September 3, 1829, to Charles Francis Adams, hardly a year older than herself. Their first child, born in 1830, was a daughter, Louisa Catherine; the second, a son, John Quincy; the third, Charles Francis; the fourth, being of less account, was in a way given to his mother, who named him Henry Brooks, after a favorite brother just lost. More followed, but these, being younger, had nothing to do with the arduous process of educating.

The Adams connection was singularly small in Boston, but the family of Brooks was singularly large and even brilliant, almost wholly of clerical New England stock. One might have sought long for three brothers-in-law more distinguished than Edward Everett, Dr. Frothingham, and Mr. Adams, or for seven brothers-in-law more unlike. Everett had broken bounds by leaving the Unitarian pulpit for Congress to support J. Q. Adams’s administration; the social consequence led to the marriage of the President’s son with Abigail Brooks. The wreck of parties under Andrew Jackson had spoiled many careers, Everett’s among them; yet with the Whigs he rose, went as Minister to England, and came back with a European halo, next to Daniel Webster as orator and representative figure of Boston. Dr. Frothingham followed the same clerical school. Neither had much in common with Mr. Adams, a younger man, biassed by his father and by the inherited feud between Quincy and State Street; but relations were friendly as far as a boy could see, and the innumerable cousins went to the First Church every Sunday in winter, and slept through their uncle’s sermons, never asking what they were supposed to mean. For two hundred years the First Church had seen the same little boys, dimly conscious of the same feuds; death had cleared the old score; the quarrels of John Adams and of John Quincy Adams were no longer acutely personal; the game was considered as drawn; and Charles Francis Adams might then have taken his inherited rights of political leadership in succession to Webster and Everett. Instead, he drew himself aloof and renewed the old war. He could not help it. With the record of J. Q. Adams fresh, he could make no terms with the slave-power; and the slave-power overshadowed State Street. Even his children, who as yet had no principles, could as little follow Webster or Seward. They were anti-slavery by birth, as their name was Adams and their home was Quincy. Had State Street been Paradise, they must hunger for it in vain, and it hardly needed Daniel Webster as archangel with flaming sword to order them away from the door.

Time altered many perspectives and taught gentler judgment, but at ten years old the boy’s face was already fixed, his heart stone, against State Street; his education warped beyond recovery toward Puritan politics. The year 1848, like enough to 1776 to make a fair parallel, stamped him, and a few months after the death of John Quincy Adams, when anti-slavery delegates met at Buffalo to name Martin Van Buren for President and Charles Francis Adams for Vice-President, the stamp was set. For a boy, his father’s running for office dwarfed every other excitement; but even without that bias, 1848 decided twenty years to come. The Puritan thought his thought higher, and his moral standards better, and could not be convinced that utilitarian morality was good enough. Nature had given to the boy Henry a character that, in any previous century, would have led him into the Church; he inherited dogma and a priori thought; and anti-slavery politics swept him back into Puritanism with the violence of a religious war.

His father’s character was therefore the larger part of his education. To Henry, Charles Francis Adams possessed the only perfectly balanced mind that ever existed in the name. Newspapers had long abused the elder Adamses for want of judgment; they abused Charles Francis for his judgment. He was singular for mental poise—absence of self-assertion or self-consciousness—the faculty of standing apart without seeming aware that he was alone—instinctive mastery of form. His memory was hardly above the average; his mind was not bold, restless, imaginative, or mathematical; but it worked with admirable self-restraint. Within its range it was a model.

Boston standards were high, much affected by clerical self-respect; Dr. Channing, Mr. Everett, Dr. Frothingham, Dr. Palfrey, President Walker, Emerson, commanded distinction; a literary group—Ticknor, Prescott, Longfellow, Motley, Holmes; science in medicine; but Mr. Adams was not of them; he cared little for science; he stood alone—no master, hardly even his father; no scholars, hardly even his sons. Almost alone among his contemporaries, he was not English in sympathies, and became indifferent to social distinction. Never once in forty years did his son notice a trace of snobbishness; an English duke or duchess was indifferent; royalty a slightly inconvenient presence. Never did his son see him flatter or vilify, or show a sign of envy or jealousy; never a shade of vanity or self-conceit. "Never a tone of arrogance! Never a gesture of pride!"

The same might have been said of John Quincy Adams, but his associates averred it was marred by restlessness and want of judgment. No one charged Charles Francis with this fault; critics called him cold. The world never loved perfect poise; it loves absence of it, for it must be amused. Had he been cold, he would have followed Webster, Everett, Seward, Winthrop; had he been less balanced, he would have gone with Garrison, Wendell Phillips, Edmund Quincy, Theodore Parker, into secession. Between the two paths he set up a party of his own.

This party became a chief influence in the boy’s education. Its social centre in Mount Vernon Street drew Dr. John G. Palfrey, Richard H. Dana, and Charles Sumner. Palfrey, though clerically trained, had wit and range, and yearned, like every true Bostonian, for the Athenaeum Club in Pall Mall. Dana, affecting to be still before the mast, worked by grim self-discipline—"I pride myself on my success in doing not the things I like to do, but the things I don’t like to do"—and, aristocratic to the tenth generation, seemed always fully in sight. Sumner, unlike the others, adored English standards and rivalled Edmund Burke. Boston admired his social success abroad, while politics made him an outcast at home; Beacon Street would not receive the Free Soil leaders; Sumner, without wife or household, and hungriest for polite society, took refuge often in Mount Vernon Street; his solitude was glacial, and reacted on his character; but his superiority was incontestable; he was the classical ornament of the party. The boy worshipped him. Sumner was the boy’s ideal of greatness—heroic.

As the boy grew to ten or twelve, his father gave him a writing-table in an alcove of the Boston library; there Henry worked over Latin Grammar and listened to the four discussing anti-slavery politics. Mr. Adams edited a newspaper as their organ; at the same time he edited the "Works" of John Adams, making the boy read texts for proof. The Boston Whig discussions ran much in the style of Novanglus and Massachusettensis; the boy got as little education for his own time from one as from the other; the men were types of the past.

Down to 1850, New England society was directed by professions—lawyers, physicians, professors, merchants—acting as though each were a church. Politics required competent expression; the old Ciceronian idea produced a line of New England statesmen; Boston chose Webster, paid him by honorarium; Everett succeeded; Winthrop claimed succession; Sumner aspired to break the succession, but not the system. The Adamses had chiefly won distinction outside the State, but had required State support. The Mount Vernon Street group were statesmen, not politicians; they guided public opinion, and were little guided by it.

From such air the boy learned only one lesson: this was the world he was to fit. Europe was but a variety of the same upper-class bourgeoisie—the Paris of Louis Philippe and Guizot; the London of Peel, Macaulay, Mill; even Carlyle found alliances in Boston and Concord. The system seemed so successful that Germany wanted to try it and Italy yearned for it. Even the violent reaction after 1848 and Europe’s return to military practices never shook the faith. No one foresaw radical change. The world was producing sixty or seventy million tons of coal and beginning to make itself felt with steam; all experience and revelation conspired to deceive a twelve-year-old boy who assumed that his ideas, alone respectable, would be alone respected.

Viewed from Mount Vernon Street, life was simple. Politics offered no difficulties; the moral law was a sure guide. Social perfection was sure, because human nature worked for Good, and three instruments were all she asked—Suffrage, Common Schools, and Press. On these points doubt was forbidden. Education was divine, and man needed only correct knowledge of facts:

  "Were half the power that fills the world with terror,
    Were half the wealth bestowed on camps and courts,
  Given to redeem the human mind from error,
    There were no need of arsenals nor forts."

Nothing quieted doubt like the mental calm of the Unitarian clergy, who insisted on no doctrine, but taught the means of leading a virtuous, useful, unselfish life, sufficient for salvation. Boston had solved the universe; the problem was worked out.

Of all the conditions of his youth which afterwards puzzled the grown-up man, this disappearance of religion puzzled him most. The boy went to church, read his Bible, learned religious poetry, believed in a mild deism, prayed, went through forms; but to none of them was religion real. They threw off discipline at the first moment, and never afterwards entered a church. The faculty of turning away one’s eyes at a chasm was common; in politics some protested; in religion and philosophy no one. Theodore Parker’s deism, and that of Octavius Frothingham, distressed Beacon Street without solving old problems; Emerson’s protest was, from an old-world point of view, naif.

The children reached manhood without knowing religion, and sure that dogma and metaphysics were not worth knowing; their education became the more literary and political. They heard daily table-talk as good as they were ever to hear again. Louisa sparkled; John talked best and was most popular though apt to be on the unpopular side; Palfrey and Dana could be entertaining; Sumner smiled grandly; Mr. Adams, talking little, listened well, and laughed till he choked. To amuse and educate them, Mr. Adams read much aloud—Horace Mann’s speeches, the "Epistles" of "Hosea Biglow"; Longfellow and Tennyson as they appeared; the children took Dickens and Thackeray for themselves. The boy, desultory, read eighteenth-century historians and poets; when offered Wordsworth as a gift on condition of reading it through, he declined; Pope and Gray asked no effort; he reached Wordsworth at thirty.

The family was rather an atmosphere than an influence. His father did him a great service trying to teach him French and accent. Otherwise the brothers and sisters were replicas of the same type, facets of one individual, all wishing to control power in some form; their form was tied to politics or literature. Whether the individual fit to deal with life, no one knew.

A family of turbulent children, given free rein, should have come to grief; but, by chance, they grew up decent citizens. Home influences alone never saved the New England boy; outside influences were negative. School helped only by reaction. He detested it, and the dislike grew. He reckoned his school-days, from ten to sixteen, time thrown away. For success he needed the facile use of only four tools: Mathematics, French, German, and Spanish. Latin and Greek he could learn more completely by six weeks’ intelligent work than by six years at school. He mastered none. Thus at the outset he was condemned to failure more or less complete; indeed, had his father given him half an hour’s daily direction, he would have done more than school ever did.

Boston offered few healthy resources for boys. Bar-rooms and billiard-rooms were familiar; sports were rudimentary; sport as a pursuit unknown. Books remained the source of life. As they came—Thackeray, Dickens, Bulwer, Tennyson, Macaulay, Carlyle—they were devoured; but the happiest hours were passed in summer, lying on a musty heap of Congressional Documents in the old farmhouse at Quincy, reading "Quentin Durward," "Ivanhoe," and "The Talisman," and raiding the garden at intervals for peaches and pears. On the whole he learned most then.

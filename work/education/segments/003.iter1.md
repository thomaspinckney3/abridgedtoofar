UNDER the shadow of Boston State House, turning its back on the house of John Hancock, the little passage called Hancock Avenue ran from Beacon Street, skirting the State House grounds, to Mount Vernon Street, on the summit of Beacon Hill; and there, in the third house below Mount Vernon Place, February 16, 1838, a child was born and christened later by his uncle, minister of the First Church after the tenets of Boston Unitarianism, as Henry Brooks Adams.

Had he been born in Jerusalem under the shadow of the Temple and circumcised in the Synagogue by his uncle the high priest, under the name of Israel Cohen, he would scarcely have been more distinctly branded, and not much more heavily handicapped, for the races of the coming century; yet, for the ordinary traveller, to be ticketed through life by an old, established traffic had conveniences. A hundred years earlier such safeguards would have secured any young man’s success; even in 1838 the accident of starting from so colonial—so troglodytic—a nest as First Church, Beacon Hill, John Hancock and John Adams, Mount Vernon Street and Quincy, crowded on ten pounds of unconscious babyhood, was queer enough to tempt speculation. What could become of such a child of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries when required to play the game of the twentieth? Had he been consulted, would he have cared to play the game at all, holding such cards as he held, and suspecting that the game was to be one of which neither he nor any one else back to the beginning of time knew the rules or the risks or the stakes? He was not consulted and was not responsible, but had he been taken into the confidence of his parents, he would certainly have told them to change nothing as far as concerned him. He would have been astounded by his own luck. Probably no child, born in the year, held better cards than he. Whether life was an honest game of chance, or whether the cards were marked and forced, he could not refuse to play his excellent hand. He could never make the usual plea of irresponsibility. He accepted the situation as though he had been a party to it, and under the same circumstances would do it again, the more readily for knowing the exact values. To his life as a whole he was a consenting, contracting party and partner from the moment he was born to the moment he died. Only so—as a consciously assenting member in full partnership with the society of his age—had his education an interest.

As it happened, he never got to the point of playing the game at all; he lost himself in the study of it, watching the errors of the players. A story of education—seventy years of it—the practical value remains to the end in doubt; but though every one cannot be a Gargantua-Napoleon-Bismarck and walk off with the great bells of Notre Dame, every one must bear his own universe, and most persons care to learn how their neighbors have carried theirs.

This problem of education, started in 1838, went on for three years, while the baby grew, like other babies, unconsciously, as a vegetable, the outside world working as it never had worked before, to get his new universe ready for him. Often in old age he puzzled over the question whether, on the doctrine of chances, he was at liberty to accept himself or his world as an accident. No such accident had ever happened before in human experience. For him, alone, the old universe was thrown into the ash-heap and a new one created. He and his eighteenth-century, troglodytic Boston were suddenly cut apart—separated forever—in act if not in sentiment, by the opening of the Boston and Albany Railroad; the appearance of the first Cunard steamers in the bay; and the telegraphic messages which carried from Baltimore to Washington the news that Henry Clay and James K. Polk were nominated for the Presidency. This was in May, 1844; he was six years old; his new world was ready for use, and only fragments of the old met his eyes.

Of all this that was being done to complicate his education, he knew only the color of yellow. He first found himself sitting on a yellow kitchen floor in strong sunlight. He was three years old when he took this earliest step; a lesson of color. The second followed; a lesson of taste: on December 3, 1841, scarlet fever; in convalescence, about New Year’s Day, 1842, his aunt entered with a saucer and a baked apple. He remembered also discomfort—bundled in blankets for the move from Hancock Avenue to Mount Vernon Street, midwinter, January 10, and the want of air under his wraps; the noise of moving furniture.

The fever took importance in his eyes as he grew older. He fell behind his brothers in height, in bone and weight; his nerves were more delicate than boys’ nerves ought to be; and he came to exaggerate these weaknesses. The habit of doubt; of distrusting his own judgment, and of rejecting the judgment of the world; the tendency to regard every question as open; the hesitation to act except as a choice of evils; the shirking of responsibility; the love of line, form, quality; the horror of ennui; the passion for companionship and the antipathy to society—well-known New England qualities—seemed in him to be stimulated by the fever. His brothers were the type; he was the variation. Yet he grew up in excellent health as far as he knew, accepting local standards, enjoying life keenly; whatever was peculiar in him was education, not character, and came of that eighteenth-century inheritance which he took with his name.

The atmosphere in which he lived was colonial, revolutionary, almost Cromwellian; resistance to something was the law of New England nature. The boy looked out on the world with the instinct of resistance; for numberless generations his predecessors had viewed the world chiefly as a thing to be reformed, filled with evil forces to be abolished, and they saw no reason to suppose that they had wholly succeeded in the abolition; the duty was unchanged. That duty implied not only resistance to evil, but hatred of it. Boys naturally look on all force as an enemy, and generally find it so, but the New Englander, whether boy or man, in his long struggle with a stingy or hostile universe, had learned also to love the pleasure of hating; his joys were few.

Politics, as a practice, whatever its professions, had always been the systematic organization of hatreds, and Massachusetts politics had been as harsh as the climate. The charm of New England was harshness of contrasts—cold that froze the blood, heat that boiled it—so that the pleasure of hating—one’s self if no better victim offered—was not its rarest amusement. The double exterior nature made the strongest motive of education. Town and country, winter and summer, force and freedom, marked two modes of life, balanced like lobes of the brain. Town was winter confinement, school, rule, discipline; straight, gloomy streets; frosts that made the snow sing under wheels; thaws when the streets were dangerous; society of uncles, aunts and cousins who expected children to behave. Town was restraint, law, unity. Country, only seven miles away, was liberty, diversity, outlawry—the endless delight of mere sense impressions given by nature for nothing.

To the boy Henry Adams, summer was drunken. Among senses, smell was the strongest—hot pine-woods and sweet-fern; new-mown hay; salt water and low tide. Next came taste—pennyroyal and flagroot; the shell of a pignut; even the letters of a spelling-book—the taste of A-B, AB, suddenly revived on his tongue sixty years afterwards. Light, line, color were crude pleasures—the blaze of a New England sun; a peony with early dew; the intense blue of the sea from the Quincy hills; cumuli in a June sky; strong reds and greens of children’s prints. The opposites were the cold grays of November and the muddy thaws of Boston winter. Winter and summer were hostile lives; winter the effort to live; summer tropical license. Summer and country were sensual living; winter was compulsory learning.

The bearing of these seasons on his education was no fancy; from earliest childhood life was double. Winter and summer, town and country, law and liberty, were hostile, and the man who pretended they were not, was in his eyes a schoolmaster—that is, a man employed to tell lies to little boys. Though Quincy was but two hours’ walk from Beacon Hill, it belonged in a different world. For two hundred years, every Adams, from father to son, had lived within sight of State Street, and sometimes had lived in it, yet none had ever taken kindly to the town, or been taken kindly by it. The boy inherited his double nature. Never for a moment did he connect the two ideas of Boston and John Adams; the idea of John Adams went with Quincy.

He liked the Adams side best because it meant country and freedom; yet he felt that Quincy was, in a way, inferior to Boston, and that Boston looked down on Quincy. Quincy had no Boston style. The President’s place was larger and more interesting than his grandfather Brooks’s Medford house, but wanted wealth; it smacked of colonial age, not plush curtains. He was born an eighteenth-century child. The old house at Quincy was eighteenth century, with Queen Anne mahogany panels and Louis Seize chairs and sofas—neither comfortable. Daily life painted the dark panels white. Most people preferred the new, with good reason, and the child felt at a disadvantage for the taste.

He inherited no prejudice from persons. Both grandfathers, born in 1767, died in 1848, were kind; the one associated with winter and Boston, the other with summer and Quincy. The attachment to Quincy was not all roses. Everywhere a cruel universe combined to crush a child; between boy and master was always war. Rarely has the boy felt kindly towards his tamers.

So much the more singular that his first serious contact with the President—John Quincy Adams—should have been a struggle of will which, instead of leaving a lifelong sting, left a sense of fair treatment from a natural enemy. He could not have been much more than six or seven when, staying at Quincy, he broke into passionate rebellion against going to school. His mother, a guest, could not enforce obedience. He refused to start, holding his own at the bottom of the stairs to the President’s library, when the door opened and the old man slowly came down. Putting on his hat, he took the boy’s hand without a word, and walked with him up the shadeless road to town. The boy counted on a corner to dodge; the old man did not stop. One after another his strategical points were turned till he was seated in school, centre of curious criticism; then the President released his hand and departed. The act ought to have made the boy dislike his grandfather for life; it did not. The old man had shown no temper, made no display of force, uttered no cant about duty. Above all, he held his tongue. For this forbearance the boy felt instinctive respect. He admitted force as a form of right; he admitted even temper, under protest; but the seeds of a moral education would at that moment have fallen on the stoniest soil in Quincy, which is, as every one knows, the stoniest glacial and tidal drift known in any Puritan land.

Their relations were friendly. The boy haunted the library; deranged papers; searched drawers; drew the sword-cane; snapped travelling-pistols; upset corners; penetrated the dressing-closet where inverted tumblers covered caterpillars, which never became moths. The President rode hobbies—tree-culture; acorns planted in cut-glass bowls to the Madam’s protest; peaches and pears rotting for seed. With Puritan virtues, the boy brought him the finest peaches and ate the less perfect. Somewhere there exists a little volume of Nursery Rhymes with the boy’s name in the President’s trembling hand; the Bible at birth; the Brooks silver mugs.

A cottage “on the hill,” with far views over Quincy Bay and Boston, held them. Till his twelfth year his summers centred there. Of education he had little to complain; country schools were not serious; home impressions were sharpest. None compared, as influences that warped a mind, with the back of the President’s bald head in his pew on Sundays, in line with President Quincy’s. The Irish gardener once said to the child: “You’ll be thinkin’ you’ll be President too!” The remark fixed itself; he had never thought on the subject; that there could be doubt was new. What had been would continue to be.

The Madam, Louisa Catherine, was more remote, more decorative: a refined figure in her panelled room with Dutch tiles, her gentle voice, her exotic Sevres. Born in London; driven to Nantes; brought back; her father, Joshua Johnson, Consul in London; John Quincy married her in 1797. Louisa was charming, like a Romney portrait, but among her many charms that of being a New England woman was not one. The defect was serious. Berlin; America; St. Petersburg with a baby; the winter of 1814–15 crossing Europe alone with a boy of seven; England again; then Secretary of State’s wife; eight years in F Street; four miserable years in the White House; then wife of a Member of the House till 1848. By the time the boy remembered her, she was seventy, “a vision of silver gray,” hardly more Bostonian than on her wedding-day. She was Louis Seize like the furniture. He never dreamed that from her might come some of those doubts and self-questionings, those hesitations, those rebellions against law and discipline, which marked more than one of her descendants; but he might even then have felt some vague instinctive suspicion that he was to inherit from her the seeds of the primal sin, the fall from grace, the curse of Abel, that he was not of pure New England stock, but half exotic. As a child of Quincy he was not a true Bostonian, but even as a child of Quincy he inherited a quarter taint of Maryland blood.

This prehistoric stage ended abruptly with his tenth year. A winter morning confusion; the word paralysis; the President in a tall-backed invalid chair by the spare-room fireplace, Dr. Lunt’s parishioners and old friends dozing opposite. On February 21, 1848, the eighteenth century, as an actual companion, vanished. The funeral at Quincy, with Dr. Lunt’s deeply cadenced voice and remarkable English, left an overwhelming impression. He was to see many great functions afterwards, but never again anything so impressive to him as the last services at Quincy over the body of one President and the ashes of another. The impression was deepened by Faneuil Hall, where Edward Everett delivered a Eulogy—admirable oratory; but the boy already knew the dead President could not be in it, and why. The shadow of 1812 still hung over State Street; the shadow of Civil War had begun to darken Faneuil Hall. How could an orator say there that the distinctive mark of all the Adamses had been their inherited quarrel with State Street, breaking out into riot, bloodshed, feuds, foreign and civil war? How whisper Hartford Convention? How suggest Secession?

Thus at ten, the boy stood before a dilemma that might have puzzled an early Christian. What was he?—where was he going? Something was wrong; it must be Boston. Quincy represented a moral principle—the law of Resistance; of Truth; of Duty; of Freedom. He was a ten-year-old priest and politician. Could he have guessed the next fifty years? Supposing he had seen a New York stock-list of 1900, and studied railways, telegraphs, coal, steel—would he have quitted his eighteenth-century prejudices and abstract ideals to make an expiatory pilgrimage to State Street, and ask for the fatted calf of his grandfather Brooks and a clerkship in the Suffolk Bank?

Sixty years afterwards he was still unable to decide. Each course had its advantages; but the material advantages, looking back, seemed to lie wholly in State Street.

WASHINGTON was always amusing, but in 1900, as in 1800, its chief interest lay in its distance from New York. The movement of New York had become planetary—beyond control—while the task of Washington, in 1900 as in 1800, was to control it. The success of Washington in the past century promised ill for its success in the next.

To a student who had passed his best years pondering the political philosophy of Jefferson, Gallatin, and Madison, the problem Roosevelt took in hand seemed alive with historical interest, but as yet the forces had not aligned themselves except in foreign affairs; and there one turned to seek the channel of wisdom as naturally as though Washington did not exist.

Hay had reached the summit of his career, and saw himself on the edge of wreck. Committed to keeping China "open," he saw China about to be shut. Almost alone in the world, he represented the "open door," and could not escape being crushed by it. Yet luck had been with him. The appointment of Michael Herbert counted for double the value of an ordinary diplomat: to reduce friction is the chief use of friendship, and in politics the loss by friction is outrageous. This personal sympathy prolonged Hay's life, and led directly to a revolution in Germany. Down to that moment, the Kaiser had counted as the ally of the Czar in all matters relating to the East. Suddenly Holleben received a telegram ordering him to pretext illness and come home, which he obeyed within four-and-twenty hours. No cause was guessed except the Kaiser's wish for a personal representative at Washington; breaking precedent, he sent Speck von Sternburg to counterbalance Herbert.

The scholar was studying, not on Hay's account, but on his own. He had seen Hay, in 1898, bring England into his combine; he had seen France brought back into an Atlantic system; and now he saw the dramatic swing of Germany towards the west—the movement of all others nearest mathematical certainty. Whether the Kaiser meant it or not, he gave the effect of meaning to assert his independence of Russia. That Germany could be so quickly detached from separate objects and brought into an Atlantic system seemed a paradox more paradoxical than any his education had offered. If Germany could be held there, a century of friction would be saved. The Kaiser, by one personal act of energy, freed Hay's hands so completely that he saw his problems simplified to Russia alone.

Naturally Russia was a problem ten times as difficult. The history of Europe for two hundred years had accomplished little but to state one or two sides of the Russian problem. Even a cave-dweller in La Fayette Square saw before his eyes, in the spring of 1903, a survey of future order or anarchy that would exhaust the power of his telescopes and defy the accuracy of his theodolites.

The drama grew every day more Byzantine; the Russian Government itself showed clear signs of dislocation, and the orders of Lamsdorf and de Witte were reversed when applied in Manchuria. Adams had private reasons for wishing well to the Czar: the friendliness of Alexander I had saved J. Q. Adams's fortunes in 1810; the neutrality of Alexander II had saved him some terribly anxious days in 1862. The last and highest triumph of history would, to his mind, be the bringing of Russia into the Atlantic combine. But for the moment, the gravest doubts and ignorance covered the whole field. Perhaps Hay protected Cassini for the very reason that Cassini could not disguise an emotion, and never failed to betray that, in setting the enormous bulk of Russian inertia to roll over China, he regretted infinitely that he should have to roll it over Hay too. His political philosophy, like that of all Russians, seemed fixed in the single idea that Russia must fatally roll—must, by her irresistible inertia, crush whatever stood in her way.

For Hay's pooling policy, the fatalism of Russian inertia meant the failure of American intensity. When Russia rolled over a neighboring people, she absorbed their energies in her own movement of custom and race which neither Czar nor peasant could convert into any Western equivalent. In 1903 Hay saw Russia knocking away the last blocks that held back the launch of this huge mass into the China Sea. The vast force of inertia known as China was to be united with the huge bulk of Russia in a single mass which no amount of new force could henceforward deflect.

Russia held Europe and America in her grasp, and Cassini held Hay in his. The Siberian Railway offered checkmate to all possible opposition. Japan must make the best terms she could; England must go on receding; America and Germany would look on at the avalanche. The wall of Russian inertia that barred Europe across the Baltic would bar America across the Pacific; and Hay's policy of the open door would infallibly fail.

Thus the game seemed lost. To the humble student, the loss of Hay's game affected only Hay; for himself, the game—not the stakes—was the chief interest. The Atlantic would have to deal with a vast continental mass of inert motion, like a glacier, which moved, and consciously moved, by mechanical gravitation alone. Was volume or intensity the stronger? What and where was the *vis nova* that could hold its own before this prodigious ice-cap of *vis inertiae*? What was movement of inertia, and what its laws?

Naturally a student knew nothing about mechanical laws, but went to his books to learn that inertia is a property of matter, by which matter at rest tends to remain so, and in motion to move on in a straight line. Finding that his mind refused to imagine itself at rest or in a straight line, he decided that his mind was never at rest, but moved—when normal—about something it called a motive. So long as these motives were habitual, the result might, for convenience, be called movement of inertia; but the greater the bulk to move, the greater the force to deflect it.

This seemed simple as running water; but simplicity is the most deceitful mistress that ever betrayed man. For years the student had complained that minds were unequally inert. One class of minds responded only to habit; another only to novelty. Race classified thought. Class-lists classified mind. No two men thought alike, and no woman thought like a man.

Race-inertia made the chief trouble in the Russian future. History looked doubtful when asked whether race-inertia had ever been overcome without destroying the race; but surely sex-inertia had never been overcome at all. Of all movements of inertia, maternity and reproduction are the most typical, and women's property of moving in a constant line forever is ultimate, uniting history in its only unbroken and unbreakable sequence. Whatever else stops, the woman must go on reproducing, as she did in the Siluria of Pteraspis; sex is a vital condition, and race only a local one. The American always ostentatiously ignored sex, and American history mentioned hardly the name of a woman; but the task of accelerating or deflecting the movement of the American woman had interest infinitely greater than that of any race whatever.

On this subject, Adams was conscious of having been born an eighteenth-century remainder. As he grew older, he found that Early Institutions lost their interest, but that Early Women became a passion. Without understanding movement of sex, history seemed to him mere pedantry. The woman seldom knows her own thought; she is as curious to understand herself as the man to understand her. Sometimes, at dinner, one might ask one's liveliest neighbor whether she could explain why the American woman was a failure. Without an instant's hesitation, she was sure to answer: "Because the American man is a failure!" She meant it.

Adams owed more to the American woman than to all the American men he ever heard of, and from the point of view of sex felt much curiosity to know how far she was right, and caught the trick of affirming that the woman was the superior. The cleverer the woman, the less she denied the failure. She was bitter at heart about it. She had failed to hold the family together; the family was extinct like chivalry. She had failed to create a new society that satisfied her, or hold her own in Church or State. Never had the world known a more capable or devoted mother, but at forty her task was over, and she was left with no stage except her old duties, or Washington society where nine men out of ten refused her request to be civilized, and the tenth bored her.

He was studying the laws of motion, and had struck two large questions of vital importance to America—inertia of race and inertia of sex. He had seen artificial energy to the amount of twenty or five-and-twenty million steam horse-power created in America since 1840, which had been socially turned over to the American woman, she being the chief object of social expenditure. According to scientific notions of inertia and force, what ought to be the result?

In Russia, no result had yet shown itself, but in America the results were evident. The woman had been set free—volatilized like Clerk Maxwell's perfect gas; almost brought to the point of explosion, like steam. One had but to walk through the Place Vendome, or join a party of Cook's tourists to Jerusalem, to see that the woman had been set free; but these swarms were ephemeral like clouds of butterflies in season, blown away and lost, while the reproductive sources lay hidden. Behind them, in every city, town, and farmhouse, were myriads of new types—or type-writers—telephone and telegraph-girls, shop-clerks, factory-hands, running into millions, and, as classes, unknown to themselves as to historians. All these new women had been created since 1840; all were to show their meaning before 1940.

Whatever they were, they were not content; and they were hungry for illusions as ever in the fourth century of the Church. The problem remained—to find out whether movement of inertia, inherent in function, could take direction except in lines of inertia.

The American woman at her best exerted great charm on the man, but not the charm of a primitive type. She appeared as the result of a long series of discards, and her chief interest lay in what she had discarded. She seemed making a violent effort to follow the man, who had turned his mind and hand to mechanics. The typical American man had his hand on a lever and his eye on a curve in his road; his living depended on keeping up an average speed of forty miles an hour, tending always to become sixty, eighty, or a hundred, and he could not admit emotions or anxieties or subconscious distractions, more than he could admit whiskey or drugs, without breaking his neck. He could not run his machine and a woman too; he must leave her, even though his wife, to find her own way, and all the world saw her trying to find her way by imitating him.

The result was often tragic, but tragedy had been woman's lot since Eve. The woman's force had counted as inertia of rotation, and her axis of rotation had been the cradle and the family. The idea that she was weak revolted all history; but if her force were to be diverted from its axis, it must find a new field, and the family must pay for it. So far as she succeeded, she must become sexless like the bees, and must leave the old energy of inertia to carry on the race.

The story was not new. For thousands of years women had rebelled, made a fortress of religion, buried themselves in the cloister, in self-sacrifice, in good works—or even in bad. One's studies in the twelfth century, as in the fourth, showed her always busy in the illusions of heaven or of hell—ambition, intrigue, jealousy, magic—but the American woman had no illusions or new resources, and nothing to rebel against, except her own maternity; yet the rebels increased by millions from year to year till they blocked the path of rebellion. Socialism and collectivism, promising paradise on earth for every male, cut off the few avenues of escape capitalism had opened to her, and she saw before her only the future reserved for machine-made, collectivist females.

From the male, she could look for no help; his instinct of power was blind. The Church had known more about women than science will ever know; the historian who studied the sources of Christianity felt sometimes convinced that the Church had been made by the woman chiefly as her protest against man. After the overthrow of the Church, the woman had no refuge except such as the man created for himself. She was free; she was sexless; she had discarded all that the male disliked; though she regretted the discard, she could not go backward. She must, like the man, marry machinery. Already the American man sometimes felt surprise at finding himself regarded as sexless; the American woman was oftener surprised at finding herself regarded as sexual.

No honest historian can take part with—or against—the forces he has to study. To him even the extinction of the human race should be merely a fact to be grouped with other vital statistics. The truth lay somewhere unconscious in the woman's breast. He could draw no conclusions whatever except from the birth-rate. He could not frankly discuss the matter with the young women themselves, because Faust was helpless in the tragedy of woman. The Marguerite of the future could alone decide whether she were better off than the Marguerite of the past; whether she would rather be victim to a man, a church, or a machine.

Between these various forms of inevitable inertia—sex and race—the student of multiplicity felt inclined to admit that—ignorance against ignorance—the Russian problem seemed somewhat easier of treatment than the American. Inertia of race and bulk would require an immense force to overcome it, but in time might perhaps be partially overcome. Inertia of sex could not be overcome without extinguishing the race, yet an immense force, doubling every few years, was working irresistibly to overcome it. One gazed mute before this ocean of darkest ignorance that had already engulfed society. One was almost glad to act the part of horseshoe crab in Quincy Bay, and admit that all was uniform—that nothing ever changed—and that the woman would swim about the ocean of future time, as she had swum in the past, with the gar-fish and the shark, unable to change.

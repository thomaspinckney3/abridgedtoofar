A dynamic theory, like most theories, begins by begging the question: it defines Progress as the development and economy of Forces. It defines force as anything that does, or helps to do work. Man is a force; so is the sun; so is a mathematical point, though without dimensions or known existence.

Man commonly begs the question again by taking for granted that he captures the forces. A dynamic theory, assigning attractive force to opposing bodies in proportion to the law of mass, takes for granted that the forces of nature capture man. The sum of force attracts; the feeble atom or molecule called man is attracted; he suffers education or growth; he is the sum of the forces that attract him; his body and thought are alike their product; the movement of forces controls the progress of his mind, since he can know nothing but the motions impinging on his senses, whose sum makes education.

For convenience as an image, the theory may liken man to a spider in its web, watching for chance prey. Forces of nature dance like flies before the net, and the spider pounces when it can, though it makes many fatal mistakes. The spider-mind acquires memory, and with it a skill of analysis and synthesis, taking apart and putting together the meshes of its trap. Man in the beginning had no such power approaching the spider's, but acute sensibility to the higher forces. Fire taught him secrets no other animal could learn; running water taught him more; the animals helped to educate him; the grasses and grains were academies of study. With little effort on his part, all these forces formed his thought and shaped his figure.

Long before history began, his education was complete, for the record could not have started until he had been taught to record. The universe that had formed him took shape in his mind as a reflection of his own unity, containing all forces except himself. Susceptibility to the highest forces is the highest genius; selection between them is the highest science; their mass is the highest educator. Man always made grotesque blunders in selecting and measuring forces, but he never made a mistake in the value he set on the whole, which he symbolized as unity and worshipped as God. To this day, his attitude towards it has never changed, though science can no longer give to force a name.

Man's function was to assimilate other forces as he assimilated food. He called it the love of power. He felt his feebleness, and sought an ass, a camel, a bow or sling, to widen his range, as he sought fetish or planet in the world beyond. He waited for the object to teach him its use, and the process was slow. According to Arthur Balfour, his lines of illusion had not essentially varied down to 1900.

To the highest attractive energy man gave the name of divine, and for its control he invented Religion, meaning cultivation of occult force. Unable to define Force as a unity, he symbolized it as philosophy and theology; the mind is itself the subtlest of known forces. Hunger, whether for food or for the infinite, sets in motion multiplicity and infinity of thought, and the sure hope of gaining infinite power in eternal life would lift most minds to effort.

He had reached this completeness five thousand years ago, and added nothing to his stock of known forces for a long time. Such progress as the world made consisted in economies of energy rather than its development; proved in mathematics by Archimedes, Aristarchus, Ptolemy, Euclid; in Civil Law; in coinage, most beautiful near its beginning and most barbarous at its close; in roads, ships, harbors, in the use of metals, instruments, and writing — all economies of force, but the roads were still travelled by the horse, the ass, the camel, or the slave; the ships propelled by sails or oars; the lever, the spring, and the screw bounded applied mechanics. Even the metals were old.

Much the same could be said of religious or supernatural forces. Down to the year 300 of the Christian era they were little changed, and in spite of Plato and the sceptics were more apparently chaotic than ever.

There the Western world stood till A.D. 305, when the Emperor Diocletian abdicated; and there it was that Adams broke down on the steps of Ara Coeli, his path blocked by the scandalous failure of civilization at the moment it had achieved complete success. The Pax Romana, the Civil Law, and Free Trade should, in four hundred years, have put Europe far in advance of the point reached by modern society in the four hundred years since 1500.

None of the proposed explanations suited Adams. Rome had not exhausted her resources; on the contrary, the empire developed resources and energies astounding. In northwestern Europe alone it had developed three energies — France, England, and Germany — competent to master the world. The trouble seemed rather that the empire developed too much energy, and too fast.

A dynamic law requires that nature and man react on each other without stop, as the sun and a comet react. The theory exacts excess, rather than deficiency, of action and reaction to account for the dissolution of the Roman Empire, which should have been torn to pieces by acceleration. With the relentless logic of Roman thought, the empire, having established unity on earth, could not help establishing unity in heaven. It was induced by its dynamic necessities to economize the gods.

The Church has long protested against the charge that Christianity ruined the empire. Any dynamic theory gladly admits its reforms saved the State; it asks only to find the force that attracts. Good taste forbids saying that Constantine the Great speculated as audaciously as a modern stock-broker, merging all uncertain forces into a single trust which he enormously overcapitalized; but this is the substance of his Edict of Milan in 313: "We have resolved to grant to Christians as well as all others the liberty to practice the religion they prefer, in order that whatever exists of divinity or celestial power may help and favor us and all who are under our government." The empire pursued power — not merely spiritual but physical — in the sense of Constantine's army order at the Milvian Bridge: In hoc signo vinces! using the Cross as a train of artillery, which, to his mind, it was. Eighty years later, Theodosius marched against Eugene with the Cross for physical champion; Eugene raised Hercules for the pagans; and society on both sides looked on as though it were a boxing-match. The laity bet on the gods as they bet on a horse.

Society was almost wholly pagan in its point of view, and was drawn to the Cross because, in its system of physics, the Cross had absorbed all the old occult or fetish-power. The symbol represented the sum of nature — the Energy of modern science — and society believed it to be as real as X-rays; perhaps it was! The emperors used it like gunpowder in politics; the physicians used it like rays in medicine; the dying clung to it as the quintessence of force, to protect them from the forces of evil on their road to the next life.

Throughout these four centuries the empire knew that religion disturbed economy, but no one could afford a costly machine when he could hire an occult force at trifling expense. Outside fetish-power, the Roman world was incredibly poor; it knew but one productive energy resembling a modern machine — the slave.

The economic needs of a violently centralizing society forced the empire to enlarge its slave-system until the slave-system consumed itself and the empire too, leaving society no resource but further enlargement of its religious system. For a vicious circle, its mathematical completeness approached perfection.

At last, in 410, Alaric sacked Rome, and the slave-ridden Western Empire went to pieces. Society felt deeply the disappointment in its new power, the Cross, which had failed to protect its Church. The outcry became so loud among Christians that its literary champion, Bishop Augustine of Hippo, wrote a famous treatise in defence, defending feebly the mechanical value of the symbol but insisting on its spiritual value in the Civitas Dei: "Granted that we have lost all we had! Have we lost faith? Have we lost piety? Have we lost the wealth of the inner man who is rich before God? These are the wealth of Christians!" The Civitas Dei in its turn became the sum of attraction for the Western world, though it showed the same weakness in mechanics that had wrecked the Civitas Romae.

The attraction remained constant. The delight of experimenting on occult force absorbs all the free thought of the human race. Never has Nature offered the brain so violent a stimulant as when she opened to it the possibility of sharing infinite power in eternal life, and it might well need a thousand years of experiment to prove the value of the motive. During these Middle Ages, the Western mind reacted in many forms — Romanesque and Gothic architecture, glass windows and mosaic walls, sculpture and poetry, war and love — which still affect some people as the noblest work of man, so that great masses of tourists travel from far countries to look at Ravenna and San Marco, Palermo and Pisa, Assisi, Cordova, Chartres, with vague notions about the force that created them.

The dynamic scheme began by asserting that between the Pyramids (B.C. 3000) and the Cross (A.D. 300), no new force affected Western progress; the motive influence which raised both was the same attraction of power in a future life that raised the dome of Sancta Sofia and the Cathedral at Amiens. No single event has more puzzled historians than the sudden appearance of at least two new natural forces of the highest educational value in mechanics. Literally, they seemed to drop from the sky at the precise moment when the Cross on one side and the Crescent on the other proclaimed the complete triumph of the Civitas Dei.

Of the compass, one may confidently say that it proved, better than any other force, the widening scope of the mind, since it widened immensely the range of contact between nature and thought. The compass educated.

Of Greek fire and gunpowder, the same cannot be said, for they have the air of accidents due to religious motives. They were chemical forces, mostly explosives — the most violent educators ever known to man, justly feared as diabolic. The Sieur de Joinville left a record of how Greek fire educated the French mind in a single night in 1249. The good king St. Louis and all his staff dropped on their knees at every fiery flame that flew by, praying — "God have pity on us!" The battle of religion between Christian and Saracen was trifling compared with that of education between gunpowder and the Cross.

The fiction that society educated itself, or aimed at a conscious purpose, was upset by the compass and gunpowder. When the Crescent drove the Cross from Constantinople in 1453, Gutenberg and Fust were printing their first Bible at Mainz, thinking they helped the Cross. When Columbus discovered the West Indies in 1492, the Church looked on it as a victory of the Cross. When Luther and Calvin upset Europe, they were trying to substitute the Civitas Dei for the Civitas Romae. When the Puritans set out for New England in 1620, they too looked to found a Civitas Dei in State Street; and when Bunyan made his Pilgrimage in 1678, he repeated St. Jerome. Even when the Church burned Giordano Bruno in 1600 and condemned Galileo in 1630, it condemned anarchists, not atheists. None of the astronomers were irreligious men. Neither Galileo nor Kepler, neither Spinoza nor Descartes, neither Leibnitz nor Newton, any more than Constantine the Great, doubted Unity. The utmost range of their heresies reached only its personality.

This persistence of thought-inertia is the leading idea of modern history. Except as reflected in himself, man has no reason for assuming unity in the universe. The a priori insistence on this unity ended by fatiguing the more active minds; and Lord Bacon tried to stop it. He urged society to lay aside the idea of evolving the universe from a thought, and to try evolving thought from the universe. "Nature, to be commanded, must be obeyed." "The imagination must be given not wings but weights." As Galileo reversed the action of earth and sun, Bacon reversed the relation of thought to force. The mind was thenceforth to follow the movement of matter, and unity must be left to shift for itself.

The revolution seemed voluntary, but was as mechanical as the fall of a feather. After 1500, the speed of progress so rapidly surpassed man's gait as to alarm every one, as though it were the acceleration of a falling body. The telescope held it rigidly standing on its head; the microscope revealed a universe that defied the senses; gunpowder killed whole races that lagged behind; the compass coerced the most imbruted mariner to act on the impossible idea that the earth was round; the press drenched Europe with anarchism. Europe saw itself wrenched into false positions, drawn along new lines as a fish caught on a hook, unable to understand by what force it was controlled. Its contortions in the eighteenth century are best studied in the wit of Voltaire, but all philosophy from Montaigne and Pascal to Schopenhauer and Nietzsche deals with nothing else; and still the Baconian law held good — thought did not evolve nature, but nature evolved thought.

Slowly the accretion of these new forces, chemical and mechanical, grew until they took the place of the old religious science. Nature, not mind, did the work. Bacon foretold it: "Neither the naked hand nor the understanding, left to itself, can effect much. It is by instruments and helps that the work is done." For his true followers science always meant self-restraint, obedience, sensitiveness to impulse from without. "Non fingendum aut excogitandum sed inveniendum quid Natura faciat aut ferat."

Evidently a new variety of mind had appeared. Certain men merely held out their hands — Newton watched an apple; Franklin flew a kite; Watt played with a tea-kettle — and great forces of nature stuck to them as though she were playing ball. Governments did almost nothing but resist. Society was hostile or indifferent, until all mankind seemed dragged on by an attractive power in advance, which even the leaders obeyed without understanding, as the planets obeyed gravity.

As Nature developed her hidden energies, they tended to become destructive. Thought itself became tortured, suffering reluctantly, impatiently, painfully, the coercion of new method. The stupendous acceleration after 1800 ended in 1900 with the appearance of the new class of supersensual forces, before which the man of science stood at first as bewildered and helpless as, in the fourth century, a priest of Isis before the Cross of Christ.

This, then, would be a dynamic formula of history. Any schoolboy knows enough to object that it is the oldest and most universal of theories. Church and State, theology and philosophy, have always preached it, differing only in the allotment of energy between nature and man. Whether the attractive energy be called God or Nature, the mechanism has been the same. Science has proved that forces surround, traverse, vibrate, rotate, repel, attract, without stop; man's senses are conscious of few; but from the beginning of organic existence, his consciousness has been induced, expanded, trained in the lines of his sensitiveness; the rise of his faculties may be due to assimilating outside forces. Beyond the lines of force felt by the senses, the universe may be a supersensuous chaos or a divine unity, irresistibly attractive, and either life or death to penetrate. As the mind enlarged its range, it enlarged the field of complexity, and must continue, even into chaos, until the reservoirs of energies are exhausted, or he succumbs to their excess.

For past history, this way of grouping its sequences may answer for a chart of relations; but past history is only a value of relation to the future, and this value is wholly one of convenience, which can be tested only by experiment. Any law of movement must include some mechanical formula of acceleration.

HARVARD COLLEGE (1854-1858)

One day in June, 1854, young Adams walked for the last time down the steps of Mr. Dixwell's school in Boylston Place, and felt only unqualified joy that this experience was ended. Never again did he close so long a period without some sense of loss; school alone seemed an intolerable bore. He was born too old for it, as most New England boys were. Their education as men should have begun at ten. For them the first six years of a possible education were wasted in doing imperfectly what might have been done perfectly in one, and in any case would have had small value. The next step was Harvard College. He was more than glad to go.

For generations, Adamses and Brookses and Boylstons and Gorhams had gone to Harvard, and though none of them was the better for it, custom, ties, convenience, and economy kept the track. No one took Harvard seriously. All went because their friends went; the College was their ideal of social self-respect.

Harvard, as far as it educated at all, was a mild and liberal school, which sent young men into the world with what they needed to make respectable citizens, and something of what they wanted to make useful ones. Leaders it never tried to make. The Unitarian clergy gave it a character of moderation, balance, judgment, restraint—what the French called mesure. Excellent traits it attained with singular success, so that its graduates could commonly be recognized by the stamp; but such a type rarely lent itself to autobiography. In effect, the school created a type but not a will. Four years, if successful, resulted in an autobiographical blank, a mind on which only a water-mark had been stamped.

The stamp, as such things went, was a good one. The chief wonder of education is that it does not ruin everybody concerned in it. Sometimes in after life Adams debated whether it had not ruined him and most of his companions, but Harvard was probably less hurtful than any other university then in existence. It taught little, and that little ill, but it left the mind open, free from bias, ignorant of facts, but docile; supple, ready to receive knowledge.

What most disappointed the boy was the little he got from his mates. Speaking exactly, he got less than nothing, a result common enough in education. The Class of 1858 shows a list rather distinguished in their time—Alexander Agassiz and Phillips Brooks led it; H. H. Richardson and O. W. Holmes helped to close it—yet as help towards education he got nothing whatever from them or they from him until long after they had left college. Accident counts for much in companionship as in marriage; boys brought up together under like conditions have nothing to give each other. The Class of 1858, to which he belonged, was a typical collection of young New Englanders, quietly penetrating and aggressively commonplace; free from meannesses, jealousies, intrigues, enthusiasms, and passions; singularly indifferent to display; distrustful of themselves, and little disposed to trust any one else; with not much humor of their own, but readiness to enjoy that of others; negative to a degree that in the long run became positive and triumphant. Not harsh, rather liberal and open-minded, they were still as a body the most formidable critics one would care to meet. They never flattered, seldom praised; their attitude was a law of nature; their judgment beyond appeal, not an act either of intellect or emotion or of will, but a sort of gravitation. This was Harvard College incarnate, and the Class of 1858 somewhat extreme.

Of unity this band of nearly a hundred young men had no keen sense, but little energy of repulsion. They were pleasant to live with; chiefly because each individual appeared satisfied to stand alone. It seemed a sign of force; yet to stand alone is quite natural when one has no passions; still easier when one has no pains.

Into this dissolvent medium, chance enlarged Henry Adams’s education by tossing a trio of Virginians as little fitted for it as Sioux Indians to a treadmill. By further affinity, they fell into relation with the Bostonians, and in the end with Adams himself, though they and he knew how thin an edge of friendship separated them in 1856 from mortal enmity. One was the son of Colonel Robert E. Lee; the two others were town-Virginians from Petersburg. A fourth outsider came from Cincinnati and was half Kentuckian. For the first time his education brought him in contact with new types and taught him their values. He saw the New England type measure itself with another and was part of the process.

Lee, known through life as “Roony,” was a Virginian of the eighteenth century, much as Adams was a Bostonian of the same age. Tall, largely built, handsome, genial, with liberal Virginian openness, he had also the habit of command and took leadership as his natural habit. No one cared to contest it; none of the New Englanders wanted command. For a year, at least, Lee was the most popular and prominent young man in his class, but then slowly dropped into the background. The habit of command was not enough, and the Virginian had little else. He was simple beyond analysis; so simple that even the simple New England student could not realize him. No one knew enough to know how ignorant he was; how childlike; how helpless before the relative complexity of a school. As an animal, the Southerner seemed to have every advantage, but even as an animal he steadily lost ground.

The lesson in education was vital to these young men, who, within ten years, killed each other by scores in the act of testing their college conclusions. Strictly, the Southerner had no mind; he had temperament. He was not a scholar; he had no intellectual training; he could not analyze an idea, and he could not even conceive of admitting two; but in life one could get on very well without ideas, if one had only the social instinct. Dozens of eminent statesmen were men of Lee’s type and maintained themselves in the legislature, but college was a sharper test. The habits of neither section were good; both were apt to drink hard and to live low lives; but the Bostonian suffered less than the Virginian. Commonly the Bostonian could take some care of himself even in his worst stages, while the Virginian became quarrelsome and dangerous. When a Virginian had brooded a few days over an imaginary grief and substantial whiskey, none of his Northern friends could be sure that he might not be waiting, round the corner, with a knife or pistol, to revenge insult by the dry light of delirium tremens; and when things reached this condition, Lee had to exhaust his authority over his own staff. Lee was a gentleman of the old school; he kept his temper and his friends under control.

Adams liked the Virginians. No one was more obnoxious to them by name and prejudice; yet their friendship was unbroken and even warm. At a moment when the immediate future posed no problem so vital as the relative energy and endurance of North and South, this contact with Southern character was a sort of education for its own sake. No doubt the self-esteem of the Yankee, which tended to self-distrust, was flattered by gaining the slow conviction that the Southerner, with his slave-owning limitations, was as little fit to succeed in the struggle of modern life as though he were still a maker of stone axes, living in caves, and hunting the bos primigenius, and that every quality in which he was strong, made him weaker; but Adams began to fear that even in this respect one eighteenth-century type might not differ deeply from another. Roony Lee had changed little from the Virginian of a century before; but Adams was himself a good deal nearer the type of his great-grandfather than to that of a railway superintendent. He was little more fit than the Virginians to deal with a future America which showed no fancy for the past. Already Northern society betrayed a preference for economists over diplomats or soldiers—one might even call it a jealousy—against which two eighteenth-century types had little chance to live, and which they had in common to fear.

Nothing short of this curious sympathy could have brought into close relations two young men so hostile as Lee and Adams; but the chief difference between them as collegians consisted only in scholarship: Lee was a total failure; Adams a partial one. Lee felt his failure more sensibly, and gladly seized escape by accepting a commission offered him by General Winfield Scott in the force being organized against the Mormons. He asked Adams to write his letter of acceptance, which flattered Adams’s vanity more than any Northern compliment; in days of violent bitterness it showed good temper. The diplomat felt his profession.

If the student got little from his mates, he got little more from his masters. The four years passed at college were, for his purposes, wasted. He did not want to be one in a hundred—one per cent of an education. He regarded himself as the only person for whom his education had value, and wanted the whole of it. He got barely half an average. Long afterwards, when life led him back to teach in his turn what no student naturally cared or needed to know, he looked up his record and found himself graded precisely in the middle. In the one branch he most needed—mathematics—failure was so nearly universal that grading had little value. At best he could never have been a mathematician; at worst he would never have cared to be one; but he needed to read mathematics, like any other universal language, and he never reached the alphabet.

Beyond two or three Greek plays, he got nothing from the ancient languages. Beyond some incoherent theories of free-trade and protection, he got little from Political Economy. He did not remember to have heard the name of Karl Marx or the title of “Capital.” He was equally ignorant of Auguste Comte. These were the two writers of his time who most influenced its thought. The bit of practical teaching he afterwards reviewed with most curiosity was Chemistry, which taught him a number of theories that befogged his mind for a lifetime. The only teaching that appealed to his imagination was a course by Louis Agassiz on the Glacial Period and Paleontology, which had more influence on his curiosity than the rest of the instruction altogether. The entire work of the four years could have been easily put into the work of any four months in after life.

Harvard was a negative force, and negative forces have value. Slowly it weakened the violent political bias of childhood, not by putting interests in its place, but by mental habits which had no bias at all. It would also have weakened the literary bias, had he been capable of finding other amusement, but the climate kept him steady to desultory and useless reading, till he had run through libraries whose titles he forgot. Rather by instinct than by guidance, he turned to writing, and his professors or tutors occasionally gave his English composition a hesitating approval; but even when he made a long struggle for recognition, he never convinced his teachers that his abilities warranted placing him among the first third of his class. Instructors generally reach a fairly accurate gauge of their scholars’ powers. He himself held the opinion that his instructors were nearly right; later, as professor, after making mortifying mistakes in ranking his own scholars, he still insisted that on the whole he was not far wrong. Student or professor, he accepted the negative standard because it was the standard of the school.

From the first he wanted to be done with it, and stood watching vaguely for a path and a direction. The world seemed large, but the paths that led into it were not many and lay mostly through Boston, where he did not want to go. By pure chance the first door of escape that seemed to offer a hope led into Germany, and James Russell Lowell opened it.

Lowell, on succeeding Longfellow as Professor of Belles-Lettres, had duly gone to Germany and brought back whatever he found to bring. The literary world then agreed that truth survived in Germany alone, and Carlyle, Matthew Arnold, Renan, Emerson, with scores of followers, taught the German faith. The literary world had revolted against the yoke of coming capitalism—its money-lenders, bank directors, and railway magnates. Thackeray and Dickens followed Balzac in scratching and biting the middle class with savage ill-temper. The satirists and idealists seized the press and, as they thought the Second Empire a disgrace to France and a danger to England, turned to Germany because at that moment Germany was neither economical nor military, and a hundred years behind western Europe in the simplicity of its standard. German thought, method, honesty, and even taste became the standards of scholarship. Goethe was raised to the rank of Shakespeare—Kant ranked as a law-giver above Plato. All serious scholars were obliged to become German, for German thought was revolutionizing criticism. Lowell had followed, not very enthusiastically, but with sufficient conviction, and invited his scholars to join him. Adams accepted the invitation, rather for Lowell than for Germany, but in good faith. It was the first serious attempt he had made to direct his own education; he was sure of getting some education out of it; not perhaps what he expected, but at least a path.

Singularly circuitous and excessively wasteful of energy the path proved to be, but he could never see what other was open to him; he could have done no better had he foreseen every stage of his coming life, and would probably have done worse. The preliminary step was pure gain. Lowell had brought back the habit of allowing students to read with him privately in his study. Adams asked the privilege, and used it to read a little, and to talk a great deal, for the personal contact pleased and flattered him, as that of older men ought to flatter and please the young. Lowell was a new element in the boy’s life. As practical a New Englander as any, he leaned towards the Concord faith rather than Boston; for Concord, in the dark days of 1856, glowed with pure light. Adams approached it much as he would have entered a Gothic Cathedral, for he knew the priests regarded him as only a worm. To the Concord Church all Adamses were minds of dust and emptiness, devoid of feeling, poetry or imagination; little higher than the common scourings of State Street. Already, at eighteen, he had begun to feel uncertainty about so many matters more important than Adamses that his mind rebelled against no discipline merely personal; he was ready to admit his unworthiness if only he might penetrate the shrine. He was slipping away from fixed principles; from Mount Vernon Street; from Quincy; from the eighteenth century; and his first steps led toward Concord.

He never reached Concord, and to Concord Church he, like the rest of mankind who accepted a material universe, remained always an insect—or something much lower—a man. It was surely no fault of his that the universe seemed to him real; perhaps—as Mr. Emerson justly said—it was so. In spite of a lifetime’s effort, he fell back into the heresy that if anything universal was unreal, it was himself and not the appearances; it was the poet and not the banker; his own thought, not the thing that moved it. He did not lack the wish to be transcendental. Concord seemed to him, at one time, more real than Quincy; yet in truth Russell Lowell was as little transcendental as Beacon Street. From him the boy got no revolutionary thought—objective or subjective as they used to call it—but good-humored encouragement to do what amused him, which consisted in passing two years in Europe after finishing the four at Cambridge.

The result seemed small in proportion to the effort, but it was the only positive result he could ever trace to the influence of Harvard, and he doubted whether Harvard influenced even that. Negative results in plenty he could trace, but he tended towards negation on his own account, as one side of the New England mind had always done; even there he could never feel sure that Harvard had more than reflected a weakness. In his opinion the education was not serious; hardly any Boston student took it seriously; none was sure that President Walker himself, or President Felton after him, took it more seriously than the students. For them all, the college offered chiefly advantages vulgarly called social, rather than mental.

Unluckily for this boy, social advantages were his only capital in life. Of money he had not much, of mind not more, but he could be certain that, barring his own faults, his social position would never be questioned. What he needed was a career in which social position had value. Never would he have to explain who he was; never need acquaintance to strengthen his standing; but he needed some one to show how to use the acquaintance he cared to make. He made no acquaintance in college which proved to have the smallest use in after life. All his Boston friends he knew before; contact of Bostonian with Bostonian was the last education they needed. Cordial as their relations were, they all flew off in different directions the moment they took their degrees. Harvard remained a tie, but a tie little stronger than Beacon Street and not so strong as State Street. A stranger like H. H. Richardson, who came from far New Orleans, and had his career before him to chase rather than to guide, might make valuable friendships; certainly Adams made none that he valued more in after life than Richardson, but the college relation had little to do with the later friendship. Life is a narrow valley, and the roads run close together. He would have attached himself to Richardson in any case, as he attached himself to others not at Harvard. The valley of life grew more narrow with years, and men with common tastes were bound to come together. He only knew that he would have felt himself on a more equal footing had he been less ignorant, and had he not thrown away ten years in acquiring what he might have acquired in one.

Socially or intellectually, the college was for him negative and in some ways mischievous. The most tolerant man of the world could not see good in the lower habits of the students, but the vices were less harmful than the virtues. The habit of drinking may have done no great harm; but the habit of looking at life as a social relation—an affair of society—did no good. It cultivated a weakness which needed no cultivation. If it had helped to make men of the world, or given the manners and instincts of any profession—temper, patience, courtesy, a faculty of profiting by the social defects of opponents—it would have been education worth more than mathematics or languages; but so far as it helped to make anything, it helped only to make the college standard permanent through life. The Bostonian educated at Harvard remained a collegian, if he stuck only to what the college gave. If parents went on, generation after generation, sending their children to Harvard for its social advantages, they perpetuated an inferior social type, as ill-fitted as the Oxford type for success in the next generation.

Luckily the old social standard, as President Walker or James Russell Lowell still showed it, was admirable, and if it had little practical value or personal influence on the mass, at least it preserved the tradition for those who liked it. The Harvard graduate was neither American nor European, nor even wholly Yankee; his admirers were few, his critics many; perhaps his worst weakness was his self-criticism and self-consciousness; but his ambitions, social or intellectual, were necessarily cheap even though negative. Afraid of serious risks, and still more afraid of ridicule, he seldom made a great failure of life, and nearly always led a life more or less worth living. So Henry Adams, well aware that he could not succeed as a scholar, and finding his social position beyond improvement or need of effort, betook himself to the single ambition that otherwise would scarcely have seemed a true outcome of the college, though it was the last remnant of the old Unitarian supremacy. He took to the pen. He wrote.

The College Magazine printed his work, and the Societies listened to his addresses. Lavish of praise the readers were not; the audiences listened in silence; but grave silence was the form of patience that meant possible future acceptance, and he went on writing. Soon he began to suffer from reaching his own limits. He could not be this—or that—or the other; always precisely the things he wanted to be. He had not wit or scope or force. Judges always ranked him beneath a rival, if he had any; and he believed the judges were right. His work seemed to him thin, commonplace, feeble. At best it showed only a feeling for form; an instinct of exclusion. Nothing shocked—not even its weakness.

Inevitably effort creates an ambition, and at that time the ambition of the literary student, which almost took the place of the regular prizes, was to be chosen Class Orator. This was political as well as literary success—precisely the eighteenth-century combination that fascinated an eighteenth-century boy. The idea lurked as a dream; he was outside the popular men. Year by year, his position seemed to improve, or rivals disappeared, until at last, to his astonishment, he found himself a candidate. The habits of the college permitted no active candidacy; he and his rivals had not a word to say for or against themselves; he was never consulted; he was not present at the proceedings; but it did happen that, one evening on returning from Boston, he received notice of his election, after a close contest, as Class Orator over the head of the first scholar, who was undoubtedly a better orator and a more popular man. In politics the success of the poorer candidate is common enough, and Henry Adams was a fairly trained politician, but he never understood how he defeated not only a more capable but a more popular rival.

To him the election seemed a miracle. This was no mock-modesty; he knew his rivals and their following as well as himself. What he did not know, even after four years, was Harvard College. He could never measure the bewildering impersonality of the men, who, at twenty, set no value either on official or personal standards. Here were nearly a hundred young men who had lived together intimately during four of the most impressionable years, and who, not only once but again and again, deliberately chose as their representatives precisely those of their companions who seemed least to represent them. As far as these Orators and Marshals had any position in a collegiate sense, it was that of indifference to the college. He never professed the smallest faith in universities of any kind; nor had he the faintest admiration for the university graduate; as a collegian he was known apart only by his habit of standing outside; and yet the singular fact remained that this commonplace body chose him to express his and their commonplaces. Secretly the successful candidate flattered himself—and them—with the hope that they might perhaps not be so commonplace as they thought; but this was only another proof that all were identical. They saw in him a representative—the kind they wanted—and he saw in them the most formidable array of judges he could ever meet, like so many mirrors of himself, an infinite reflection of his own shortcomings.

All the same, the choice was flattering; so flattering that it actually shocked his vanity, and would have shocked it more had he known it was to be the only flattery of the sort he was ever to receive. The function of Class Day was, in the eyes of nine-tenths, the most important of the college, and the figure of the Orator the most conspicuous. Unlike the Orators at regular Commencements, the Class Day Orator stood alone, or had only the Poet for rival. Crowded into the large church, the students, their families, friends, aunts, uncles and chaperones, attended with all the girls of sixteen or twenty, and there, in heat that might have melted bronze, they listened to an Orator and a Poet in gowns, reciting such platitudes as their experience and mild censors permitted. What he said he soon forgot, nor had it the least value for education; but he remembered what was said of it. One eminent relation remarked that, as the work of so young a man, the oration was singularly wanting in enthusiasm. The young man asked himself whether, rhetoric aside, this absence of enthusiasm was a defect or a merit, since it was all that Harvard taught, and all that the hundred young men, whom he tried to represent, expressed. Another comment threw more light. One elderly gentleman noticed the orator’s “perfect self-possession.”

Self-possession indeed! If Harvard gave nothing else, it gave calm. For four years each student had been obliged to figure daily before dozens who knew each other to the last fibre. One had done little but read papers to Societies, or act comedy in the Hasty Pudding, not to speak of regular exercises, and no audience in future life would ever be so intimately and terribly intelligent as these. Three-fourths of the graduates would rather have addressed the Council of Trent or the British Parliament than have acted Sir Anthony Absolute before a gala audience of the Hasty Pudding. Self-possession was the strongest part of Harvard, which certainly taught men to stand alone, so that nothing seemed stranger to its graduates than the paroxysms of terror before the public which often overcame the graduates of European universities.

Whether this was, or was not, education, Henry Adams never knew. He was ready to stand up before any audience in America or Europe, with nerves rather steadier for the excitement, but whether he should ever have anything to say, remained to be proved. As yet he knew nothing. Education had not begun.

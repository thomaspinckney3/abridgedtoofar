## V. Berlin (1858–1859)

A fourth child has the strength of his weakness. Being of no great value, he may throw himself away if he likes, and never be missed. Charles Francis Adams felt no love for Europe, which, as he and all the world agreed, unfitted Americans for America. A captious critic might have replied that all the success he or his father or his grandfather achieved was chiefly due to the field that Europe gave them; without it they would likely have remained local politicians to the end. So much more timid are parents for their children than for themselves that Mr. and Mrs. Adams would have been content to keep their children forever in Mount Vernon Street—could they have relied on the moral influences of Boston, which they could not. On all sides were risks. The boy Henry wanted to go to Europe; he seemed well behaved when anyone was looking at him; his morals were apparently good; and above all he was timid and showed a certain self-respect in public view. Therefore, when he presented to an exceedingly indulgent father and mother his request to begin at a German university the study of the Civil Law—although neither he nor they knew what the Civil Law was, or any reason for his studying it—the parents dutifully consented, and walked with him down to the railway station at Quincy to bid him good-bye, with a smile which he almost thought a tear.

He began this third or fourth attempt at education in November, 1858, by sailing on the steamer *Persia*, the pride of the Cunard Line. On the third day the world ran into a heavy storm. He learned then a lesson that stood by him better than any university teaching—the meaning of a November gale on the mid-Atlantic, which for mere physical misery passed endurance. The stride in knowledge on this first month of travel seemed gigantic: a great many impressions were needed to make very little education, but how many could be crowded into one day without making any education at all became the *pons asinorum* of tourist mathematics.

The *Persia* and Captain Judkins vanished one Sunday morning in the Mersey, to give way to Liverpool in November murk—followed instantly by Chester's red-sandstone romance. When the boys went out to Eaton Hall, aristocracy was real in a way Thackeray or Dickens would have felt. The very name of Grosvenor struck a note of grandeur; the portraits, the terraces, the gardens actually set the rich English nobleman apart from Americans and shopkeepers. So was the England of Dickens: Oliver Twist and Little Nell lurked in every churchyard shadow, not as shadows but alive. An eighteenth-century American boy fresh from Boston naturally took it all for education, or at least thought he felt it.

Then came the journey through Birmingham and the Black District—the plunge into darkness lurid with flames, the sense of unknown horror in a gloom that had existed nowhere else except in volcanic craters, followed by the soft green charm one glided into on emerging. The boy ran away from it, as he ran away from everything he disliked—not knowing that Karl Marx was standing there waiting for him, and that the process of education would eventually have to deal with Karl Marx far more than with Professor Bowen of Harvard.

London in November 1858 was still the London of the eighteenth century. History muttered down Fleet Street like Dr. Johnson; Vanity Fair was alive on Piccadilly in yellow chariots with coachmen in wigs and footmen with canes; half the great houses bore large funereal hatchments. Education went backward. Dropped next into Berlin without guide or direction, the young man in search of education floundered in a mere mess of misunderstandings. He ran about with the rest to beer-cellars and music-halls, smoking bad tobacco and eating sauerkraut, which was easy enough—one can always descend the social ladder. The trouble came when he asked for the education he had been promised. His friends led him to his first lecture on the Civil Law.

His first lecture was his last. One hour satisfied him that he had made another failure in education, and this time a fatal one. The language would require three months' hard work before he could touch the Law, but the worse shock was the university itself. Harvard College had seemed torpid; it was instinct with life compared to Berlin. The professor mumbled his comments; the students made notes; they could have learned from books in a day more than he gave them in a month, but they must pay his fees and follow his course. To an American the result was worthless.

His friends suggested he was at least learning the language. So the young man fell back on the language, and being slow at it, he fell behind all his companions. One day on the Linden he caught sight of Charles Sumner in a cab and ran after him. Sumner, then recovering from the blows of the South Carolinian's cane, was pleased to find a young worshipper in the remote Prussian wilderness. He tried to encourage Adams about the language: "I came to Berlin," he said with his grand air of mastery, "unable to say a word; and three months later when I went away, I talked it to my cabman." Adams felt quite unable to attain such social advantages in so short a time, and one day complained to Mr. Robert Apthorp of Boston, who was wintering in Berlin for its music. Apthorp described his own remedy: he had entered a public school and sat for months with ten-year-old boys, reciting their lessons and catching their phrases. The idea suited Adams's desperate frame of mind. Mr. Apthorp negotiated with the head-master of the Friedrichs-Wilhelm-Werdersches Gymnasium, and there Adams went for three months as a member of the *Ober-tertia*, a class of boys twelve or thirteen years old. He never did anything else so foolish, but he was given a bit of education which served him some purpose in life.

The German high school was something very near an indictable nuisance. The arbitrary training given to the memory was stupefying; no other faculty seemed to be recognized—least of all was any use made of reason, analytic or synthetic. All State education is a sort of dynamo machine for polarizing the popular mind, turning and holding its lines of force in the direction most effective for State purposes. The German machine was terribly efficient. The boys never breathed fresh air; in all Berlin not a cubic inch of oxygen was admitted in winter into an inhabited building; when the American opened a window in the five minutes between hours, he was invariably rebuked. With foul air and bad food, the boys were required to prepare daily lessons that would have broken down strong men of a healthy habit.

Yet the curious and perplexing result of the total failure of German education was that the student's only clear gain—his single step to a higher life—came from time wasted, studies neglected, education reversed. It came from the despised beer-garden and music-hall; and it was accidental, unintended, unforeseen.

When his companions insisted on passing two or three afternoons a week at music-halls, drinking beer and looking at fat German women knitting while an orchestra played, Adams went with them for the company but with no pretense of enjoyment. When Mr. Apthorp gently protested that Adams enjoyed Beethoven, Adams replied simply that he loathed Beethoven—and felt slight surprise when the others laughed as though it were humor. He supposed that, except musicians, everyone thought Beethoven a bore, as everyone except mathematicians thought mathematics a bore. Sitting thus at his beer-table, mentally impassive, he was one day surprised to notice that his mind followed the movement of a *Sinfonie*. He could not have been more astonished had he suddenly read a new language. A prison-wall that had barred his senses on one great side of life suddenly fell, of its own accord, without so much as his knowing when it happened. Amid the fumes of coarse tobacco and poor beer, surrounded by the commonest of German *Hausfrauen*, **a new sense burst out like a flower in his life**—so superior to the old senses, so bewildering, so astonished at its own existence, that he could not credit it, and watched it as something apart, accidental, and not to be trusted. He slowly came to admit that Beethoven had partly become intelligible to him, but was the more inclined to think Beethoven must be much overrated as a musician, to be so easily followed.

No other sense awoke. His sense of line and color remained as dull as ever; his metaphysical sense did not spring into life. When April came and some genius suggested a tramp in Thuringia, his heart sang like a bird; he realized what a nightmare he had suffered; and he made up his mind that, wherever else he might seek education in the infinities of space and time, it should not be again in Berlin.

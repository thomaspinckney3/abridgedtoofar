The tramp in Thuringen lasted four-and-twenty hours. By the end of the first walk his three companions—John Bancroft, James J. Higginson, and B. W. Crowninshield, all Boston and Harvard—were satisfied with what they had seen; and when they sat down where Goethe had written, "Warte nur! balde Rubest du auch!" the profundity of the counsel so affected them that they hired a wagon and drove to Weimar the same night. They were happy in the first fresh breath of leafless spring, and the beer was better than at Berlin; but all were equally in doubt why they had come to Germany, and none could say why they stayed. Adams stayed because he did not want to go home, and feared his father's patience might be exhausted if he asked to waste time elsewhere.

They could not think their education required a return to Berlin. A few days at Dresden satisfied them that Dresden was a better spot for general education than Berlin, and equally good for reading Civil Law. There was nothing to study in Dresden and no education to be gained, but the Sistine Madonna and the Correggios were famous; the theatre and opera were sometimes excellent; the Elbe was prettier than the Spree; and one could always fall back on the language. So he took a room in the household of the usual small government clerk with the usual plain daughters, and continued the study of the language. For the next eighteen months he pursued accidental education, since he could pursue no other; and by great good fortune, Europe and America were too busy with their own affairs to give much attention to his. Accidental education had every chance in its favor, especially because nothing came amiss.

Perhaps the chief obstacle to the youth's education, now that he had come of age, was his honesty—his simple-minded faith in his intentions. Even after Berlin had become a nightmare, he still persuaded himself that his German education was a success. He loved, or thought he loved, the people; but the Germany he loved was the eighteenth century which the Germans were ashamed of, and were destroying as fast as they could. Military Germany was his abhorrence. What he liked was the simple character; the good-natured sentiment; the musical and metaphysical abstraction; the blundering incapacity for practical affairs. Everyone looked on Germany as incapable of competing with France, England or America in organized energy. Until coal-power and railways were created, she was mediaeval by nature and geography, and this was what Adams, under the teachings of Carlyle and Lowell, liked.

He was in a fair way to do himself lasting harm, floundering between worlds passed and worlds coming, which had a habit of crushing men who stayed too long at the points of contact. Suddenly the Emperor Napoleon declared war on Austria and raised a confused point of morals in the mind of Europe. One morning the government clerk rushed in to consult a map and measure the distance from Milan to Dresden. The third Napoleon had reached Lombardy; only fifty or sixty years had passed since the first Napoleon began there.

An enlightened young American, with eighteenth-century tastes capped by fragments of a German education and the most excellent intentions, had to make up his mind about the moral value of these forces. France was the wicked spirit of moral politics, and whatever helped France must be evil. Austria was another evil spirit. Italy was the prize they disputed; for at least fifteen hundred years the question of sympathy had been put in cross-lights, and wisdom had been baffled by it. Better take sides first, and reason for the rest of life.

Not that Adams felt real doubt about sympathies or wishes; he had not been German long enough for befogging to that point. But the moment was decisive for political morals. Steam and electricity had brought new concentrations, or were making them necessary in the line of his moral principles—freedom, education, economic development—which required association with allies as doubtful as Napoleon III, and robberies with violence on a very extensive scale. As long as he could argue that his opponents were wicked, he could join in robbing and killing them without a qualm; but it might happen that the good were robbed. Education insisted on finding a moral foundation for robbery. He could hope to begin life in the character of no animal more moral than a monkey unless he could satisfy himself when and why robbery and murder were a virtue and duty. Education founded on mere self-interest was merely Guelph and Ghibelline over again—Machiavelli translated into American.

Luckily he had a sister much brighter than he ever was—though he thought himself a rather superior person—who, after marrying Charles Kuhn of Philadelphia, had come to Italy, and was hotly Italian. In July, 1859, at Thun, Henry joined them. Women commonly have a very positive moral sense; that which they will, is right; that which they reject, is wrong; and their will commonly ends by settling the moral. Mrs. Kuhn not only adored Italy; she cordially disliked Germany in all its varieties. She saw no gain in helping her brother to be Germanized, and wanted him much to be civilized. She was the first young woman he was ever intimate with—quick, sensitive, wilful, energetic, sympathetic, and intelligent enough to supply a score of men with ideas—and he was delighted to give her the reins, to let her drive him where she would. It was his first experiment in giving the reins to a woman, and he was so much pleased with the results that he never wanted to take them back. In after life he made a general law of experience—no woman had ever driven him wrong; no man had ever driven him right.

Nothing would satisfy Mrs. Kuhn but to go to the seat of war as soon as the armistice was declared. They crossed the St. Gothard and reached Milan, picturesque with every sort of uniform and every sign of war. To young Adams this first plunge into Italy passed Beethoven as a piece of accidental education: not a means of pursuing life, but one of the ends attained. It had but one defect—that of attainment.

Not satisfied with Milan, Mrs. Kuhn insisted on invading the enemy's country by the Stelvio Pass. The Valtellina showed war; Garibaldi's Cacciatori were the only visible inhabitants. Handsome young officers, delighted to talk of their battles, could not say whether the abhorred Austrian Jagers would let travellers through. After climbing what was said to be the finest carriage-pass in Europe, the carriage turned the last shoulder where the glacier of the Ortler Spitze tumbled down upon the road, and was driven directly up to the barricade—double lines of sentries stretching on either side up the mountains till the flash of gun barrels was lost in the flash of the snow. For accidental education the picture had its value; the earliest of such pictures count for most. The handsome blond officers of the Jagers were not to be beaten in courtesy by the olive-toned Cacciatori. The eternal woman had her way, and the barricade offered none. In fifteen minutes the carriage was rolling down to Mals, swarming with German soldiers and German fleas, and German atmosphere, of which, thanks to the glimpse of Italy, Adams never again felt quite the old confident charm.

Faithful to his self-contracted scheme of two winters in Civil Law, he went back to Dresden with a letter to the Frau Hofrathin von Reichenbach, in whose house Lowell and other Americans had pursued studies. In those days The Initials was a new book, and its cobweb charm over Munich gave a reflected light to Dresden. Young Adams had nothing to do but take fencing-lessons, visit galleries, and go to the theatre; but his social failure in the line of The Initials was humiliating, and the Frau Hofrathin sometimes laughed hugely at his helplessness before her society. Even the doctrine of accidental education broke down. There were no accidents in Dresden. As soon as the winter was over, he closed and locked the German door with a long breath of relief and took the road to Italy. He had pursued his education, as it pleased him, for eighteen months; in spite of infinite variety of new impressions packed into his mind, he knew no more, for his practical purposes, than the day he graduated. He had made no step towards a profession; was as ignorant as a schoolboy of society; unfit for any career in Europe and unfitted for any career in America; and he had not natural intelligence enough to see what a mess he had made of his education.

By twisting life to follow accidental and devious paths one might perhaps find use for accidental and devious knowledge, but nothing had been further from his mind than to become a tourist; and a mere tourist, and nothing else, he had become in April, 1860, when he joined his sister in Florence. His father had been in the right. Supposing his father asked what equivalent he had brought back for the time and money put into his experiment! The only possible answer would be: "Sir, I am a tourist!"

Meanwhile his father, sent to Congress by his Quincy district, was in the confusion of nominating candidates for the Presidential election. He supported Mr. Seward. The Republican Party was an unknown force; the Democratic Party was torn to pieces. No one could see far. For the time, the young man was safe from interference, and went on his way to take whatever chance fragments of education God or the devil was pleased to give him, for he knew no longer the good from the bad.

Perhaps the most useful purpose he served was that of his pen, for he wrote long letters to his brother Charles, who printed them in the Boston Courier; and the exercise was good. The habit of expression leads to the search for something to express; something remains as a residuum of the commonplace itself. Young men as a rule saw little in Italy, and in after life, when Adams began to learn what some men could see, he shrank into corners of shame at the thought that he had betrayed his inferiority as though it were his pride; but it was still the nearest approach he had made to an intelligent act.

For the rest, Italy was mostly an emotion, and the emotion naturally centred in Rome. The American parent, curiously enough, while bitterly hostile to Paris, seemed disposed to accept Rome as legitimate education, though abused; but to young men seeking education in a serious spirit, taking for granted that everything had a cause and that nature tended to an end, Rome was the most violent vice in the world, and Rome before 1870 was seductive beyond resistance. The lights and shadows were still mediaeval; the shadows breathed and glowed, full of soft forms felt by lost senses. No sand-blast of science had skinned off the epidermis of history, thought, and feeling. The pictures were uncleaned, the churches unrestored, the ruins unexcavated. Mediaeval Rome was sorcery. Rome was the worst spot on earth to teach nineteenth-century youth what to do with a twentieth-century world. One's emotions in Rome were one's private affair; they must be hurtful, else they could not have been so intense; and they were surely immoral, for no one could honestly read in the ruins any certain lesson but the just judgments of an outraged God against all the doings of man. This moral made Rome a gospel of anarchy and vice; yet it was, by common consent, the only spot that the young—of either sex and every race—passionately, perversely, wickedly loved.

Boys never see a conclusion; only on the edge of the grave can man conclude anything; but the first impulse given to the boy is apt to lead him for the rest of his life into conclusions he never dreamed of reaching. One looked idly enough at the Forum or at St. Peter's, but never forgot the look, and it never ceased reacting. Rome was not a beetle to be dissected and dropped; not a bad French novel to be read in a railway train and thrown out of the window after other bad French novels, the morals of which could never approach the immorality of Roman history. Rome was actual; it was England; it was going to be America. Rome could not be fitted into an orderly, Bostonian, systematic scheme of evolution. No law of progress applied. Not even time-sequences—the last refuge of helpless historians—had value for it. The Forum no more led to the Vatican than the Vatican to the Forum. Rienzi, Garibaldi, Tiberius Gracchus, Aurelian might be mixed in any relation of time and never lead to a sequence. The great word Evolution had not yet, in 1860, made a new religion of history, but the old religion had preached the same doctrine for a thousand years without finding in the entire history of Rome anything but flat contradiction.

Murray's Handbook had the grace to quote from Gibbon's Autobiography the words that started the Decline and Fall: "in the close of the evening, as I sat musing in the Church of the Zoccolanti or Franciscan Friars, while they were singing Vespers in the Temple of Jupiter, on the ruins of the Capitol." More than once Adams sat at sunset on the steps of Santa Maria di Ara Coeli, wondering that not an inch had been gained by Gibbon—or all the historians since—towards explaining the Fall. The mystery remained unsolved; the charm remained intact. Two great experiments of Western civilization had left there the chief monuments of their failure, and nothing proved that the city might not still survive to express the failure of a third.

The young man had no idea what he was doing. The thought of posing for a Gibbon never entered his mind. He was a tourist, even to the depths of his sub-consciousness, and it was well, for even the greatest cannot sit with dignity among the ruins of the Capitol unless they have something quite original to say. Tacitus could do it; so could Michael Angelo; and so, at a pinch, could Gibbon; but, in sum, none of them could say very much more than the tourist, who went on repeating to himself the eternal question:—Why! Why!! Why!!!—as his neighbor, the blind beggar, might do. No one ever had answered the question to the satisfaction of any one else; yet every one who had either head or heart felt that sooner or later he must make up his mind what answer to accept. Substitute the word America for the word Rome, and the question became personal.

Perhaps Henry learned something in Rome, though he never knew it. Rome dwarfs teachers. The greatest men of the age scarcely bore the test of posing with Rome for a background. One morning, chatting in Hamilton Wilde's studio, a middle-aged Englishman came in, excited, and told of his shock at coming unexpectedly on the guillotine near the Circus Maximus, where some criminal had been put to death an hour or two before. Only by slow degrees did Adams grapple the conviction that the victim of the shock was Robert Browning; and, on the background of the Circus Maximus, the Christian martyrs flaming as torches, and the morning's murderer on the block, Browning seemed rather in place, a middle-aged gentlemanly English Pippa Passes; while afterwards, in the light of Belgravia dinner-tables, he never made part of his background except by effacement. Browning might have sat with Gibbon among the ruins, and few Romans would have smiled.

Yet Browning never revealed the poetic depths of Saint Francis; William Story could not touch the secret of Michael Angelo; Mommsen hardly said all that one felt by instinct in the lives of Cicero and Caesar. They taught what, as a rule, needed no teaching, the lessons of a rather cheap imagination and cheaper politics. Rome was a bewildering complex of ideas, experiments, ambitions, energies; without her, the Western world was pointless and fragmentary; she gave heart and unity to it all; yet Gibbon might have gone on for the whole century, and no one would have passed, capable of telling him what it meant. Perhaps it meant nothing.

So ended the happiest month of May life had yet offered, fading beyond the past somewhere into abstract time, grotesquely out of place with the Berlin scheme or a Boston future. Adams explained to himself that he was absorbing knowledge. He would have put it better had he said that knowledge was absorbing him. He was passive. In spite of swarming impressions he knew no more when he left Rome than when he entered it. As a marketable object, his value was less.

He went on to Naples, and there, in the hot June, heard rumors that Garibaldi and his thousand were about to attack Palermo. Calling on the American Minister, Chandler of Pennsylvania, he was kindly treated, not for his merit but for his name, and sent as bearer of despatches to Captain Palmer of the Iroquois. He went to Palermo in a government transport filled with fleas, commanded by a charming Prince Caracciolo, and told all about it to the Boston Courier; but of its bearing on education the Courier did not speak. He himself would have liked to know whether it had any bearing, and what was its value as a post-graduate course. Loosely, the tourist called it knowledge of men, but it was just the reverse; it was knowledge of one's ignorance of men.

Captain Palmer took him with the officers to make an evening call on Garibaldi, whom they found in the Senate House towards sunset, at supper with his picturesque and piratic staff, in the full noise and color of the Palermo revolution. Garibaldi left the table, and, sitting at the window, had a few words with Captain Palmer and young Adams. At that moment he was certainly the most serious of the doubtful energies in the world; even then society was dividing between banker and anarchist; one or the other Garibaldi must serve. Himself a typical anarchist, sure to overshadow Europe and alarm empires bigger than Naples, his success depended on his mind.

Adams had the chance to look this sphinx in the eyes, and for five minutes to watch him like a wild animal, at the moment of his greatest achievement and most splendid action. One saw a quiet-featured, quiet-voiced man in a red flannel shirt; absolutely impervious; sympathetic and simple, perhaps childlike, but betraying no thought. In his own eyes he might be a Napoleon or a Spartacus; in the hands of Cavour he might become a Condottiere; in the eyes of history he might be only the vigorous player in a game he did not understand. The student was none the wiser. This compound nature of patriot and pirate had illumined Italian history from the beginning, and was no more intelligible to itself than to a young American who had no experience in double natures. In the end, if the Autobiography tells truth, Garibaldi saw and said that he had not understood his own acts; that he had been an instrument; that he had served the purposes of the class he least wanted to help; yet in 1860 he thought himself the revolution, Napoleonic, anarchic, with unbounded ambition. What should a young Bostonian have made of a character like this, internally alive with childlike fancies, externally quiet, simple, almost innocent; uttering with conviction the commonplaces of popular politics; and never betraying a thought?

Precisely this class of mind was to be the toughest problem of Adams's practical life, but he could never make anything of it. The lesson of Garibaldi, as education, seemed to teach the extreme complexity of extreme simplicity; but one could have learned this from a glow-worm. One did not need the vivid recollection of the low-voiced, simple-mannered, seafaring captain of Genoese adventurers and Sicilian brigands, supping in the July heat and Sicilian dirt and revolutionary clamor among the barricaded streets of Palermo, merely to remember that simplicity is complex.

He came north to stumble over others, less picturesque but nearer. He squandered two or three months on Paris. From the first he had avoided Paris and wanted no French influence; he disapproved of France in the lump; the Empire and the Emperor particularly; most of all the French mind. To save himself the trouble of a long list of dislikes, he shut them figuratively out of his life. France was not serious, and he was not serious in going there.

He did this in good faith, obeying his teachers; but the curious result followed that, being in no way responsible for the French and sincerely disapproving them, he felt quite at liberty to enjoy to the full everything he disapproved. Several thousand Americans passed much of their time there on this understanding. They sought to take share in every function open to approach as they sought tickets to the opera, because they were not a part of it. Adams did like the rest. He tried to acquire a few idioms, without aspiring to master a subjunctive; succeeded better in acquiring a modest taste for Bordeaux and Burgundy and one or two sauces; for the Trois Freres Provencaux and Voisin's and Philippe's and the Cafe Anglais; for the Palais Royal Theatre, and the Varietes and the Gymnase; for the Brohans and Bressant, Rose Cheri and Gil Perez, and other lights of the stage. Life was amusing; Paris rapidly became familiar; in a month or six weeks he forgot even to disapprove; but he studied nothing, entered no society, and made no acquaintance. Accidental education went far in Paris, and one picked up a deal of knowledge that might become useful; perhaps, after all, the three months there might serve better purpose than the twenty-one months elsewhere; but he did not intend it, did not think it, and looked at it as a momentary and frivolous vacation before going home to fit himself for life. Therewith, after staying as long as he could and spending all the money he dared, he started with mixed emotions but no education, for home.

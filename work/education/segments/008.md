## VII. Treason (1860–1861)

When, forty years afterwards, Henry Adams looked back over his adventures in search of knowledge, he asked himself whether fortune had ever dealt its cards quite so wildly as when it led him to begin the study of law and to vote for Abraham Lincoln on the same day.

He dropped back on Quincy like a lump of lead; he rebounded like a football, tossed into space by an unknown energy which played with all his generation as a cat plays with mice. Not one man in America wanted the Civil War, or expected or intended it. A small minority wanted secession. The vast majority wanted to go on with their occupations in peace. Not one, however clever or learned, guessed what happened.

His past melted away. The prodigal was welcomed home, and his father quietly invited his son to act as private secretary during the winter in Washington—as though any young man who could afford to throw away two winters on the Civil Law could afford to read Blackstone for another without a master. The young man asked only a pretext for throwing all education to the east wind. He packed his trunk, which had not yet had time to be unpacked, and started for Washington with his family.

Ten years had passed since his last visit, but very little had changed. As in 1800 and in 1850, the same rude colony was camped in the same forest, with the same unfinished Greek temples for workrooms and sloughs for roads. The Government had an air of social instability and incompleteness that went far to support the right of secession in theory as in fact. The Union was a sentiment, but not much more, and in December 1860 the sentiment about the Capitol was chiefly hostile.

Of all the crowd swarming in Washington that winter, young Adams was surely among the most ignorant and helpless, but he saw plainly that the knowledge possessed by everyone about him was hardly greater than his own. The Southern secessionists were certainly unbalanced—fit for medical treatment, like other victims of hallucination, haunted by suspicion and violent morbid excitement—but this was not all. They were stupendously ignorant of the world. The cotton-planters were mentally one-sided, ill-balanced, and provincial to a degree rarely known, a close society on whom a stream of wealth and slaves had poured like oil on flame. They showed a young student his first object-lesson in the way excess of power worked when held by inadequate hands.

Thus, at one sweep, the whole field of instruction south of the Potomac was shut off. From the Confederate school one learned nothing but bad temper, bad manners, poker, and treason.

Perforce the student was thrown back on Northern precept. Republican houses were few in Washington, and Mr. and Mrs. Adams took a house on I Street looking over Pennsylvania Avenue, where the private secretary began to learn his social duties. Setting aside Charles Sumner, who stood quite alone and was the boy's oldest friend, all the New Englanders were sane and steady men, well-balanced and free from meanness—men who bore the stamp of Harvard College. Their strength was their poise, which almost amounted to a defect: they attracted as little as they repelled; even as machines, their motion seemed never accelerated.

The hope of education, till the arrival of the President-elect, narrowed itself to the possible influence of two men—Sumner and Seward.

Sumner was then fifty years old. Since his Brooks injuries his nervous system had never quite recovered its tone, and ten years of solitary existence as Senator had stiffened him further. No man, however strong, can serve ten years as schoolmaster, priest, or Senator, and remain fit for anything else. Dogmatic stations fix a certain stiffness of attitude forever, as though they mesmerized the subject. Yet Sumner at least had qualities that warranted dogmatism. He justly thought, as Webster had thought before him, that his great services and sacrifices, his oratorical power, his representative character at the head of the whole New England contingent, and his knowledge of the world made him the most important member of the Senate.

His rival was Senator Seward. The two men would have disliked each other by instinct had they lived in different planets; the virtues of one were the faults of his rival. That the public service must suffer was certain—but what were the sufferings of the public service compared with the risks run by a young mosquito, a private secretary, trying to buzz admiration in the ears of each, unaware that each would impatiently slap at him for belonging to the other?

Governor Seward was an old friend of the family, a professed disciple of John Quincy Adams, Senator since 1849. When Mr. Adams came to Congress in December 1859, Seward instantly renewed his attitude of family friend, became a daily intimate in the household, and lost no chance of forcing his fresh ally to the front.

A few days after their arrival in December 1860, the Governor came to dinner, alone, as one of the family, and the private secretary had the chance he wanted to watch him carefully. *A slouching, slender figure; a head like a wise macaw; a beaked nose; shaggy eyebrows; unorderly hair and clothes; hoarse voice; offhand manner; free talk, and perpetual cigar* offered a new type—of western New York—to fathom. At table he threw off restraint, or seemed to; he appeared as a free talker who loathed pomposity and enjoyed a joke; but how much was nature and how much was mask, he was himself too simple a nature to know. He had some means, unknown to other Senators, of producing the effect of unselfishness. From the first sight, Henry Adams loved the Governor, who, though sixty years old, had the youth of his sympathies.

The inevitable followed, as a child fresh from the nursery should have had the instinct to foresee. Seward, representing the new Administration in the Senate, took the lead in offering concessions to the border States, to keep Virginia in the Union; Mr. Adams took the lead in the House; and the party united on tactics that risked—or incurred the certainty of—dividing their own side. As Seward said at dinner, in his gruff way, after their speeches: "If there's no secession now, you and I are ruined." They won their game.

The private secretary had perhaps felt almost as much interest in the sudden appearance of another young secretary of his own age, John Hay, who lighted on Lafayette Square at the same moment. Friends are born, not made, and Henry never mistook a friend except when in power. From their first slight meeting in February and March 1861 he recognized Hay as a friend, and never lost sight of him at the future crossing of their paths.

He had tried to make himself useful—acting in secret as newspaper correspondent, cultivating acquaintance, even haunting ballrooms where the old-fashioned Southern tone was pleasant even in an atmosphere of conspiracy. The sum was next to nothing for education, because no one could teach; all were as ignorant as himself. He saw Lincoln but once, at the melancholy function called an Inaugural Ball, and looked anxiously for a sign of character. He saw a long, awkward figure; a plain, ploughed face; a mind absent in part, and in part evidently worried by white kid gloves; above all, a lack of apparent force. Any private secretary fit for his business would have thought, as Adams did, that no man living needed so much education as the new President—but that all the education he could get would not be enough.

Only one man in Adams's reach seemed supremely fitted to be adviser and friend. This was Senator Sumner; and there, in fact, the young man's education began—and ended.

He was thunderstruck to learn that Sumner privately denounced the course pursued by Mr. Adams and Seward, regarded Mr. Adams as betraying the principles of his life, and broke off relations with his family. Many a shock was Henry Adams to meet in the course of a long life passed chiefly near politics and politicians, but the profoundest lessons are not lessons of reason; they are sudden strains that permanently warp the mind. He felt a profound respect for Sumner himself; the shock opened a chasm in life that never closed, and as long as life lasted, he found himself invariably taking for granted—as he took for granted that arsenic poisoned—the rule that *a friend in power is a friend lost*.

Not one rebel defection—not even Robert E. Lee's—cost young Adams a personal pang. But Sumner's struck home.

This, then, was the result of the new attempt at education, down to March 4, 1861. Washington was a dismal school. The vultures descended in swarms, and tore the carrion of political patronage into fragments on the very steps of the White House. Not a man knew what his task was to be, or was fitted for it; everyone, without exception, was to learn his business at the cost of the public. Lincoln, Seward, Sumner, and the rest could give no help to the young man seeking education; they knew less than he; within six weeks they were all to be taught by the uprising of such as he, and their education was to cost a million lives and ten thousand million dollars before the country recovered its balance. Henry was a helpless victim who could only wait for he knew not what, to send him he knew not where.

With the close of the session he returned with his father and mother to Boston, and with childlike docility sat down at a desk in the law-office of Horace Gray to begin again: "My Lords and Gentlemen"—his attempt at education in treason having, like all the rest, disastrously failed.

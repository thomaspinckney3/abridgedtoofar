PETER CHARDON BROOKS, the other grandfather, died January 1, 1849, leaving what was called the largest estate in Boston--some two millions--to his seven children: the sons Edward, Peter Chardon, Gorham, and Sydney; the daughters Charlotte, married to Edward Everett; Ann, to Nathaniel Frothingham of the First Church; and Abigail Brown, born April 25, 1808, who, September 3, 1829, married Charles Francis Adams, hardly older than herself. Their eldest was Louisa Catherine, named for her Johnson grandmother; their second, John Quincy, for his President grandfather; the third bore his father's name; the fourth, being of less account, fell in a way to his mother, who named him Henry Brooks, after a favorite brother just lost. Others came after, but these were the children concerned with the arduous process of educating.

The Adams connection in Boston was singularly small; the Brookses singularly large, and as clerical as New England could make. Three brothers-in-law--Everett, Dr. Frothingham, and Mr. Adams--might have been sought for long in older societies. One might have sought as long for seven brothers-in-law more unlike. Boston stamped them, but the shades amounted to contrasts. Everett, ambitious and early defiant of bounds, had left his Unitarian pulpit for Congress, given support to J. Q. Adams, and so brought about the marriage of the President's son with his wife's sister. The wreck of parties under Jackson spoiled many careers, Everett's among them; yet he rose with the Whigs, went to England, and came home with a European halo and rank only second to Webster as orator and Boston's representative figure. Dr. Frothingham, of the same clerical school, was less clerical in manner. Neither had much in common with Mr. Adams--younger, biassed by his father and by the inherited feud between Quincy and State Street--but relations were friendly as far as a boy could see, and the innumerable cousins went to the First Church every winter Sunday and slept under their uncle's sermons without once asking what the sermons meant for them. For two hundred years the First Church had seen the same little boys asleep under the same conditions, dimly conscious of the same feuds. The feuds never ceased; the boys grew to inherit them. Those of 1812 had mostly disappeared by 1850; death cleared that score; the quarrels of John and of John Quincy Adams were no longer acutely personal; the game was accounted drawn; and Charles Francis Adams might then have taken his inherited rights of political leadership in succession to Webster and Everett. Between him and State Street the relation was more natural than between Everett and State Street; but he drew himself aloof and renewed the old war that had lasted since 1700. He could not help it. With J. Q. Adams's record fresh in memory, his son, his only representative, could make no terms with the slave-power; and the slave-power overshadowed all Boston interests. No doubt he had principles of his own as well as inherited; but even his children, who had none, could as little follow Webster or even Seward. They would have lost in consideration more than they would have gained in patronage. They were anti-slavery by birth, as their name was Adams and their home was Quincy. Had they hungered for State Street as Paradise, State Street never would have trusted them, or they it. It hardly needed Daniel Webster as archangel with flaming sword to order them away from the door.

Time and experience, which alter all perspectives, altered this among the rest, and taught the boy gentler judgment; but even at ten his face was fixed, his heart stone, against State Street; his education was warped beyond recovery toward Puritan politics. Between him and his patriot grandfather at the same age the conditions had changed little. The year 1848 was like 1776 enough to make a fair parallel, and as concerned bias of education the parallel became complete when, a few months after John Quincy Adams's death, a convention of anti-slavery delegates at Buffalo organized a new party and named as candidates Martin Van Buren for President and Charles Francis Adams for Vice-President.

For any American boy, a father's candidacy would have dwarfed every other excitement; but, apart from personal bias, the year 1848, for a boy's road, was decisive for twenty years. There was never a side-path. The stamp of 1848 was almost as indelible as the stamp of 1776, but earlier centuries had set a standard stamp which hurt no one because all bore it; while men whose lives were to fall between 1865 and 1900 had first of all to get rid of it and take the stamp that belonged to their time. This was their education. Outsiders, immigrants, adventurers found it easy; the old Puritan nature rebelled. It gave a forcible reason: it thought its thought higher and its moral standards better than those of its successors. So they were. It could not be convinced that moral standards had nothing to do with it, and that utilitarian morality was good enough for it as for the graceless. Nature had given to the boy Henry a character that, in any previous century, would have led him into the Church; he inherited dogma and a priori thought from the beginning of time; and he scarcely needed a violent reaction like anti-slavery politics to sweep him back into Puritanism with a violence like a religious war.

Thus far he had nothing to do with it; his education was inheritance. For the next five or six years, his father alone counted for much. If he were to worry through life's quicksands, he must trust chiefly to his father's pilotage; but for the father the channel lay clear, while for the son an unknown ocean lay beyond. The father's business was to get past the dangers of the slave-power, or to fix its bounds at least. The task done, he might be content to let his sons pay for the pilotage; and it mattered little to his success whether they paid with lives wasted on battle-fields, or in misdirected energies and lost opportunity. The generation that lived from 1840 to 1870 could do very well with old forms of education; that which had its work to do between 1870 and 1900 needed something quite new.

His father's character was therefore the larger part of his education, so far as any single person affected it; and for that reason, if for no other, the son was always a much interested critic of his father's mind and temper. Long after, the sons still discussed it with difference of view. To Henry, the quality that distinguished his father from all other figures in the family group was that, in his opinion, Charles Francis Adams possessed the only perfectly balanced mind that ever existed in the name. For a hundred years every newspaper scribbler had derided or abused the older Adamses for want of judgment. They abused Charles Francis for his judgment. Naturally they never assigned values; that was the children's affair; but the traits were real. Charles Francis Adams was singular for mental poise--absence of self-assertion or self-consciousness--the faculty of standing apart without seeming aware that he was alone--a balance that neither challenged nor avoided notice, nor admitted question of superiority or inferiority, of jealousy or personal motive, even under pressure. This unusual poise, ripened by age, became the more striking as his son learned to measure mental faculties which were in no way exceptional for depth or range. His memory was hardly above average; his mind was not bold like his grandfather's, or restless like his father's, or imaginative or oratorical--still less mathematical; but it worked with singular perfection, admirable self-restraint, and instinctive mastery of form. Within its range it was model.

The standards of Boston were high, much affected by the old clerical self-respect that gave the Unitarian clergy unusual social charm. Channing, Everett, Frothingham, Palfrey, Walker, Emerson--any of them would have commanded distinction anywhere; but the Adamses had little or no affinity with the pulpit, and still less with its eccentric offshoots like Theodore Parker, Brook Farm, or Concord's philosophy. Boston showed a literary group led by Ticknor, Prescott, Longfellow, Motley, Holmes; but Mr. Adams was not one of them; as a rule they were much too Websterian. Even in science Boston claimed eminence, especially in medicine; Mr. Adams cared little for science. He stood alone; he had no master--hardly even his father; no scholars--hardly even his sons.

Almost alone among his Boston contemporaries, he was not English in feeling. Perhaps a hundred years of acute hostility to England had helped to form the family trait; but in him it went further and became indifference to social distinction. Never, in forty years of intimacy, did his son notice a trace of snobbishness. A duke or duchess seemed indifferent; royalty a slight inconvenience. He had every possible reason to affect courtier's manner, even if he did not feel the sentiment; he never did. Never did his son see him flatter or vilify, or show a sign of envy or jealousy; never a shade of vanity or self-conceit; never a tone of arrogance; never a gesture of pride.

The same might perhaps have been said of John Quincy Adams, but his associates averred it was accompanied by mental restlessness and want of judgment. No one charged Charles Francis with this fault; they charged him with its opposite. They called him cold. No doubt such perfect poise--such intuitive self-adjustment--was not maintained without sacrifice of qualities which would have upset it. No doubt, too, that his restless-minded, introspective, self-conscious children, too ignorant of the world to suspect how rare and complete the model was before them, would have been more impressed by a coarser instrument. Average human nature is coarse, and its ideals must be average. The world never loved perfect poise. What the world does love is commonly absence of poise, for it must be amused. Napoleons and Andrew Jacksons amuse it; perfect balance does not. Had Mr. Adams's nature been cold, he would have followed Webster, Everett, Seward, Winthrop, in lines of party discipline and self-interest. Had it been less balanced, he would have gone with Garrison, Wendell Phillips, Edmund Quincy, and Parker, into secession. Between the two he found an intermediate path, distinctive and characteristic--he set up a party of his own.

This political party became a chief influence in the boy's education from 1848 to 1854, violently affecting character when most plastic. The group with whom Mr. Adams associated himself, whose social centre was Mount Vernon Street, numbered only three: Dr. John G. Palfrey, Richard H. Dana, and Charles Sumner. Dr. Palfrey, the oldest, though clerically bred, was often the most agreeable; his talk was lighter, his range wider; he had wit or humor and the give-and-take of dinner-table exchange. Born to be a man of the world, he forced himself to be clergyman, professor, statesman, while, like every other true Bostonian, he yearned for the ease of the Athenaeum in Pall Mall or the Combination Room at Trinity. Dana at first suggested the opposite; he affected to be still before the mast, a direct, rather bluff, vigorous seaman; only, as one knew him better, one found the man of excessive refinement trying with success to work like a day-laborer, deliberately hardening his skin to the burden, as though still carrying hides at Monterey. He succeeded, for mind and will were robust; but he might have said with Evarts, his lifelong friend: "I pride myself on my success in doing not the things I like to do, but the things I don't like to do." Dana's ideal was to be a great Englishman, with a seat on the front benches till he should be promoted to the woolsack; beyond all, with a social status that should set him above provincial annoyances; but he forced himself to take life as it came, suffocating longings with grim self-discipline by mere force of will. Of the four, Dana was most marked. Without dogmatism or self-assertion, he seemed always in full sight, a figure that filled its space. He talked well; his mind worked close to its subject, as a lawyer's should; but, disguise and silence it as he liked, it was aristocratic to the tenth generation.

In that respect, and that only, Sumner was like him; otherwise Sumner stood out of line. He adored English standards; his ambition led him to rival Edmund Burke. No young Bostonian had made so brilliant a start, rather in Everett's steps than Webster's. As orator he achieved a triumph by his oration against war; but Boston admired him chiefly for social success in England and on the Continent, success giving to every Bostonian a halo never acquired by domestic sanctity. Sumner, by interest and instinct, felt the value of his English connection, and cultivated it the more as, socially outcast in Boston by political passion, he had nothing but himself to think about. He was rarely without a pocketful of letters from duchesses or noblemen. Having sacrificed to principle his social position in America, he clung to his foreign attachments. The Free Soil Party fared ill in Beacon Street. Ticknor and the rest had to admit, unwillingly, that Free Soil leaders could not mingle with Webster's friends and followers. Sumner was ostracized; so were Palfrey, Dana, Russell, Adams, and the other avowed anti-slavery leaders; but for them it mattered less, for they had houses and families; Sumner had neither wife nor household, and, though the most socially ambitious and the hungriest for what used to be called polite society, he could enter hardly half-a-dozen houses in Boston. Longfellow stood by him at Cambridge; in Beacon Street he could take refuge with Mr. Lodge; yet few days passed when he did not spend some time in Mount Vernon Street. Even with that, his solitude was glacial, and reacted on character. His superiority was real and incontestable; he was the classical ornament of the party; their pride in him unbounded, their admiration outspoken.

The boy worshipped him, and, if he ever regarded any older man as a personal friend, it was Sumner. The relation in the household was closer than any of blood. None of the uncles approached such intimacy. Sumner was the boy's ideal of greatness, the highest product of nature and art. The only fault of such a model was its superiority which defied imitation. To the twelve-year-old, his father, Palfrey, Dana, were men, more or less like what he might become; Sumner was a different order--heroic.

As the boy grew ten or twelve, his father gave him a writing-table in an alcove of the Boston library, and there, winter after winter, he worked over Latin Grammar and listened to these gentlemen discussing anti-slavery politics. The discussions were always serious; the Free Soil Party took itself quite seriously; and they were habitual, for Mr. Adams edited a newspaper as their organ, and they came to discuss policy and expression. At the same time he edited the Works of John Adams, and made the boy read texts for proof-correction. After years, his father sometimes complained that, as reader of Novanglus and Massachusettensis, Henry showed little consciousness of punctuation; but the boy regarded this part of schooling chiefly as a warning, if he ever grew to write dull discussions, to try to be dull in some different way from his great-grandfather. Yet the discussions in the Boston Whig ran in much the same style as those of John Adams and his opponent, and appealed to much the same society and habit of mind. The boy got as little education fitting him for his own time from one as from the other, and no more from contact with the men themselves, all types of the past.

Down to 1850, and later, New England society was still directed by the professions. Lawyers, physicians, professors, merchants were classes, acting not as individuals but as clergymen, each profession a church. In politics the system required competent expression; it was the old Ciceronian idea of government by the best, that produced the New England statesmen. They chose men to represent them because they wished to be well represented; they chose the best they had. Boston chose Webster, and Webster took not pay but honorarium, cheques raised by Peter Harvey from the Appletons, Perkinses, Amorys, Searses, Brookses, Lawrences, and the rest, who begged him to represent them. Everett held rank in regular succession to Webster; Winthrop claimed succession to Everett; Sumner aspired to break succession, not system. The Adamses had seldom been part of this State succession; they preferred national service, won all their distinction outside, yet required State support and commonly received it. The little group in Mount Vernon Street was an offshoot of this system; they were statesmen, not politicians; they guided opinion, and were little guided by it.

The boy learned but one lesson from such saturation: he took for granted that this sort of world--more or less the same that had always existed in Boston--was the world he was to fit. Had he known Europe he would have learned no better. The Paris of Louis Philippe, Guizot, de Tocqueville, and the London of Peel, Macaulay, Mill, were varieties of the same upper-class bourgeoisie that felt instinctive cousinship with the Boston of Ticknor, Prescott, Motley. Even Carlyle, who cast doubts on the capacity of the middle class and fancied himself eccentric, found friendship in Boston and still more in Concord. The system had proved so successful that Germany wanted to try it and Italy yearned for it. England's middle-class government was the ideal.

Even the violent reaction after 1848, and Europe's return to military practices, never for a moment shook the faith. No one, except Karl Marx, foresaw radical change. What announced it? The world was producing sixty or seventy million tons of coal, and might be using nearly a million steam-horsepower, just beginning to make itself felt. All experience since the creation of man, all divine revelation or human science, conspired to deceive a twelve-year-old boy who took for granted that his ideas, alone respectable, would be alone respected.

Viewed from Mount Vernon Street, life was as simple as classic. Politics offered no difficulties, for there the moral law was a sure guide. Social perfection was sure, because human nature worked for Good, and three instruments were all she asked--Suffrage, Common Schools, and Press. On these points doubt was forbidden. Education was divine, and man needed only a correct knowledge of facts to reach perfection:

"Were half the power that fills the world with terror,
  Were half the wealth bestowed on camps and courts,
Given to redeem the human mind from error,
  There were no need of arsenals nor forts."

Nothing quieted doubt so completely as the mental calm of the Unitarian clergy. In uniform excellence of life and character, moral and intellectual, the score of Unitarian clergymen about Boston, who controlled society and Harvard College, were never excelled. They insisted on no doctrine, but tried to teach the means of leading a virtuous, useful, unselfish life, which they held sufficient for salvation. Difficulties might be ignored; doubts were waste; nothing exacted solution. Boston had solved the universe; or had offered and realized the best solution yet tried. The problem was worked out.

Of all the conditions of youth which afterwards puzzled the man, this disappearance of religion puzzled him most. The boy went to church twice every Sunday; was taught to read his Bible; learned religious poetry by heart; believed in a mild deism; prayed; went through the forms; but to none of them was religion real. Even the mild discipline of Unitarianism was so irksome that they all threw it off at the first moment and never again entered a church. That the most powerful emotion of man, next to the sexual, should disappear, might be a personal defect; but that the most intelligent society, led by the most intelligent clergy, in the most moral conditions he ever knew, should have solved all problems so thoroughly as to cease anxiety about past or future, and should have persuaded itself that the problems which had convulsed human thought from earliest time were not worth discussing, seemed to him the most curious social phenomenon he had to account for. The faculty of turning away one's eyes as one approaches a chasm is not unusual, and Boston showed, under Webster, how well it could be done in politics; but there some men protested. In religion and philosophy no one protested. Such protest as was made took forms simpler than silence; the deism of Theodore Parker and of his own cousin Octavius Frothingham distressed his father and scandalized Beacon Street by avowing scepticism that solved no old problems and raised many new. The less aggressive protest of Emerson, from an old-world point of view, was less serious. It was naïf.

The children reached manhood without knowing religion, and with certainty that dogma, metaphysics, and abstract philosophy were not worth knowing. So one-sided an education could have existed in no other country or time, but it became almost of necessity more literary and political. As the children grew, they exaggerated those interests. They joined in dinner-table discussions, and from childhood the boys were used to table-talk as good as they were ever likely to hear again. Louisa, the eldest, was one of the most sparkling creatures her brother met in a long experience of bright women. John, the eldest son, was afterwards regarded as one of the best talkers in Boston, perhaps the most popular man in the State, though apt to be on the unpopular side. Palfrey and Dana could be entertaining when they pleased; Sumner could hardly be called light in hand, but was willing to be amused, and smiled grandly from time to time; while Mr. Adams, who talked little, was always a good listener, and laughed over a witticism till he choked.

By way of educating and amusing, Mr. Adams read much aloud, sure to choose political literature, especially satirical, like Horace Mann's speeches and the Hosea Biglow epistles, with delight to the youth. So he read Longfellow and Tennyson as their poems appeared; but the children took Dickens and Thackeray for themselves. Both were too modern for tastes founded on Pope and Johnson. The boy soon became a desultory reader of every book he found readable, but these were commonly eighteenth-century historians, his father's library being full of them. In want of positive instincts, he drifted into the mental indolence of history. So, too, he read shelves of eighteenth-century poetry; but when his father offered his own Wordsworth as a gift on condition of reading it through, he declined. Pope and Gray called for no effort; they were easy; but the boy was thirty before his education reached Wordsworth.

This is the story of an education, and the persons figure in it only as educators or educated. The surroundings concern it only as they affect it. Sumner, Dana, Palfrey had values of their own, like Hume, Pope, Wordsworth, which any one may study; here they appear only as influences on the mind of a boy nearly the average in body and mind. The influence was wholly political and literary. His father forced no mind, but left free play, which was perhaps best. Only in one way he did a great service--trying to teach French and giving some idea of accent. Otherwise the family was rather atmosphere than influence. The boy had a large, overpowering set of brothers and sisters, modes or replicas of the same type, getting the same education, struggling with the same problems, solving or leaving them unsolved in much the same way. They knew no more than he what they wanted or what to do for it, but were conscious that they would like to control power in some form; the same could be said of an ant or an elephant. Their form was tied to politics or literature. They amounted to one individual with half-a-dozen facets; their temperaments reacted and made each more like the other. This also was education--in type; and the Boston type was well enough known. What no one knew was whether the individual who thought himself representative of this type was fit to deal with life.

As far as outward bearing went, such a family of turbulent children, given free rein by parents, or indifferent to check, should have come to grief. Certainly no one was strong enough to control them, least of all their mother, queen-bee of the hive, on whom nine-tenths of the burden fell, on whose strength all depended, but whose children were too self-willed and self-confident to take guidance from her or from any one, unless in the direction they fancied. Father and mother were about equally helpless. Almost every large family then produced at least one black sheep; if this generation escaped, it was as much a surprise to themselves as to neighbors. By some happy chance they grew up decent citizens, but Henry Adams, as a brand from the burning, always looked back with astonishment at their luck. The fact seemed to prove they were born, like birds, with an innate balance. Home influences alone never saved the New England boy from ruin, though sometimes they helped to ruin him; influences outside were negative. If school helped, it was by reaction. The dislike of school was so strong as to be a positive gain. The passionate hatred of school methods was almost a method in itself. Yet the day-school of that time was respectable, and the boy had nothing to complain of. In fact, he never complained. He hated it because he was there with a crowd of other boys and compelled to learn by memory a quantity of things that did not amuse him. His memory was slow, the effort painful. For him to conceive that his memory could compete for prizes with machines of two or three times its power, was to prove himself wanting not only in memory but flagrantly in mind. He thought his mind a good machine if given time to act, but it acted wrong if hurried. Schoolmasters never gave time.

In any and all forms the boy detested school; the prejudice deepened with years. He always reckoned his school-days, from ten to sixteen, as time thrown away. Perhaps his needs turned out exceptional, but his existence was exceptional. Between 1850 and 1900 nearly every one's existence was exceptional. For success in the life imposed on him he needed, as afterwards appeared, the facile use of only four tools: Mathematics, French, German, and Spanish. With these he could master in short time any special branch, and feel at home in any society. Latin and Greek he could, with help of the modern languages, learn more completely by the intelligent work of six weeks than in the six years spent at school. These four tools were necessary to his success, but he never controlled any one of them.

Thus, at the outset, he was condemned to failure more or less complete in the life awaiting him, though not more so than his companions. Indeed, had his father kept him at home and given him half an hour's direction every day, he would have done more than school ever could do. School-taught men and boys looked down on home-bred boys and rather prided themselves on their ignorance; but the man of sixty can see what he needed in life, and in Henry Adams's opinion it was not school.

Most school experience was bad. Boy associations at fifteen were worse than none. Boston then offered few healthy resources for boys or men. The bar-room and billiard-room were more familiar than parents knew. As a rule boys could skate and swim and were sent to dancing-school; they played a rudimentary baseball, football, hockey; a few could sail; still fewer had been out with a gun to shoot yellow-legs or a stray wild duck; one or two learned something of natural history if from Concord; none could ride across country or knew what shooting with dogs meant. Sport as a pursuit was unknown. Boat-racing came after 1850. For horse-racing, only the trotting-course existed. Of pleasures, winter sleighing was the gayest and most popular. From none of these amusements could the boy learn anything likely to be of use in the world. Books remained, as in the eighteenth century, the source of life, and as they came out--Thackeray, Dickens, Bulwer, Tennyson, Macaulay, Carlyle, and the rest--they were devoured; but, as far as happiness went, the happiest hours of the boy's education were passed in summer, lying on a musty heap of Congressional Documents in the old farmhouse at Quincy, reading Quentin Durward, Ivanhoe, and The Talisman, and raiding the garden at intervals for peaches and pears. On the whole, he learned most then.

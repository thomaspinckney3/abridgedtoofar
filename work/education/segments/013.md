# CHAPTER VIII

DIPLOMACY (1861)

Hardly a week passed when the newspapers announced that President Lincoln had selected Charles Francis Adams as his Minister to England. Once more, silently, Henry put Blackstone back on its shelf. As Friar Bacon's head sententiously announced many centuries before: Time had passed! The law, as path of education, vanished in April, 1861, leaving a million young men planted in the mud of a lawless world, to begin a new life without education at all.

Mr. Adams once more intimated that he thought himself entitled to the services of one of his sons, and indicated Henry as the only one who could be spared. Henry packed his trunk again without a word; he could offer no protest. Ridiculous as he knew himself about to be in his new rôle, he was less ridiculous than his betters. He was at least no public official, like the thousands of improvised secretaries and generals who crowded their jealousies and intrigues on the President. He was not a vulture of carrion — patronage. He knew that his father's appointment was the result of Governor Seward's personal friendship; he did not then know that Senator Sumner had opposed it. That Mr. Adams was unfit might well be, since it was hard to find a fit appointment except Sumner himself; the weakest of Mr. Adams's proofs of fitness was his consent to quit a safe seat in Congress for an exceedingly unsafe seat in London.

The Secretary of Legation was the editor of a Chicago newspaper who had applied for the Chicago Post-Office, with no thought of staying. The Assistant Secretary was inherited from Buchanan's time, a hard worker, but socially useless. Mr. Adams made no effort to find efficient help.

The private secretary was no worse off than his neighbors except in being called earlier into service. On April 13 the storm burst and rolled several hundred thousand young men like Henry Adams into the surf of a wild ocean, all helpless like himself, to be beaten about for four years by the waves of war. He had time to watch the regiments form ranks before Boston State House in the April evenings, and to go down the harbor to see his brother Charles quartered in Fort Independence before being thrown into the furnace of the Army of the Potomac. Few things were for the moment so trivial in importance as the solitary private secretary crawling down to the wretched old Cunard steamer Niagara at East Boston to start again for Liverpool. This time the pitcher of education had gone to the fountain once too often; it was fairly broken; and the young man had got to meet a hostile world without defence — or arms.

Minister Adams sailed for England, May 1, 1861, with much the same outfit as Admiral Dupont would have enjoyed if the Government had sent him to attack Port Royal with one cabin-boy in a rowboat. The private secretary would have been cabin-boy still, with only his father for master — who never fretted, never dictated, never disciplined, and whose idea of American diplomacy was that of the eighteenth century. Minister Adams remembered how his grandfather had sailed from Mount Wollaston in midwinter, 1778, taking his eleven-year-old son John Quincy with him, for secretary, on a diplomacy of adventure that had hardly a parallel for success. He remembered how John Quincy, in 1809, had sailed for Russia, with himself, a baby of two years old, to cope with Napoleon and the Czar single-handed. He thought it natural that the Government should send him out as an adventurer also, with a twenty-three-year-old son, and he did not even notice that he left not a friend behind him. He thought it right to play the adventurer as his father and grandfather had done before him, without a murmur.

This was a lofty view, and for him answered his objects, but it bore hard on cabin-boys, and when the young man realized what had happened, he felt it as a betrayal. For the first time America was posing as the champion of legitimacy and order; her representatives should know how to play their rôle; but the only rag of legitimacy or order in the mission was the private secretary, whose stature was not sufficient to impose awe on the Court and Parliament of Great Britain.

By way of teaching him quickly, they sent out their new Minister to Russia in the same ship: Cassius M. Clay, a teacher having no equal though possibly some rivals. No young man, not in Government pay, could draw from such lessons any confidence in himself.

Above all, the private secretary could not grumble to his chief. He never labored so hard to learn a language as he did to hold his tongue, and it affected him for life. The habit of reticence — of talking without meaning — is never effaced. He was already an adept when the party landed at Liverpool, May 13, 1861, and went instantly up to London: a family of early Christian martyrs about to be flung into an arena of lions, under the glad eyes of Tiberius Palmerston.

Then came the Trent Affair: the seizure of Mason and Slidell brought the Legation to the brink of war, and the Minister, summoned from Yorkshire, met the crisis without losing his nerve. The shock passed; the secretaries learned that patience, silence, and the closing of newspaper indiscretions were the only diplomacy left to them.

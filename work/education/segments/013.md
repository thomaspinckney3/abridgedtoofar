## XII. Eccentricity (1863)

Knowledge of human nature is the beginning and end of political education, but several years of arduous study in the neighborhood of Westminster led Henry Adams to think that knowledge of English human nature had little or no value outside of England. In Paris such a habit stood in one's way; in America it roused all the instincts of native jealousy. The English mind was one-sided, eccentric, systematically unsystematic, and logically illogical. The less one knew of it, the better.

From the old-world point of view, the American had no mind at all—only an economic thinking-machine that worked on a fixed line. The American mind exasperated the European as a buzz-saw might exasperate a pine forest. The English mind at least recognized the French mind as a thought; the American mind was not a thought at all, merely a cutting instrument, practical, economical, sharp, and direct.

The defect that most struck an American observer in the English was its enormous waste in eccentricity. Americans used their whole energy with close economy; English society was eccentric by law and for eccentricity's own sake. The commonest phrase overheard at an English dinner-table was that So-and-So "is quite mad"—no offense to So-and-So, who was hardly distinguished from his fellows by it. Mr. Gladstone himself went to see Sothern act Dundreary and laughed till his face was distorted—not because Dundreary was exaggerated, but because he was ridiculously like the types Gladstone had seen in any club in Pall Mall. Society swarmed with exaggerated characters; it contained little else.

By natural affinity the social eccentrics commonly sympathized with political eccentricity. The English mind took naturally to rebellion—when foreign—and it felt particular confidence in the Southern Confederacy because of its combined attributes: foreign rebellion of English blood, which came nearer ideal eccentricity than Poles or Hungarians could manage. All the English eccentrics rushed into the ranks of rebel sympathizers, leaving few but well-balanced minds to attach themselves to the Union. The "cranks" were all rebels, and the list was portentous. The Church was rebel; the universities were rebel. Meanwhile the friends of the Union—Forster, Cobden, Bright—were marked by their practicality, their consistency, their refusal to box the compass. John Bright was "furiously earnest," as Monckton Milnes said, but he stuck to his point, and his point was practical.

As for the rebel managers themselves, they adopted as their parliamentary champion Roebuck—an eccentric of eccentrics, a tribune of the people grown fatuous, with a bitter tongue and a mind enfeebled by the political epidemic of egotism. The private secretary had no direct concern with Roebuck's doings except that the Minister sent him down to the House of Commons on June 30, 1863, to report the result of Roebuck's motion to recognize the Southern Confederacy. The Legation felt no anxiety, having Vicksburg already in its pocket; and the private secretary went down and listened with great content while John Bright, with astonishing force, caught and shook and tossed Roebuck as a big mastiff shakes a wiry, ill-conditioned, bad-tempered Yorkshire terrier.

The scene had a retrospective pendant. Near the year 1879, in Washington, Adams heard Lamar—then Senator from Mississippi, one of the calmest and most amiable Union men in the United States—tell the story of his earlier, brief career as Confederate agent. Lamar had been detained in Paris when the Roebuck motion was pending, and was brought down to Lindsay's villa on the Thames, where the rebel agents were assembling. Finding himself alone with Roebuck, Lamar asked whether he expected Bright to speak. "No, sir!" said Roebuck sententiously. "Bright and I have met before. It was the old story—the story of the sword-fish and the whale! NO, sir! Mr. Bright will not cross swords with me again!"

So assured, Lamar went with confidence to the House on the appointed evening, and followed the debate with an experienced debater's pleasure—until, as he said, he became aware that a man with a singularly rich voice and imposing manner had taken the floor and was giving Roebuck the most deliberate and tremendous pounding he had ever witnessed, "until at last it dawned on my mind that the sword-fish was getting the worst of it."

Bright's courage was almost as irrational as that of the rebels themselves. Everyone knew he had the courage of a prize-fighter. Even among friends at the dinner-table he talked as though denouncing them on a platform; he measured his phrases, built his sentences, cumulated his effects, and pounded his opponents, real or imagined. His humor was glow, like iron at dull heat; his blow was elementary, like the thrash of a whale. At the Trades-Union meeting at St. James's Hall in March 1863—where Professor Beesly had labored to unite Bright and the unions on an American platform—Bright managed at the outset, in his opening paragraph, to insult every class of Englishman commonly considered respectable, and, for fear of any escaping, insulted them repeatedly under consecutive heads:

"Privilege thinks it has a great interest in the American contest, and every morning, with blatant voice, it comes into our streets and curses the American Republic. Privilege has beheld an afflicting spectacle for many years past. It has beheld thirty million of men happy and prosperous, without emperors—without king—without the surroundings of a court—without nobles, except such as are made by eminence in intellect and virtue—without State bishops and State priests, those vendors of the love that works salvation—without great armies and great navies—without a great debt and great taxes—and Privilege has shuddered at what might happen to old Europe if this great experiment should succeed."

Years later, in 1880, Adams was in London again when Lowell came as Minister, and he brought Bright to dinner. Bright was then in the Cabinet, but he still did most of the talking—and talked, as usual, of what was most on his mind. Over the wine, he took possession of the table and ended with a superb denunciation of the Bench: "For two hundred years, the Judges of England sat on the Bench, condemning to the penalty of death every man, woman, and child who stole property to the value of five shillings; and, during all that time, not one Judge ever remonstrated against the law. We English are a nation of brutes, and ought to be exterminated to the last man." As the party passed into the drawing-room, Adams said to Lowell that Bright was very fine. "Yes!" replied Lowell, "but too violent!" Bright knew his Englishmen better. He knew what amount of violence in language was necessary to drive an idea into a Lancashire head—and knew, too, that no violence was enough for Wiltshire.

The sum of these experiences in 1863 left the conviction that eccentricity was weakness. The young American who should adopt English thought was lost. The years of Palmerston's last Cabinet—1859 to 1865—were years of truce, of arrested development. The British system was in its last stage of decomposition; eccentricities had a free field. A young American in 1863 could see little or nothing of the future—could not foretell the suddenness with which the old Europe, England in its wake, was to vanish in 1870. He was in dead-water, and the parti-colored, fantastic cranks swam about his boat, as though he were the Ancient Mariner.

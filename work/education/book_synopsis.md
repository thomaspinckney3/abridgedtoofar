Henry Adams begins by setting his own education against that of his forebears, born in 1838 under the shadow of Boston’s Puritan past and between the twin poles of Beacon Hill and the rocky fields of Quincy. Childhood becomes a study in opposites—winter and summer, town and country, law and liberty—that imprint a “double nature” on a boy already heir to the Adams name and the stern moral energy it carried. He recoils from schools and prizes, preferring the smell of orchards and the moral perfumes of his grandfather John Quincy Adams’s library, yet is profoundly shaped by the rituals, arguments, and ironies of Boston’s politics and pulpit. As the national struggle over slavery quickens—1848, Free Soil maneuvers, Sumner’s rise—he learns that public virtue and personal loyalty seldom meet neatly, and that education in America is as much a matter of party, press, and social friction as of books. Harvard gives him more stamp than substance, and Germany—its law-lectures dead, its air pasty with metaphysics—teaches him more by negation than instruction. What does shape him is the shock of travel and contagion of events: Italian sunlight at Garibaldi’s heels, London’s gray respectability, and the revelation that the modern mind must take its measure from force as much as from form.

The Civil War throws him into Washington’s ad hoc stage, a private secretary to his father before the son follows the elder to London as minister’s attaché, where diplomacy becomes his university. There he watches the Trent Affair de-ice British fastidiousness; he learns the practical grammar of power when Earl Russell’s polite evasions and the Lairds’ ironclad rams put England’s scruples on the scales against America’s survival. His lessons accumulate through the confusing splendor of Palmerston’s drawing rooms, the paradoxes and violences of Gladstone’s eloquence, and the dry, implacable habits of the British cabinet. The Alabama escapes; the rams nearly do; but patience, documentation, and quiet pressure—backed by war’s proof—eventually detain them, teaching Adams that force slow-walks behind pretense and that diplomacy is a game of friction. Through these years he loses illusions about men while gaining a mature appetite for relation and measure: society is a mechanism, and the mind must find a scale on which to weigh it. In 1868 he returns home, wondering whether his education—partly absorbed from Sumner and the London fog, partly from incidents more violent than reason—has taught him to read the drift of a nation that has changed continents under his feet.

The postwar Republic makes him a writer and sometimes a professor, but in both roles he calls himself a failure; his reviews and histories bite at the present with facts filleted from the past, while Harvard’s lecture rooms turn quick minds into catalogues. He secures intimacy with men and women of brilliance—Clarence King, John Hay, Edith and Henry Cabot Lodge—and sits inside the operating theater of Gilded Age politics and press, but rarely mistakes access for mastery. History seems to him a contest of forces more than of principles, and the century’s exploding engines confirm it; he studies the Chicago and Paris Expositions as if they were dynamos with the covers off. The revelation of 1900 in Paris—the dynamo’s silent majesty across from the lost unity of the twelfth century—crystallizes his great metaphor: compare the Virgin of Chartres, who drew an age into a self-coherent unity of feeling, with the whirring dynamo that condenses new infinities of power without a moral map. Art and architecture, once the world’s grammar, now read like dialects; he turns from cathedrals’ glass to steel’s glow and from Thomas Aquinas’s synthesis to Karl Pearson’s skepticism, finding that order has dissolved into multiplicity. If the Middle Ages made a universe of “One,” the modern makes it many, and education—his and ours—must learn to think in contradictions.

These contradictions gather as friends die and power modernizes its cruelty. The Spanish War and McKinley’s assassination pass swiftly from spectacle to structure; meanwhile Hay’s “open door” diplomacy and the sudden tilt of Germany toward the Atlantic balance hint—briefly—at a modern pax. Yet Russia’s inertia, the Boer War’s ironies, and the Boxer crisis’s theater show that new forces overwhelm old ethics; bombs educate more swiftly than sermons, and the Senate calls force by another name. In this press of events, Adams looks for a law of motion: if man is a force among forces, his mind is a function of acceleration; modern life doubles its power like coal tons and intensities like heat. He comes to a dynamic theory of history: as new energies—steam, electricity, radium—enter the system, the mind is reoriented, the old unities crack, and education, if honest, must prepare for shock rather than repose. Chartres and the Virgin still model a unity of appeal, but the dynamo and its derivatives command habit; the modern student must triangulate from both, or be sliced by the machine.

The book closes in an Indian summer of clarity and fatigue: Adams, an elderly “pedometer” of forces, confesses that his dynamic law may be no more than an instrument, and that the next generation will test it by living through the next doubling of speed. He proposes no program: the Senate cannot legislate unity, nor can a press arouse ethics in an engine. His ultimate “education” is to stand on the steps of Ara Coeli, as in youth, and acknowledge that history is a sequence of accelerations whose unity may exist only in our need to find one. The deaths of Clarence King and John Hay, the last of his trio, cue the curtain’s fall; with them goes the last of a certain American candor. Adams accepts that he has learned chiefly how ignorant he is, but he bequeaths a working image: between the Virgin and the Dynamo lies the path of the modern mind, and the only preparation worth having is the agility to move between them as the forces that summon us grow stronger and less humane.

Chapter I — Our Society

In the first place, Cranford is in possession of the Amazons; all the holders of houses above a certain rent are women. If a married couple come to settle, somehow the gentleman disappears—frightened to death by being the only man at our evening parties, or spirited away by regiment, ship, or business all the week in Drumble, the great neighbouring commercial town, twenty miles off by railroad. In short, whatever does become of the gentlemen, they are not at Cranford. What could they do if they were there? The surgeon rides his thirty miles and sleeps in Cranford; but every man cannot be a surgeon. For keeping trim gardens, frightening away boys from the railings, rushing out at geese that venture in at an open gate; for deciding all questions of literature and politics without troubling themselves with unnecessary reasons; for obtaining correct knowledge of everybody’s affairs; for keeping neat maid-servants in admirable order; for kindness (somewhat dictatorial) to the poor, and real tender good offices to each other in distress—the ladies of Cranford are quite sufficient. “A man,” as one of them observed to me once, “is so in the way in the house!” Though each knows all the others’ proceedings, they are exceedingly indifferent to one another’s opinions; individuality, not to say eccentricity, is strongly developed, so that verbal retaliation is easy; yet good-will reigns to a considerable degree.

Their dress is independent of fashion. “What does it signify how we dress here at Cranford, where everybody knows us?” If we go from home: “What does it signify how we dress here, where nobody knows us?” The materials are good and plain; I will answer for it the last gigot sleeve and the last tight scanty petticoat in England were seen in Cranford—and seen without a smile. I can testify to a magnificent family red silk umbrella, under which a gentle little spinster, left alone of many brothers and sisters, used to patter to church on rainy days. We had a tradition of the first red silk umbrella ever seen in Cranford; the little boys mobbed it, and called it “a stick in petticoats.” It might have been that very one I describe; the poor little lady—the survivor of all—could scarcely carry it.

Rules for visiting were as solemnly announced to young people as the old Manx laws on the Tinwald Mount. “Our friends have sent to inquire how you are after your journey to-night, my dear; they will give you some rest to-morrow, but the next day, I have no doubt, they will call; so be at liberty after twelve—from twelve to three are our calling hours.” And after they had called: “It is the third day; never let more than three days elapse between receiving a call and returning it; and never stay longer than a quarter of an hour.” “But am I to look at my watch?” “You must keep thinking about the time, my dear, and not allow yourself to forget it in conversation.” Accordingly no absorbing subject was ever spoken about; we kept to short sentences of small talk, and were punctual.

A few gentlefolks were poor, I imagine, yet like Spartans concealed the smart under a smiling face. We none of us spoke of money, because that savoured of commerce; though some might be poor, we were all aristocratic. The kindly esprit de corps made us overlook failures in success when poverty tried to hide itself. When Mrs Forrester gave a party in her baby-house of a dwelling, and the little maiden disturbed the ladies on the sofa to get the tea-tray from underneath, every one took this novel proceeding as the most natural thing in the world, and talked on about household forms as if our hostess had a housekeeper and steward, instead of the one little charity-school maiden, whose short ruddy arms could never have carried the tray upstairs if she had not been assisted in private by her mistress, who now sat in state, pretending not to know what cakes were sent up—though she knew, and we knew, and she knew that we knew, and we knew that she knew that we knew, she had been busy all the morning with tea-bread and sponge-cakes.

From this unacknowledged poverty, and our very much acknowledged gentility, arose certain improvements. We kept early hours, and clattered home in our pattens, under the guidance of a lantern-bearer, about nine; and the whole town was abed by half-past ten. It was considered “vulgar” (a tremendous word in Cranford) to give anything expensive at evening entertainments. Wafer bread-and-butter and sponge-biscuits were all that the Honourable Mrs Jamieson gave; and she was sister-in-law to the late Earl of Glenmire, although she practised such “elegant economy.”

“Elegant economy!” How naturally one falls back into Cranford phraseology! Economy was always “elegant,” and money-spending always “vulgar and ostentatious”; a sort of sour-grapeism which made us peaceful and satisfied. I never shall forget the dismay when a certain Captain Brown came to live at Cranford and openly spoke, in the public street! in a loud military voice! of his being poor, alleging it as a reason for not taking a particular house. The ladies were already moaning over the invasion by a man and a gentleman. He was half-pay, and had some situation on a neighbouring railroad (vehemently petitioned against by the town); and if, in addition, he talked of being poor—he must be sent to Coventry. Death was as common as poverty; yet people never spoke of that loud out in the streets. We had tacitly agreed to ignore that any among us could be prevented by poverty from doing as we wished. If we walked to a party, it was because the night was so fine; if we wore prints instead of silks, it was because we preferred a washing material; till we blinded ourselves to the vulgar fact that we were people of very moderate means. Of course, then, we did not know what to make of a man who could speak of poverty as if it was not a disgrace. Yet, somehow, Captain Brown made himself respected, and was called upon in spite of resolutions. I was surprised to hear his opinions quoted as authority a year after he had settled. He came even in the tabooed hours before twelve—to discover the cause of a smoking chimney; walked upstairs, nothing daunted; spoke in a voice too large for the room; and joked quite in the way of a tame man about the house. Blind to small slights and omissions, friendly where we were cool, answering small sarcastic compliments in good faith, his manly frankness overpowered all our shrinking from a man not ashamed to be poor. At last his excellent masculine common sense, and facility in devising expedients for domestic dilemmas, gained him an extraordinary place as authority. He went on, unaware that he had become popular, as he had been of the reverse; and was startled one day to find advice he had given in jest taken in sober earnest.

An old lady, Miss Betsy Barker, had an Alderney cow, regarded as a daughter. In an unguarded moment the poor beast tumbled into a lime-pit; rescued, she had lost most of her hair, and came out naked, cold, and miserable. Miss Barker cried, and was said to think of a bath of oil; but Captain Brown’s decided counsel was: “Get her a flannel waistcoat and flannel drawers, ma’am, if you wish to keep her alive. But my advice is, kill the poor creature at once.” Miss Betsy dried her eyes, thanked the Captain, and by-and-by all the town turned out to see the Alderney meekly going to her pasture, clad in dark grey flannel. I have watched her myself many a time. Do you ever see cows dressed in grey flannel in London?

Captain Brown had taken a small house on the outskirts, where he lived with his two daughters. He must have been upwards of sixty, with a wiry, elastic figure, a stiff military throw-back of the head, and a springing step. His eldest, Miss Brown, looked almost as old as himself—sickly, pained, careworn, plain, with the gaiety of youth long faded. Miss Jessie Brown was ten years younger, and twenty shades prettier: round and dimpled, large blue wondering eyes, an unformed snub nose, red dewy lips, and little rows of curls that heightened a childlike look. Miss Jenkyns once said, in a passion against Captain Brown (the cause of which I will tell you presently), “she thought it was time for Miss Jessie to leave off her dimples, and not always to be trying to look like a child.” She had something of her father’s jauntiness; and a female observer might detect that her attire was about two pounds a year more expensive than her sister’s. Two pounds was a large sum in Captain Brown’s annual disbursements.

In church, he held his double eye-glass to his eyes during the Morning Hymn, then lifted up his head and sang out loud and joyfully; he made the responses louder than the clerk, who quivered higher and higher in consequence. Coming out, he paid the most gallant attention to his daughters; he nodded to acquaintances, but shook hands with none till he had helped Miss Brown to unfurl her umbrella, relieved her of her prayer-book, and waited till she had taken up her gown to walk through the wet roads.

We had often rejoiced that there was no gentleman to be attended to at our card-parties, and had almost persuaded ourselves that to be a man was to be “vulgar”; so when my friend and hostess, Miss Jenkyns, made a party, and Captain and the Miss Browns were invited, I wondered how the evening would go. Card-tables with green baize were set out; candles and clean packs laid; the fire made up; we stood in our best, each with a candle-lighter, ready to dart at the wicks. Parties were solemn festivities, making us feel gravely elated. As soon as three had arrived, we sat down to Preference, I being the unlucky fourth; the next four were put to another table; presently the tea-trays were placed on each. The china was delicate egg-shell; the old silver glittered; the eatables were of the slightest description. When Captain and the Miss Browns came in, I could see he was a favourite. Ruffled brows were smoothed, sharp voices lowered. Miss Brown looked ill, depressed to gloom; Miss Jessie smiled as usual, and was nearly as popular as her father. He quietly assumed the man’s place; attended to every one’s wants; lessened the pretty maid-servant’s labour; played for threepenny points with grave interest; and all the while had an eye on his suffering daughter. Miss Jessie could not play; she talked to the sitters-out, and sang “Jock of Hazeldean” a little out of tune to an old cracked piano; we were none of us musical, though Miss Jenkyns beat time, out of time, by way of appearing to be so. It was good of her; for a little before she had been annoyed by Miss Jessie’s unguarded admission (à propos of Shetland wool) that she had an uncle, her mother’s brother, a shopkeeper in Edinburgh. Miss Jenkyns tried to drown this confession by a terrible cough—for the Honourable Mrs Jamieson sat nearest—and what would she say to a shopkeeper’s niece! But Miss Jessie (who had no tact, as we all agreed next morning) would repeat it, and assure Miss Pole she could get the identical wool “through my uncle, who has the best assortment of Shetland goods of any one in Edinbro’.” To take the taste of this out of our mouths, Miss Jenkyns proposed music; hence the beating of time.

When the trays reappeared with biscuits and wine, punctually at a quarter to nine, there was talk of cards; by-and-by Captain Brown sported a bit of literature. “Have you seen any numbers of ‘The Pickwick Papers’?” said he. “Capital thing!” Now Miss Jenkyns, daughter of a deceased rector, with manuscript sermons and a library of divinity, considered herself literary, and looked on book-talk as a challenge. “Yes,” said she; “indeed, I might say I have read them.” “And what do you think of them? Aren’t they famously good?” “I must say, I don’t think they are by any means equal to Dr Johnson. Still, perhaps, the author is young. Let him persevere, and who knows what he may become if he will take the great Doctor for his model?” “It is quite a different sort of thing, my dear madam,” began the Captain. “I am quite aware of that,” returned she. “And I make allowances.” “Just allow me to read you a scene out of this month’s number,” pleaded he. He read Sam Weller’s account of the “swarry” at Bath. Some of us laughed heartily. I did not dare, because I was staying in the house. Miss Jenkyns sat in patient gravity. When it was ended, she turned to me: “Fetch me ‘Rasselas,’ my dear, out of the book-room.” She read a conversation between Rasselas and Imlac, in a high-pitched, majestic voice; and, ending, said, “I imagine I am now justified in my preference of Dr Johnson as a writer of fiction.” The Captain screwed his lips, drummed on the table; she thought she would give a finishing blow or two. “I consider it vulgar, and below the dignity of literature, to publish in numbers.” “How was the Rambler published, ma’am?” asked he, low. “Dr Johnson’s style is a model for young beginners. My father recommended it when I began to write letters—I have formed my own style upon it; I recommend it to your favourite.” “I should be very sorry for him to exchange his style for any such pompous writing,” said Captain Brown. She felt this as a personal affront. Epistolary writing was her forte; I have seen many a copy written and corrected on the slate before she “seized the half-hour just previous to post-time.” Drawing herself up, she replied, with marked emphasis, “I prefer Dr Johnson to Mr Boz.” It is said—though I won’t vouch for it—that Captain Brown muttered, sotto voce, “D—n Dr Johnson!” If he did, he was penitent afterwards, for he went to stand near her arm-chair, endeavouring to beguile her into pleasanter talk. But she was inexorable; and the next day she made the remark I have mentioned about Miss Jessie’s dimples.

A MERRY CHRISTMAS.

Jo woke first in the gray dawn. No stockings hung at the fireplace; then she remembered, slipped her hand under her pillow, and drew out a little crimson-covered book—"a true guide-book for any pilgrim." She woke Meg, whose green-covered book bore a few words from their mother; Beth and Amy found dove and blue. "Mother wants us to read and love and mind these books," said Meg, and began at once; Jo leaned cheek to cheek and read too; Beth whispered, "How good Meg is! Come, Amy, let's do as they do." "I'm glad mine is blue," said Amy, and the winter sunshine crept in to touch bright heads and serious faces.

"Where is mother?" "Goodness only knows," said Hannah. "Some poor creeter come a-beggin', and your ma went straight off." They looked over the presents. Beth’s handkerchiefs, proudly marked—"Bless the child! she's gone and put 'Mother' on them instead of 'M. March,'" cried Jo—were pronounced "quite sensible" by Meg. Amy slipped in, abashed. "I only meant to change the little bottle for a big one, and I gave all my money to get it, and I'm truly trying not to be selfish any more." The handsome flask, her earnest face—"a trump," said Jo; Beth pinned on her finest rose.

"Merry Christmas, Marmee!" They had just begun when Mrs. March said, "Not far away lies a poor woman with a little new-born baby. Six children are huddled into one bed to keep from freezing... My girls, will you give them your breakfast as a Christmas present?" They were very hungry, but only for a minute. "I'm so glad you came before we began!" cried Jo. "May I go and help carry the things?" asked Beth. Amy heroically took the cream and muffins; Meg covered buckwheats.

The procession set out by back streets. A poor, bare room: broken windows, no fire, ragged bed-clothes, a sick mother, a wailing baby, pale, hungry children. "Ach, mein Gott! it is good angels come to us!" said the poor woman, crying for joy. "Funny angels in hoods and mittens," said Jo, and set them laughing. Hannah made a fire and stopped the panes; Mrs. March fed and comforted the mother and dressed the baby; the girls spread the table and fed the children "like so many hungry birds." "Das ist gut!" "Die Engel-kinder!" they cried, warming purple hands. That was a very happy breakfast, though they didn't get any of it; and when they went away, leaving comfort behind, I think there were not in all the city four merrier people than the hungry little girls who gave away their breakfasts and contented themselves with bread and milk on Christmas morning. "That's loving our neighbor better than ourselves, and I like it," said Meg, as they set out their presents while mother gathered clothes for the poor Hummels. The simple bundles, and the tall vase of red roses and white chrysanthemums, made quite an elegant show. Mrs. March was surprised and touched; slippers on, handkerchief in pocket, rose in her bosom, gloves a "perfect fit"; there was much laughing and kissing and explaining.

The rest of the day went to preparations for the evening. Necessity being the mother of invention, they made everything: pasteboard guitars, antique lamps of butter-boats covered with silver paper, gowns glittering with tin spangles, armor of the same. No gentlemen were admitted; Jo joyfully took male parts, priding herself on a pair of russet-leather boots, an old foil, and a slashed doublet. On Christmas night a dozen girls piled into the "dress-circle;" the bell sounded, curtains flew apart, and "The Witch's Curse, an Operatic Tragedy," began.

"A gloomy wood" and a cave glowed with a small furnace. Hugo the villain stalked in—slouched hat, black beard, mysterious cloak, and the boots—sang of hatred to Roderigo and love for Zara, and summoned, "What ho, minion! I need thee!" Hagar came, promised potions; a rosy spirit sang and dropped a gilded bottle; an ugly black imp croaked and tossed a dark one. Hagar vowed revenge; the curtain fell.

The next set was superb—a tower to the ceiling, lamp at the window, Zara in blue and silver. Roderigo sang a serenade, produced a rope-ladder with five steps, and invited her to descend. "Alas! alas for Zara!" She forgot her train; the tower tottered, leaned, fell with a crash, and buried the lovers. A universal shriek; the russet boots waved from the wreck; a golden head emerged, exclaiming, "I told you so! I told you so!" Don Pedro dragged his daughter out with the aside, "Don't laugh! Act as if it was all right!" and banished Roderigo; the scene went bravely on.

In the castle hall Hagar hid, saw Hugo poison the cups, changed them, and, when the "minion" bore the harmless ones away, set back the deadly draught. Hugo, thirsty after a long warble, drank, clutched and stamped, and fell flat and died, while Hagar sang "in a song of exquisite power and melody." Roderigo, despairing, was saved by a lovely song and a key; he tore off his chains in a spasm of rapture. At last Zara defied the convent, Roderigo demanded her hand, Don Pedro refused because he was not rich—till a letter and a bag from Hagar showered quarts of tin money "till it was quite glorified with the glitter." The stern sire softened; all joined a joyful chorus, and the curtain fell on lovers kneeling to receive his blessing.

Tumultuous applause; then the cot-bed under the "dress-circle" suddenly shut up and extinguished the audience. Roderigo and Don Pedro flew to the rescue, and all came out unhurt, some speechless with laughter. Hannah appeared: "Mrs. March’s compliments, and would the ladies walk down to supper."

It was like Marmee to get up a treat, but anything so fine had not been seen since the days of plenty—ice-cream, pink and white; cake, fruit, French bonbons; and, in the middle, four great bouquets of hot-house flowers. "Is it fairies?" "It's Santa Claus." "Aunt March had a good fit," guessed Jo. "All wrong. Old Mr. Laurence sent it," said Mrs. March. He had heard of their breakfast party and, remembering her father, sent "a few trifles in honor of the day." "That boy put it into his head, I know he did!" cried Jo. She longed to know the Laurence boy who "looks as if he'd like to know us; but he's bashful, and Meg is so prim." Mother liked his manners, had almost asked him in; he looked so wistful, hearing the frolic and having none. "It's a mercy you didn't, mother!" laughed Jo, eyeing her boots. "But we'll have another play some time that he can see. Perhaps he'll help act; wouldn't that be jolly?"

"They are lovely! But Beth's roses are sweeter to me," said Mrs. March, smelling the half-dead posy in her belt. Beth nestled up and whispered, "I wish I could send my bunch to father. I'm afraid he isn't having such a merry Christmas as we are."

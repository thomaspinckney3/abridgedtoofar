In Civil War–era New England, the four March sisters—sensible Meg, fiery Jo, gentle Beth, and artful Amy—chafe at poverty while their father serves as a chaplain and Marmee keeps the hearth with brisk love. To steady themselves they “play pilgrims,” practicing self-denial and improvement, and their first Christmas without presents grows rich when they carry their breakfast to the destitute Hummels. A new friend arrives in the lonely boy next door, Theodore “Laurie” Laurence, whose genial grandfather and great library draw the sisters into theatricals, shared confidences, and a widened world. Their Pickwick Club and hedge post office turn parlor fun into a school for charity, pride, and forgiveness. Rites of passage come quick: at a grand party Meg samples the glitter and emptiness of fashion, Jo’s temper and boyish gusto scrape against “proper” rules, Amy’s contraband pickled limes bring a public humiliation, and shy Beth finds a Palace Beautiful in Mr. Laurence’s piano and wins the old man’s heart. Pilgrim’s Progress shadows their days, as small stumbles and reconciliations become steps toward a “Celestial City.” Beneath the play runs hunger for vocation and for home that holds steady through trial.

The first grave trial erupts when Amy, smarting from exclusion, burns Jo’s painstaking manuscript, and icy peril follows when Jo’s anger lets Amy crash through river ice, a shock that melts bitterness into truer sisterhood. Then a telegram announces Mr. March’s dangerous illness; Jo sells her cherished hair to fund Marmee’s journey, and while she is away Beth catches scarlet fever from the Hummels and hovers near death. The crisis summons quiet heroism in them all and steadfast help from Laurie, and when Beth’s life is spared she rises frailer yet luminous. Amy is banished to formidable Aunt March’s, where she learns small self-mastery; Jo begins to publish “rubbish” and to test ambition against duty; and the Laurences knit themselves to the Marches through music, flowers, and kindly vows. John Brooke, Laurie’s earnest tutor, falls in love with Meg, and despite Aunt March’s scolding and Laurie’s forged note, Meg chooses her “little house” over grand prospects. Mr. March returns to a home tempered by sacrifice, and under June roses Meg marries John in a simple wedding that crowns her apprenticeship. Alcott tethers bread-baking to self-command and sewing to charity, arguing that true riches grow from faithful work and mutual sacrifice.

Marriage proves a second school for Meg, who scorches jelly, braves blushing mortifications, learns to steer between pride and poverty, and at last is blessed with the twins Demi and Daisy. Amy, eager to be brilliant, tries every art with audacity—poker-sketches, tempestuous landscapes, a plaster cast of her foot—and stages an elegant luncheon that ends in the famous lobster fiasco, though her tact survives. Jo, meanwhile, sells a sensational tale for a dazzling hundred dollars, sends Marmee and convalescent Beth to the sea, trims her first novel to suit the market, and endures praise and blame in equal measure. Dragged by Amy through formal “calls,” Jo’s bluntness is schooled by her sister’s polish, and both learn what true breeding means. At a charity fair Amy is snubbed, answers with magnanimity, and earns Aunt Carrol’s favor—and a ticket abroad. Swallowing disappointment, Jo quits Concord for New York to keep her freedom and to spare Laurie, who loves her too much. In Mrs. Kirke’s boardinghouse she meets Professor Friedrich Bhaer, whose hearty kindness and moral clarity steady her as cheap “Dashwood” thrills tempt; his grave disapproval sends her to burn her lurid stories and begin again. Laurie graduates with honors and, in a woodsy grove, pours out his love to Jo; she tenderly refuses, fearing a stormy marriage of two akin wills, and he flees with his grandfather to Europe.

Beth, already drifting from them like a receding tide, accepts her fate with childlike serenity, and the family keep watch as the “little saint” slips away and is made immortal in their hearts. Across the sea Amy sends lively letters from London and Paris, flirts sensibly with Fred Vaughn, and in Switzerland meets the idle, heartsore Laurie, whom she scolds and steadies into manhood. After Beth’s death their grief gentles into love, and Amy and Laurie return quietly, radiantly married. Meg settles into contented matrimony, learning frugal wisdom and sweet authority in the ledger and the nursery, while Jo, sobered, writes with a cleaner fire. On a rainy street Jo and the Professor meet again, choose shawls and grapes for the poor, and under an old umbrella exchange a promise that turns friendship into betrothal. Aunt March dies and leaves Jo the rambling comfort of Plumfield, and Jo and Fritz make of it a homelike school where rich boys and ragamuffins are fed, taught, scolded, and loved. The Bhaer-garten soon hums with ladders and cricket songs, chocolate-drops and Latin verbs, and Jo finds that mothering a wilderness of boys is the finest book she will ever write. One mellow October the tribe gathers in the orchard for apple-picking and Marmee’s sixtieth; a tender poem about “four little chests” recalls Beth’s sainted presence. Looking from Meg’s peaceful hearth to Amy’s devoted marriage and Jo’s bustling school, Mrs. March blesses the rich harvest of three very different dreams, and the sisters see that love, work, and memory have made of home a little heaven.

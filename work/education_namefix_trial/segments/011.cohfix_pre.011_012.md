# CHAPTER VI

ROME (1859-1860)

The tramp in Thüringen lasted four-and-twenty hours. By the end of the first walk, his three companions—John Bancroft, James J. Higginson, and B. W. Crowninshield, all Boston and Harvard like himself—were satisfied with what they had seen, and when they sat down to rest on the spot where Goethe had written—

"Warte nur! balde Rubest du auch!"—

the profoundness of the thought affected them so strongly that they hired a wagon and drove to Weimar the same night. They were all happy in the first fresh breath of leafless spring, the beer better than at Berlin, but all equally in doubt why they had come to Germany, and not one could say why they stayed. Adams stayed because he did not want to go home, and feared his father's patience might be exhausted if he asked to waste time elsewhere.

They could not think their education required a return to Berlin. A few days at Dresden satisfied them that Dresden was a better spot for general education than Berlin, and equally good for reading Civil Law. There was nothing to study in Dresden, and no education to be gained, but the Sistine Madonna and the Correggios were famous; the theatre and opera sometimes excellent, the Elbe prettier than the Spree. He took a room in the household of the usual small government clerk with the usual plain daughters, and continued the language. Possibly one might learn something more by accident, as one had learned something of Beethoven. For the next eighteen months the young man pursued accidental education, since he could pursue no other; and by great good fortune, Europe and America were too busy with their own affairs to give much attention to his.

Perhaps the chief obstacle to his education was his honesty—his simple-minded faith in his intentions. Even after Berlin had become a nightmare, he persuaded himself that his German education was a success. He loved, or thought he loved the people, but the Germany he loved was the eighteenth-century which the Germans were ashamed of, and were destroying as fast as they could. Military Germany was his abhorrence. What he liked was the simple character, the musical and metaphysical abstraction, the blundering incapacity of the German for practical affairs. Until coal-power and railways were created, she was mediæval by nature and geography, and this was what Adams, under the teachings of Carlyle and Lowell, liked.

He was in a fair way to do himself lasting harm, floundering between worlds passed and worlds coming. Suddenly the Emperor Napoleon declared war on Austria and raised a confused point of morals in the mind of Europe. France was the nightmare of Germany; one morning the government clerk rushed into his room to consult a map and measure the distance from Milan to Dresden.

An enlightened young American, with eighteenth-century tastes capped by fragments of a German education, had to make up his mind about the moral value of these conflicting forces. France was the wicked spirit of moral politics, and whatever helped France must be evil. Austria was another evil spirit. Italy was the prize. Should one be Guelph or Ghibelline? Wisdom had been singularly baffled by the problem. Better take sides first, and reason about it for the rest of life. Not that Adams felt any real doubt about his sympathies; but the moment was decisive for political morals. Steam and electricity had brought about new political and social concentrations, which required association with allies as doubtful as Napoleon III, and robberies with violence on a very extensive scale. As long as he could argue that his opponents were wicked, he could join in robbing and killing them without a qualm; but it might happen that the good were robbed. Education founded on mere self-interest was merely Guelph and Ghibelline over again—Machiavelli translated into American.

Luckily for him he had a sister much brighter than he ever was—who after marrying Charles Kuhn had come to Italy, and, like all good Americans, was hotly Italian. In July, 1859, she was at Thun in Switzerland, and there Henry Adams joined them. Mrs. Kuhn had a double superiority: she adored Italy and cordially disliked Germany. She was the first young woman he was ever intimate with—quick, sensitive, wilful, energetic, sympathetic and intelligent enough to supply a score of men with ideas—and he was delighted to give her the reins. It was his first experiment in giving the reins to a woman, and he was so much pleased with the results that he never wanted to take them back. In after life he made a general law of experience—no woman had ever driven him wrong; no man had ever driven him right.

Nothing would satisfy Mrs. Kuhn but to go to the seat of war as soon as the armistice was declared. The party crossed the St. Gothard and reached Milan, picturesque with every uniform and every sign of war. To young Adams this first plunge into Italy passed Beethoven as a piece of accidental education. Like music, it differed from other education in being not a means of pursuing life but one of the ends attained.

Mrs. Kuhn insisted on invading the enemy's country, and the carriage was chartered for Innsbruck by way of the Stelvio Pass. The Valtellina showed war—Garibaldi's Cacciatori the only visible inhabitants—but no one knew whether the abhorred Austrian Jägers would let the travellers through. When at last the carriage turned the last shoulder, where the glacier of the Ortler Spitze tumbled its huge mass down upon the road, even Mrs. Kuhn gasped when stopped at the barricade by the double line of sentries stretching up the mountains till the flash of the gun barrels was lost in the flash of the snow. The earliest of these pictures count for most. The handsome blond Jägers were not to be beaten in courtesy by the handsome olive-toned Cacciatori. The eternal woman as usual had her way; in fifteen minutes the carriage was rolling down to Mals, swarming with German soldiers and German fleas, of which young Adams, thanks to his glimpse of Italy, never again felt quite the old confident charm.

Yet he did his cathedrals, his Rhine, and whatever his companions suggested. Faithful to his self-contracted scheme of two winters in Civil Law, he went back to Dresden with a letter to the Frau Hofräthin von Reichenbach, in whose house Lowell and other Americans had pursued studies more or less serious. Young Adams had nothing to do but take fencing-lessons, visit the galleries and go to the theatre; his social failure was humiliating. Even the doctrine of accidental education broke down—there were no accidents in Dresden. As soon as the winter was over, he closed the German door with a long breath of relief, and took the road to Italy. He had pursued his education, as it pleased him, for eighteen months, and knew no more, for his practical purposes, than the day he graduated. He was as ignorant as a schoolboy of society. He was unfit for any career in Europe, and unfitted for any career in America, and had not natural intelligence enough to see what a mess he had thus far made of his education.

Nothing had been further from his mind when he started in November, 1858, than to become a tourist, but a mere tourist, and nothing else, he had become in April, 1860, when he joined his sister in Florence. His father had been in the right. Supposing his father asked him, on his return, what equivalent he had brought back for the time and money put into his experiment! The only possible answer would be: "Sir, I am a tourist!"

Meanwhile his father had perplexities enough of his own. His Quincy district had sent him to Congress, and in the spring of 1860 he was in the full confusion of nominating Presidential candidates. He supported Mr. Seward. The Republican Party was an unknown force, the Democratic Party torn to pieces. Fathers could blunder as well as sons; for the time, the young man went on his way with a light heart to take whatever fragments of education God or the devil was pleased to give him.

Perhaps the most useful purpose he set himself was that of his pen, for he wrote long letters to his brother Charles, which his brother caused to be printed in the Boston Courier; and the exercise was good for him. He had little to say, and said it not very well, but the habit of expression leads to the search for something to express. In after life when Adams began to learn what some men could see, he shrank into corners of shame at having betrayed his own inferiority as though it were his pride; but it was still the nearest approach he had yet made to an intelligent act.

For the rest, Italy was mostly an emotion and the emotion naturally centred in Rome. The American parent, while bitterly hostile to Paris, seemed disposed to accept Rome as legitimate education; but to young men seeking education in a serious spirit, Rome was altogether the most violent vice in the world, and Rome before 1870 was seductive beyond resistance. The month of May, 1860, was divine. In 1860 the lights and shadows were still mediæval, and mediæval Rome was alive; the shadows breathed and glowed, full of soft forms felt by lost senses. No sand-blast of science had yet skinned off the epidermis of history, thought, and feeling. The pictures were uncleaned, the churches unrestored, the ruins unexcavated. Mediæval Rome was sorcery. Rome was the worst spot on earth to teach nineteenth-century youth what to do with a twentieth-century world. One's emotions in Rome were one's private affair; they must be hurtful, else they could not have been so intense; and they were surely immoral, for no one, priest or politician, could honestly read in the ruins of Rome any other certain lesson than that they were evidence of the just judgments of an outraged God against all the doings of man. Rome was a gospel of anarchy and vice; the last place under the sun for educating the young; yet it was, by common consent, the only spot that the young—of either sex and every race—passionately, perversely, wickedly loved.

Boys never see a conclusion; only on the edge of the grave can man conclude anything. To a young Bostonian, fresh from Germany, Rome seemed a pure emotion, quite free from economic or actual values, and he could not foresee that it was mechanically piling up conundrum after conundrum in his educational path. Rome was actual; it was England; it was going to be America. Rome could not be fitted into an orderly, middle-class, Bostonian, systematic scheme of evolution. No law of progress applied to it. Not even time-sequences—the last refuge of helpless historians—had value for it. The Forum no more led to the Vatican than the Vatican to the Forum. Rienzi, Garibaldi, Tiberius Gracchus, Aurelian might be mixed up in any relation of time, and never lead to a sequence.

The problem became only the more fascinating. Probably it was more vital in May, 1860, than it had been in October, 1764, when the idea of writing the Decline and Fall first started to the mind of Gibbon, "in the close of the evening, as I sat musing in the Church of the Zoccolanti or Franciscan Friars, while they were singing Vespers in the Temple of Jupiter, on the ruins of the Capitol." Murray's Handbook had the grace to quote this passage, which led Adams more than once to sit at sunset on the steps of the Church of Santa Maria di Ara Cœli, curiously wondering that not an inch had been gained by Gibbon—or all the historians since—towards explaining the Fall. The mystery remained unsolved; the charm remained intact. Two great experiments of Western civilization had left there the chief monuments of their failure, and nothing proved that the city might not still survive to express the failure of a third.

The young man had no idea what he was doing. He was a tourist, even to the depths of his sub-consciousness, and it was well for him that he should be nothing else, for even the greatest of men cannot sit with dignity "in the close of evening, among the ruins of the Capitol," unless they have something quite original to say. Tacitus could; so could Michael Angelo; and Gibbon, at a pinch; but none could say very much more than the tourist, who went on repeating the eternal question:—Why! Why!! Why!!!—as his neighbor, the blind beggar, might do, sitting next him, on the church steps. No one ever had answered the question to the satisfaction of any one else; yet every one who had either head or heart felt that sooner or later he must make up his mind what answer to accept. Substitute the word America for the word Rome, and the question became personal.

Perhaps Henry learned something in Rome, though he never knew it. Rome dwarfs teachers. One morning Adams happened to meet Robert Browning, who came in excited, telling of the shock he had received at coming unexpectedly on the guillotine where some criminal had been put to death an hour before. On the background of the Circus Maximus, the Christian martyrs flaming as torches, and the morning's murderer on the block, Browning seemed rather in place; while afterwards, in the light of Belgravia dinner-tables, he never made part of his background except by effacement.

Yet Browning never revealed the poetic depths of Saint Francis, and Mommsen hardly said all that one felt by instinct in the lives of Cicero and Caesar. Rome was a bewildering complex of ideas, experiments, ambitions, energies; without her, the Western world was pointless and fragmentary; she gave heart and unity to it all; yet Gibbon might have gone on for the whole century, sitting among the ruins, and no one would have passed, capable of telling him what it meant. Perhaps it meant nothing.

So it ended; the happiest month of May that life had yet offered. Adams explained to himself that he was absorbing knowledge. He would have put it better had he said that knowledge was absorbing him. He was passive. He went on to Naples, where in the hot June he heard rumors that Garibaldi and his thousand were about to attack Palermo. Through the American Minister he was sent to the seat of war as bearer of despatches to the American sloop of war Iroquois, and seized the chance, going to Palermo in a government transport filled with fleas.

He told all about it to the Boston Courier; but of its bearing on education the Courier did not speak. Loosely, the tourist called it knowledge of men, but it was the reverse; it was knowledge of one's ignorance of men. With the officers of the Iroquois he made an evening call on Garibaldi, whom they found in the Senate House towards sunset, at supper with his picturesque and piratic staff, in the full noise and color of the Palermo revolution. As a spectacle, it belonged to Rossini and the Italian opera. Garibaldi sat down at the window, and had a few words of talk with young Adams. At that moment, Garibaldi was certainly the most serious of the doubtful energies in the world. Society was dividing between banker and anarchist; one or the other, Garibaldi must serve.

Adams had the chance to look this sphinx in the eyes, and, for five minutes, to watch him like a wild animal, at the moment of his greatest achievement. One saw a quiet-featured, quiet-voiced man in a red flannel shirt; absolutely impervious; a type of which Adams knew nothing. Sympathetic it was, and one felt that it was simple; one suspected even that it might be childlike, but could form no guess of its intelligence. In his own eyes Garibaldi might be a Napoleon or a Spartacus; in the hands of Cavour he might become a Condottiere; in the eyes of history he might be only the vigorous player in the game he did not understand. The student was none the wiser. The lesson of Garibaldi, as education, seemed to teach the extreme complexity of extreme simplicity; but one could have learned this from a glow-worm.

Adams left the problem as he found it, and came north to stumble over others. He squandered two or three months on Paris. From the first he had avoided Paris, and had wanted no French influence in his education. He disapproved of France in the lump—disliked the Empire, but most disliked the French mind. France was not serious, and he was not serious in going there. But the curious result followed that, being in no way responsible for the French and sincerely disapproving them, he felt quite at liberty to enjoy everything he disapproved. All thought of serious education had vanished. He tried to acquire a few French idioms, but succeeded better in acquiring a modest taste for Bordeaux and Burgundy; for the Trois Frères Provençaux and Voisin's and the Café Anglais; for the Palais Royal Theatre and the Variétés. In a month he forgot even to disapprove of it; but he studied nothing, entered no society, made no acquaintance. After spending all the money he dared, he started with mixed emotions but no education, for home.

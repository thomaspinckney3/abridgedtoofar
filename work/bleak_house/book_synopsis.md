A London fog thicker than morality settles over Chancery as the interminable suit of Jarndyce and Jarndyce devours lives, fortunes, and hope, and the story divides between an ironical, all-seeing narrator and the candid memoir of Esther Summerson. Raised in secrecy and shame by the severe Miss Barbary, Esther is summoned with fellow wards Ada Clare and Richard Carstone by the kindly John Jarndyce to Bleak House, where she finds a home and the wards find each other. Richard’s charm masks a drift toward the quicksands of the great suit, whose delays promise everything and deliver ruin. In high society, the beautiful Lady Dedlock swoons at the sight of a law-hand in a copied affidavit, unaccountably pierced by a past she has entombed, and her lawyer, Mr. Tulkinghorn—rusty, relentless—begins to burrow. Dickens scatters satirical constellations around them: Mrs. Jellyby’s telescopic philanthropy that neglects her own children, Mr. Skimpole’s childlike parasitism, Krook the rag-and-bottle hoarder with his feral French maid, the venomous Smallweed clan, and poor Jo, the crossing-sweeper “as wot don’t know nothink.” The nameless law-writer Nemo, an opium-haunted copyist, dies in Krook’s clutter, drawing Inspector Bucket—the new, implacable arm of the law—into the web. Jo is hunted from curb to curb until Esther shelters and nurses him in good faith, contracts smallpox, and survives with scars that temper but do not dim her gentleness. Meanwhile Dr. Allan Woodcourt’s quiet competence and Mr. Guppy’s comic, self-interested courtship sketch two very different futures at the edge of her life.

Krook’s shop then erupts in a grotesque blaze of spontaneous combustion, and in the sooty aftermath the Smallweeds pounce upon his papers while the great case drones on. Among the singed scraps lies a chain of confession that will tie worlds together: Nemo was Captain Hawdon, once beloved of Lady Dedlock, and the child of that hidden love is Esther herself. Sent to convalesce at Boythorn’s after her illness, Esther meets Lady Dedlock in a quiet garden; the proud woman reveals herself as Esther’s mother and bids her keep the secret that Tulkinghorn is slowly tightening into a noose. George Rouncewell, the housekeeper’s soldier-son, is driven into debt by the Smallweeds and trapped by Tulkinghorn’s threats over a packet of papers, another pawn in the lawyer’s cold, coercive game. Richard, changing professions as readily as the wind, marries Ada in secret and, under the vampiric guidance of Mr. Vholes, dedicates himself wholly to Jarndyce and Jarndyce, which drains his spirits, money, and hope. Jo fades and dies murmuring gratitude for the rare kindnesses he received, a small martyrdom to a world that polices streets more tenderly than it protects children. Skimpole’s perfumed innocence proves as mercenary as any bill of costs, and Guppy blunders in and out with gossip, affidavits, and a cooling proposal. At last Tulkinghorn confronts Lady Dedlock with proof and delivers his ultimatum of public exposure while promising her no protection. That same night he is found shot in his chambers, and London’s rumor-mill begins to grind anew.

Suspicion falls first upon George and then upon Lady Dedlock, but Bucket, with inexorable courtesy and a constable’s patience, unravels the case to arrest Hortense, the dismissed French maid, whose jealous malice turned to murder. Lady Dedlock, believing herself disgraced and accused, flees from Chesney Wold into storm and darkness, while Sir Leicester, stricken by apoplexy, declares her blameless and beloved. Bucket enlists Esther in a night-long chase through fog and sleet, tracing tearful footprints and shabby refuges across London’s wastes. They find Lady Dedlock dead upon Captain Hawdon’s grave in the wretched churchyard, pride and penitence wrapped together at last. Chesney Wold settles into echoing hush: Sir Leicester survives, diminished yet constant; Volumnia flutters to keep up appearances; Boythorn’s uproarious feud resumes as a pageant; and George becomes a stalwart attendant in the great house.

A ray of hope seems to pierce Chancery’s gloom when a late will, singed among Krook’s rubbish, is produced, but when the matter finally comes on it appears that costs have consumed the whole estate. Jarndyce and Jarndyce collapses with a laugh in court and the dry words “Estate exhausted,” and the dream that ruled Richard’s days sours into despair. Gently steadfast even in defeat, he breaks down and dies young, with Ada—who will soon bear a son named for his father—and Esther and Mr. Jarndyce beside him, blessing the friends who never deserted him. Miss Flite releases her long-caged birds, the Smallweeds wither into their own meanness, and Skimpole is unmasked and drifts away. Through all, Allan Woodcourt loves Esther without condition; and when Jarndyce, who had once asked her to be his wife, sees where her heart truly rests, he releases her from their engagement with a father’s joy. He gives Esther and Allan a modest cottage he has secretly adorned to her tastes and christens it Bleak House, while remaining in her heart forever “my guardian.” Caddy Jellyby, escaped from telescopic philanthropy, toils happily as a dancing mistress and tends a deaf and dumb child with inexhaustible patience, while Mrs. Jellyby redirects her ardor from Borrioboola-Gha to the rights of women; Guppy’s comic self-importance has one last bow and retreats. Esther’s narrative closes years later among roses and children at the new Bleak House, where Allan’s rounds bring comfort to the living and kindness keeps the balance no law can strike. The great cause has dissolved into ashes, yet the little commonwealth of friends endures, proving that in a world of fog and paper, mercy and humanity remain the only clear air.

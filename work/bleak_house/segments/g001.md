## Chapter 1

London. Michaelmas term lately over, and the Lord Chancellor sitting in Lincoln’s Inn Hall. Implacable November weather; mud crust upon crust; smoke lowering with a soft black drizzle, flakes of soot as big as full-grown snowflakes—gone into mourning, one might imagine, for the death of the sun. Dogs undistinguishable in mire; horses scarcely better; foot passengers jostling umbrellas and slipping at corners, adding to the accumulations at compound interest. Fog everywhere. Fog up the river and down; on Essex marshes and Kentish heights; creeping into cabooses; drooping on gunwales; in the eyes and throats of ancient Greenwich pensioners; in the stem and bowl of the skipper’s pipe; pinching the ’prentice on deck. Gas looming with a haggard and unwilling look; shops lighted two hours before their time. Most raw and dense about Temple Bar; and hard by, in Lincoln’s Inn Hall, at the very heart of the fog, sits the Lord High Chancellor in his High Court of Chancery.

Never can there come fog too thick, nor mud too deep, to assort with the groping and floundering condition of this High Court, most pestilent of hoary sinners. Here are wigs mistily engaged in one of the ten thousand stages of an endless cause; solicitors ranged in a matted well (truth not at the bottom of it), with bills, cross-bills, answers, rejoinders, injunctions, affidavits, issues, references to masters, masters’ reports—mountains of costly nonsense. The court is dim; the candles waste; the stained-glass admits no day; the drawl echoes to the roof where the Chancellor contemplates a lantern that has no light in it; the wigs are stuck in a fog-bank.

This is the Court of Chancery, which has its decaying houses and blighted lands in every shire; its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse and its dead in every churchyard; its ruined suitor with his slipshod heels borrowing through the round of every man’s acquaintance; which gives to monied might the means abundantly of wearying out the right; which so exhausts finances, patience, courage, hope; so overthrows the brain and breaks the heart, that there is not an honourable man among its practitioners who does not often give the warning, “Suffer any wrong that can be done you rather than come here!”

Jarndyce and Jarndyce drones on. No man alive knows what it means; the parties least of all. Children born into it, young people married into it, old people died out of it. The little plaintiff promised a rocking-horse has grown up, got a real horse, and trotted away into the other world. The legion of bills become bills of mortality. Not three Jarndyces left perhaps since old Tom Jarndyce, in despair, blew his brains out in Chancery Lane; but the suit still drags its dreary length, perennially hopeless, the profession’s joke and death too.

On this murky afternoon the Chancellor, fenced with crimson cloth and curtains, is addressed by Mr. Tangle—who knows more of Jarndyce and Jarndyce than anybody, and is supposed never to have read anything else—with eighteen learned friends bobbing like hammers, each armed with a little summary of eighteen hundred sheets. “We will proceed with the hearing on Wednesday fortnight,” says the Chancellor (the matter being a mere bud on the forest tree of the parent suit). The prisoner to purge himself of contempt is brought and sent back again; the man from Shropshire cries “My Lord!” and is frowned down by maces, bags, and purses; the little mad old woman in the squeezed bonnet peers and waits for judgment in her favour, hugging her paper matches and dry lavender.

“In reference,” says the Chancellor, “to the young girl and boy—” Mr. Tangle, premature—“Begludship’s pardon—boy.” “—the two young people,” proceeds his lordship, crushing Mr. Tangle, “whom I directed to be in attendance, I will see them and satisfy myself as to the expediency of making the order for their residing with their—” (double eye-glass)—“grandfather.” “Begludship’s pardon—victim of rash action—brains.” A very little counsel with a terrific bass arises out of the fog: “I appear for him. He is a cousin, several times removed.” He drops; the fog knows him no more.

The Chancellor will speak with both young people and mention the matter to-morrow when he takes his seat. The court empties; blue bags are shouldered; the little mad old woman marches off with her documents; the owlish doors are locked. If all the injustice it has done and misery it has caused could be locked up with it and burnt away in a great funeral pyre—so much the better for other parties than the parties in Jarndyce and Jarndyce!


## Chapter 2

It is but a glimpse of the world of fashion we want on this same miry afternoon; not so unlike Chancery but that we may pass, as the crow flies, from precedent to precedent—sleeping beauties whom the knight will wake one day, when all the stopped spits in the kitchen shall begin to turn prodigiously! It is a little world; there is much good in it; but it is wrapped in too much jeweller’s cotton and fine wool, cannot hear the rushing of the larger worlds, nor see them circling round the sun. A deadened world; its growth sometimes unhealthy for want of air.

My Lady Dedlock has returned to her house in town for a few days before she departs for Paris, where her ladyship intends to stay some weeks, after which her movements are uncertain. She has been down at what she calls her “place” in Lincolnshire. The waters are out; the bridge-arch is sapped away; the low-lying ground is a stagnant river with melancholy trees for islands; the heavy drops fall—drip, drip, drip—upon the flagged pavement called from old time the Ghost’s Walk, all night. Little church mouldy on Sundays; the oaken pulpit breaks into a cold sweat; there is a general smell and taste as of the ancient Dedlocks in their graves. My Lady (who is childless), looking out in the early twilight at a keeper’s lodge, seeing a fire upon the latticed panes, a child running into the rain to meet a wrapped-up man—has been put quite out of temper. My Lady says she has been “bored to death.”

Sir Leicester Dedlock is only a baronet, but none mightier. His family is as old as the hills and infinitely more respectable. Nature is a good idea (a little low unless enclosed with a park-fence), but dependent for execution on your great county families. He is ceremonious, stately, strictly conscientious, obstinate, prejudiced, unreasonable; a worthy presence with light-grey hair and whiskers, fine shirt-frill, pure-white waistcoat, blue coat with bright buttons; most polite on every occasion to my Lady, whom he married for love, and whose attractions he holds in the highest estimation—the one little touch of romantic fancy in him.

She has conquered her world and fallen into the freezing mood. “If she could be translated to heaven to-morrow, she might be expected to ascend without any rapture.” Beauty still; a face improved by fashionable expression into classicality; an elegant figure that has the effect of being tall, “the most is made,” says the Honourable Bob Stables on oath, “of all her points;” the best-groomed woman in the whole stud.

Into this scene presents himself Mr. Tulkinghorn—attorney-at-law and eke solicitor of the High Court of Chancery—silent depository of family confidences, of the old school (which seems never to have been young), in knee-breeches tied with ribbons, and stockings that never shine; mute, close, irresponsive; found at corners of dinner-tables and near drawing-room doors, gravely receiving, and burying, the salutations of half the Peerage. He is retainer-like; the butler of the legal cellar of the Dedlocks.

“My Lady’s cause has been again before the Chancellor?” says Sir Leicester. “Yes,” says Mr. Tulkinghorn, bowing to my Lady, who shades her face with a little priceless screen near a hot fire. “Nothing that YOU would call anything has been done to-day.” “Nor ever will be,” says my Lady. Sir Leicester has no objection to an interminable suit; it is slow, expensive, British, constitutional; devised by the perfection of human wisdom for the eternal settlement of everything.

Mr. Tulkinghorn, cautious, proceeds upon the troublesome principle of begging leave to possess his clients with any new proceedings, and, as they are going to Paris, has brought the short affidavits in his pocket. He lays them on a golden talisman at my Lady’s elbow, puts on his spectacles, and begins. The fire is hot; the hand-screen is more beautiful than useful; my Lady changes her position, sees the papers nearer, nearer—“Who copied that?” she asks, suddenly animated. “Is it what you people call law-hand?” Not quite; probably acquired after the original hand was formed, says Mr. Tulkinghorn. “Why do you ask?” “Anything to vary this detestable monotony. Oh, go on, do!”

He reads again. The heat greater; my Lady screens her face. Sir Leicester drowses and starts—“Eh? What do you say?” Mr. Tulkinghorn rises hastily; my Lady murmurs with white lips, “Faint—only that; but it is like the faintness of death. Don’t speak to me. Ring, and take me to my room!” Bells; feet; then silence. Presently Mercury begs Mr. Tulkinghorn to return. “Better now,” quoth Sir Leicester. “I never knew my Lady swoon before. But the weather is extremely trying, and she really has been bored to death down at our place in Lincolnshire.”


## Chapter 3

I have a great deal of difficulty in beginning to write my portion of these pages, for I know I am not clever. I always knew that. When I was a very little girl indeed, I used to say to my doll, “Now, Dolly, I am not clever, you know very well, and you must be patient with me, like a dear!” She would sit propped in a great arm-chair, staring, while I stitched away and told her all my secrets. My dear old doll!

I was brought up, from my earliest remembrance—like some princesses in the fairy stories, only I was not charming—by my godmother. I only knew her as such. She was a good, good woman! She went to church three times on Sundays, to morning prayers on Wednesdays and Fridays, to lectures whenever there were lectures; never smiled; always grave and strict. I felt so different from her, so poor, so trifling, and so far off, that I could never be unrestrained with her—nor love her as I wished. I hoped I might have a better heart; I talked it over with the dear old doll; but I never loved my godmother as I ought.

I had never heard my mama spoken of. Nor my papa. I had never worn a black frock that I could recollect, never been shown my mama’s grave, never taught to pray for any relation but my godmother. Once, timidly approaching what I most thought of, I said, “Oh, dear godmother, tell me, pray do tell me, did Mama die on my birthday?” “Ask me no more, child!” she returned.

It was my birthday—the most melancholy day at home in the whole year. Dinner over, I sat stitching, the clock ticking, the fire clicking, when I looked timidly up and saw in her face, gloomily regarding me, “It would have been far better, little Esther, that you had had no birthday, that you had never been born!” I cried and knelt to her, clinging to her dress. She stood still, her darkened face having such power over me that it stopped me in my vehemence. Raising me, and standing me before her, she said, slowly, with knitted brow and pointed finger, “Your mother, Esther, is your disgrace, and you were hers. The time will come—and soon enough—when you will understand this better and will feel it too, as no one save a woman can. I have forgiven her”—but her face did not relent—“the wrong she did to me, greater than you will ever know. For yourself, unfortunate girl, orphaned and degraded from the first of these evil anniversaries, pray daily that the sins of others be not visited upon your head, according to what is written. Forget your mother and leave all other people to forget her who will do her unhappy child that greatest kindness. Now, go! Submission, self-denial, diligent work, are the preparations for a life begun with such a shadow on it. You are set apart.”

I crept to bed, laid my doll’s cheek against mine wet with tears, and cried myself to sleep, knowing that I had brought no joy to anybody’s heart and that I was to no one upon earth what Dolly was to me.

Time passed quietly and dutifully, until one dreadful night when I was reading aloud to my godmother from St. John—“He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone”—she suddenly cried, from quite another part of the book, “‘Watch ye, therefore, lest coming suddenly he find you sleeping!’” and fell down. For more than a week she lay with her old handsome resolute frown carved upon her face; I kissed her, prayed for her, begged her pardon, entreated her to know me. No. To the last, and after, her frown remained unsoftened.

On the morrow, the portly gentleman in black with the white neckcloth, whom I had once seen before, reappeared. “My name is Kenge—Kenge and Carboy, Lincoln’s Inn. Pray be seated. Don’t distress yourself; it’s of no use.” He told me I had, in fact though not in law, no relation left; that Mrs. Rachael could not be expected to charge herself with me; and that he was instructed to renew an offer made to Miss Barbary two years ago by a highly humane, but at the same time singular, man—Mr. Jarndyce. He spoke in a mellow, full voice, with rounded sentences, listening to himself with satisfaction—“Conversation Kenge,” I afterwards heard he was called.

“Mr. Jarndyce, aware of the desolate position of our young friend, offers to place her at a first-rate establishment where her education shall be completed, her comfort secured, her reasonable wants anticipated; where she shall be eminently qualified to discharge her duty in that station of life unto which it has pleased—shall I say Providence?—to call her. He asks no condition beyond that she apply herself faithfully and do not remove without his concurrence.”

I could not speak, I was so grateful. He added, with importance that made the very seals of his watch fidget, much about Jarndyce and Jarndyce—one of the greatest suits known—a monument of Chancery practice, its aggregate of costs from sixty to seventy thousand pounds! I was ignorant—even of its name.

In a week I left Windsor inside the stagecoach for Reading. Mrs. Rachael was too good to feel any emotion, and gave me one cold kiss like a thaw-drop from a stone porch. I had wrapped the dear old doll in her shawl and quietly laid her in the garden-earth under the tree that shaded my old window. My bird I carried in his cage. As we rolled through the frosty country, a gentleman wrapped to the chin suddenly addressed me. “What the de-vil are you crying for?” He brushed my eyes with a furry cuff and showed me they were wet. “Don’t you want to go wherever you are going? Then look glad!” He muttered angrily about Mrs. Rachael, called her names, drew from his immense pocket a paper parcel—“Here’s the best plum-cake; here’s a little pie made in France—livers of fat geese. Now let’s see you eat ’em.” I thanked him; they were too rich for me. “Floored again!” he cried, and threw them both out of window. When he got out short of Reading, he advised me to be a good girl and studious, and shook hands. I often passed the milestone where we left him, but I never saw him again.

Miss Donny met me at Reading and took me to Greenleaf. There were two Miss Donnys, twins; there was time for everything; nothing could be more precise. As I was to depend by and by on my qualifications as a governess, I was instructed in everything taught there and soon helped to instruct others. Whenever a new pupil came who was unhappy, she made a friend of me; I had resolved on my birthday to be industrious, contented, true-hearted, to do some good and win some love if I could; and I felt almost ashamed to have done so little and have won so much. Six quiet happy years I passed at Greenleaf.

Then, one November morning, came a letter from Kenge and Carboy. Mr. Jarndyce, being about to receive into his house, under an order of the Court of Chancery, a ward of the court, wished to secure an eligible companion, and would be glad of my services in that capacity. Arrangements were made for the coach on Monday next to White Horse Cellar, Piccadilly; a clerk would convey me to their office. Oh, never shall I forget the emotion in the house! The love and kindness of all my dear girls and the two Miss Donnys; the gardener’s nosegay of geraniums; the little school children waving their hats; my heart so full that I had to say to myself, “Esther, now you really must! This will not do!”

London! We drove through the dirtiest, darkest streets (as I thought), and in such confusion that I wondered people kept their senses, until we passed into sudden quiet under an old gateway, and up a steep, broad stair with a churchyard outside the cloisters. This was Kenge and Carboy’s. The young gentleman—Mr. Guppy—politely showed me into Mr. Kenge’s room, pointed me to a little looking-glass in case I wished to look at myself before going “before the Chancellor”—“only a matter of form, miss”—and left me with biscuits and wine and a newspaper, which I read over and over without understanding a word. Two hours later, Mr. Kenge came. “As you are to be the companion of the young lady now in the Chancellor’s private room, Miss Summerson, we thought it well you should be in attendance.” I took his arm; we went under a colonnade and in at a side door to a comfortable room where a young lady and a young gentleman were leaning on a screen by a loud-roaring fire. She looked up—such a beautiful girl! With rich golden hair, soft blue eyes, and a bright, innocent, trusting face. “Miss Clare,” said Mr. Kenge, “this is Miss Summerson.” She came to meet me smiling, then kissed me; in a few minutes we were sitting in the window-seat, talking as free and happy as could be. The young gentleman was her distant cousin, Mr. Richard Carstone—handsome, ingenuous, with a most engaging laugh; nearly two years older than she; both orphans; both never met before that day.

A full-dressed gentleman in a bag wig came in and out; we heard a drawl in the distance—counsel in our case addressing the Lord Chancellor. Presently, we were called into the next room. There, plainly dressed in black and sitting in an arm-chair by the fire, was his lordship; his robe with beautiful gold lace upon another chair. He searched us with a kind, courtly look; laid bundles on his table; silently selected one.

“Miss Clare?” He admired her; I could see it. “The Jarndyce in question is Jarndyce of Bleak House.” “Jarndyce of Bleak House, my lord.” “A dreary name,” said his lordship. “But not a dreary place at present, my lord,” said Mr. Kenge. “Mr. Jarndyce of Bleak House is not married?” “He is not, my lord.” “Young Mr. Richard Carstone is present?” Richard bowed. “Mr. Jarndyce provides a suitable companion for—” “For Miss Ada Clare. This is the young lady. Miss Summerson.” His lordship gave me an indulgent look and acknowledged my curtsy very graciously. “Miss Summerson is not related to any party in the cause?” “No, my lord.” Mr. Kenge whispered; his lordship, eyes on his papers, nodded once or twice.

He spoke a little with my darling—whether she had well reflected, whether she thought she would be happy under Mr. Jarndyce’s roof, and why; then, standing with more ease, with Richard, as if he knew how to go straight to a boy’s candour. “Very well! I shall make the order. Mr. Jarndyce of Bleak House has chosen, so far as I may judge”—and here he looked at me—“a very good companion for the young lady; and the arrangement altogether seems the best of which the circumstances admit.” We were dismissed, much obliged for his affability.

Under the colonnade, while Mr. Kenge went back for a question, we stood in the fog when a little old woman in a squeezed bonnet, carrying a reticule, came curtsying and smiling with great ceremony. “Oh! The wards in Jarndyce! Ve-ry happy to have the honour! I was a ward myself. I expect a judgment. Shortly. On the Day of Judgment. I have discovered that the sixth seal is the Great Seal. It has been open a long time! Pray accept my blessing.” “Mad!” whispered Richard. “Right! Mad, young gentleman,” she returned, quick as lightning. “Youth. And hope. And beauty. And Chancery. And Conversation Kenge! Pray accept my blessing!” When Mr. Kenge rejoined us, she mincingly addressed him too—with his documents—and we returned to his room.


## Chapter 4

We were to pass the night, Mr. Kenge told us, at Mrs. Jellyby’s; he took it for granted we knew her. We did not. “Indeed! Mrs. Jellyby,” said he, as if reading from the hearth-rug, “is a lady of remarkable strength of character who devotes herself entirely to the public; at present to Africa—the general cultivation of the coffee berry and the natives, and the happy settlement, on the banks of African rivers, of our superabundant home population. Mr. Jarndyce, who is much sought by philanthropists, has a high opinion of Mrs. Jellyby.” Of Mr. Jellyby he could say little—“merged, so to speak, in the more shining qualities of his wife.” He rang; the young gentleman, Mr. Guppy, announced our boxes sent round and a coach waiting.

We twisted up Chancery Lane, cut along Holborn, and turned under an archway into a narrow street of high houses like an oblong cistern to hold the fog. A confused little crowd, principally children, was gathered about a house with JELLYBY on a tarnished brass plate. “Don’t be frightened!” said Mr. Guppy, looking in. “One of the young Jellybys been and got his head through the area railings!” The dirtiest little unfortunate I ever saw was wedged by the neck. A milkman and a beadle benevolently tugged his legs under the impression that his skull was compressible. Thinking where his head could go his body could follow, I held his pinafore while Richard and Mr. Guppy ran through the kitchen. He was happily got down—then beat Mr. Guppy with a hoop-stick in quite a frantic manner.

Within, we passed several more children we could hardly avoid treading on; one fell downstairs with a bump on every stair—Richard counted seven, and one for the landing. Mrs. Jellyby, pretty, diminutive, plump, with handsome eyes that seemed to look a long way off—as if, Richard said, they could see nothing nearer than Africa—received us with perfect equanimity. Her hair was very good, but too much occupied with African duties to be brushed; her dress did not nearly meet up the back, and the open space was railed with a lattice-work of stay-lace like a summer-house. The room was strewn with papers, filled by a great writing-table, untidy and very dirty; the two great tallow candles in tin sticks made the room taste of hot grease; there was nothing in the grate but ashes, a bundle of wood, and a poker.

At the writing-table sat a jaded, unhealthy-looking girl, by no means plain, biting the feather of her pen and staring at us—nobody ever in such a state of ink—from tumbled hair to pretty feet in broken satin slippers trodden down at heel. “You find me, my dears,” said Mrs. Jellyby sweetly, “as usual, very busy. The African project at present employs my whole time. We hope by this time next year to have from a hundred and fifty to two hundred healthy families cultivating coffee and educating the natives of Borrioboola-Gha, on the left bank of the Niger.” It was advancing; it involved correspondence with public bodies and private individuals anxious for the welfare of their species; and she wondered I had never turned my thoughts to Africa. She put towards us pamphlets; she dictated to her eldest daughter—“Caddy”—her amanuensis. “Presents her compliments to Mr. Swallow, and begs—” “No, Peepy! Not on my account!” Peepy (so self-named), with a strip of plaster on his forehead, came to exhibit wounded knees—bruises and dirt so balanced that I did not know which to pity most. “Go along, you naughty Peepy!” said Mrs. Jellyby serenely, fixing her fine eyes on Africa again. I ventured to take him and he fell asleep in my arms, sobbing at longer and longer intervals, until he was quiet; meanwhile Africa grew more momentous, and all else more insignificant.

At six o’clock, our dinner hour being nominally five (for they dined at all hours), Caddy showed us our rooms. They were excessively bare and disorderly; my curtain was fastened up with a fork. “Some hot water?” “Oh, it’s not the trouble; the question is, if there IS any.” The boiler was out of order; they could not find the kettle. The landing swarmed with children peeping and imperilling noses and fingers between hinges; I proposed they should come in and be very good, and I would tell them Little Red Riding Hood while I dressed, which they did, Peepy waking opportune for the wolf.

Downstairs a young woman with a swelled face bound in flannel blew the drawing-room fire and choked dreadfully; the room smoked so that we sat coughing and crying with the windows open for half an hour, during which Mrs. Jellyby directed letters about Africa with unabated sweetness. Richard, who had washed his hands in a pie-dish and found the kettle on his dressing-table, made Ada so merry that they made me merry too. Presently we went down to dinner by careful steps, for the stair-carpets, besides being deficient in stair-wires, were so torn as to be traps. There was a fine cod-fish, a piece of roast beef, cutlets, and a pudding—an excellent dinner if it had had any cooking; but it was almost raw. The young woman dropped everything where it happened to go; the person in pattens (supposed to be the cook) skirmished with her at the door; ill-will appeared between them. Letters arrived in heaps; Richard saw four envelopes in the gravy at once. Proceedings of ladies’ committees, resolutions of meetings, applications excited in various ways about coffee and natives; others required answers, and Caddy was sent from table again and again to write.

A mild bald gentleman in spectacles dropped into a vacant chair and submitted himself to Borrioboola-Gha in silence. Not a word did he speak; he might have been a native but for his complexion. He was Mr. Jellyby. A loquacious young man called Mr. Quale, with large shining knobs for temples and all his hair brushed back, told Ada he was a philanthropist, delighted in drawing Mrs. Jellyby out—“You have received as many as two hundred letters in a single day, have you not?”—repeating her answers like an interpreter. He called the matrimonial alliance of Mrs. Jellyby with Mr. Jellyby the union of mind and matter. During the whole evening Mr. Jellyby sat in a corner with his head against the wall as if subject to low spirits. It seemed he had several times opened his mouth when alone with Richard after dinner, as if something were on his mind, but had always shut it again without saying anything.

Mrs. Jellyby, in a nest of waste paper, drank coffee and dictated. The subject with Mr. Quale was (if I understood) the brotherhood of humanity; she uttered beautiful sentiments; but Ada and I, beset by children, whispered “Puss in Boots,” and I don’t know what else, until Mrs. Jellyby accidentally remembered them and sent them to bed. As Peepy cried for me, I carried him up, where the young woman with the flannel bandage charged among the little family like a dragon and overturned them into cribs.

I made our room a little tidy and coaxed a very cross fire to burn, which at last it did, brightly. On my return, I felt that Mrs. Jellyby looked down on me for being so frivolous; I was sorry, though I had no higher pretensions. It was nearly midnight when we could go to bed; we left Mrs. Jellyby among her papers and Caddy biting the feather of her pen.

“What a strange house!” said Ada. “How curious of my cousin Jarndyce to send us here!” “My love,” said I, “it quite confuses me. It must be very good of Mrs. Jellyby to take such pains for the natives—and yet—Peepy and the housekeeping!” Ada put her arm about my neck and told me I would make a home out of even this house. She never knew she only praised herself.

“Your cousin, Mr. Jarndyce,” I asked; “would you mind describing him to me?” She had never seen him. He had written her a plain, honest letter proposing our arrangement and saying in time it might heal some wounds made by the miserable Chancery suit. Richard had seen him once at Winchester—“a bluff, rosy fellow.” It set me wondering so that, when Ada slept, I still sat by the fire, until a tap came to the door.

Miss Jellyby stood there shivering with a broken candle in a broken candlestick and an egg-cup of vinegar. She would not sit; she smeared her inky face with vinegar, frowning gloomily. “I wish Africa was dead!” she said, on a sudden. I was going to remonstrate. “I do! Don’t talk to me, Miss Summerson. I hate it and detest it. It’s a beast!” I laid my hand upon her head and said it would be cool to-morrow. She stood pouting, then turned softly to Ada’s bed—“She is very pretty!”—and kissed her. Back again, bosom heaving, she burst out: “It’s disgraceful. The whole house is disgraceful. The children are disgraceful. I’M disgraceful. Pa’s miserable, and no wonder! Priscilla drinks—she’s always drinking. It was as bad as a public-house waiting at dinner; you know it was!” I would have spoken; she would not hear me; then, in a moment, she knelt by me, hid her face in my dress, begged my pardon, and wept. “If you could have taught me, I could have learnt from you! I am so very miserable, and I like you so much!”

She would not rise, but drew a ragged stool, and still held my dress; by degrees the poor tired girl fell asleep. I raised her head to rest upon my lap, covered us with shawls; the fire went out; and all night long she slumbered thus before the ashy grate. At length the purblind day feebly struggled with the fog; I opened my eyes to encounter those of a dirty-faced little spectre fixed upon me. Peepy had scaled his crib and crept down in his bed-gown and cap, so cold that his teeth were chattering as if he had cut them all.

It is but a glimpse of the world of fashion we want on this same miry afternoon; not so unlike Chancery but that we may pass, as the crow flies, from precedent to precedent—sleeping beauties whom the knight will wake one day, when all the stopped spits in the kitchen shall begin to turn prodigiously! It is a little world; there is much good in it; but it is wrapped in too much jeweller’s cotton and fine wool, cannot hear the rushing of the larger worlds, nor see them circling round the sun. A deadened world; its growth sometimes unhealthy for want of air.

My Lady Dedlock has returned to her house in town for a few days before she departs for Paris, where her ladyship intends to stay some weeks, after which her movements are uncertain. She has been down at what she calls her “place” in Lincolnshire. The waters are out; the bridge-arch is sapped away; the low-lying ground is a stagnant river with melancholy trees for islands; the heavy drops fall—drip, drip, drip—upon the flagged pavement called from old time the Ghost’s Walk, all night. Little church mouldy on Sundays; the oaken pulpit breaks into a cold sweat; there is a general smell and taste as of the ancient Dedlocks in their graves. My Lady (who is childless), looking out in the early twilight at a keeper’s lodge, seeing a fire upon the latticed panes, a child running into the rain to meet a wrapped-up man—has been put quite out of temper. My Lady says she has been “bored to death.”

Sir Leicester Dedlock is only a baronet, but none mightier. His family is as old as the hills and infinitely more respectable. Nature is a good idea (a little low unless enclosed with a park-fence), but dependent for execution on your great county families. He is ceremonious, stately, strictly conscientious, obstinate, prejudiced, unreasonable; a worthy presence with light-grey hair and whiskers, fine shirt-frill, pure-white waistcoat, blue coat with bright buttons; most polite on every occasion to my Lady, whom he married for love, and whose attractions he holds in the highest estimation—the one little touch of romantic fancy in him.

She has conquered her world and fallen into the freezing mood. “If she could be translated to heaven to-morrow, she might be expected to ascend without any rapture.” Beauty still; a face improved by fashionable expression into classicality; an elegant figure that has the effect of being tall, “the most is made,” says the Honourable Bob Stables on oath, “of all her points;” the best-groomed woman in the whole stud.

Into this scene presents himself Mr. Tulkinghorn—attorney-at-law and eke solicitor of the High Court of Chancery—silent depository of family confidences, of the old school (which seems never to have been young), in knee-breeches tied with ribbons, and stockings that never shine; mute, close, irresponsive; found at corners of dinner-tables and near drawing-room doors, gravely receiving, and burying, the salutations of half the Peerage. He is retainer-like; the butler of the legal cellar of the Dedlocks.

“My Lady’s cause has been again before the Chancellor?” says Sir Leicester. “Yes,” says Mr. Tulkinghorn, bowing to my Lady, who shades her face with a little priceless screen near a hot fire. “Nothing that YOU would call anything has been done to-day.” “Nor ever will be,” says my Lady. Sir Leicester has no objection to an interminable suit; it is slow, expensive, British, constitutional; devised by the perfection of human wisdom for the eternal settlement of everything.

Mr. Tulkinghorn, cautious, proceeds upon the troublesome principle of begging leave to possess his clients with any new proceedings, and, as they are going to Paris, has brought the short affidavits in his pocket. He lays them on a golden talisman at my Lady’s elbow, puts on his spectacles, and begins. The fire is hot; the hand-screen is more beautiful than useful; my Lady changes her position, sees the papers nearer, nearer—“Who copied that?” she asks, suddenly animated. “Is it what you people call law-hand?” Not quite; probably acquired after the original hand was formed, says Mr. Tulkinghorn. “Why do you ask?” “Anything to vary this detestable monotony. Oh, go on, do!”

He reads again. The heat greater; my Lady screens her face. Sir Leicester drowses and starts—“Eh? What do you say?” Mr. Tulkinghorn rises hastily; my Lady murmurs with white lips, “Faint—only that; but it is like the faintness of death. Don’t speak to me. Ring, and take me to my room!” Bells; feet; then silence. Presently Mercury begs Mr. Tulkinghorn to return. “Better now,” quoth Sir Leicester. “I never knew my Lady swoon before. But the weather is extremely trying, and she really has been bored to death down at our place in Lincolnshire.”

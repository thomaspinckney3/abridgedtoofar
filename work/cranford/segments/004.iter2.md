It was impossible to live a month at Cranford and not know the daily habits of each resident; and long before my visit was ended I knew much concerning the whole Brown trio. Their poverty had been spoken of simply and openly from the first; what remained to be discovered was the Captain’s infinite kindness of heart. We discussed, for lack of other news, his taking a poor old woman’s dinner out of her hands one very slippery Sunday, and steering with grave dignity by her side, carrying her baked mutton and potatoes safely home. This was thought very eccentric; we expected apologies; but on Monday he came down upon us, loud and bass as ever, wig jaunty and well-curled, as if he had forgotten all about Sunday.

By the fellowship of Shetland wool and new stitches, Miss Pole and Miss Jessie Brown had struck up an intimacy, and I learned more of the Browns than I had while lodging with Miss Jenkyns, who had never forgiven what she called Captain Brown’s disparaging remarks on Dr Johnson as a writer of light and agreeable fiction. I found Miss Brown seriously ill of some lingering, incurable complaint, the pain of which gave the uneasy expression to her face that I had taken for unmitigated crossness. Cross she was at times, when the nervous irritability became past endurance; and then came bitter self-upbraidings, for she accused herself of being the cause why her father and sister must pinch for the small necessaries of her condition. The very generosity of her nature added acerbity to her temper. All this was borne by Miss Jessie and her father with absolute tenderness. At home I forgave Miss Jessie her singing out of tune and juvenility of dress. Captain Brown’s dark Brutus wig and padded, threadbare coat were but remnants of his military smartness, now worn unconsciously. He was full of barrack-resource; “no one could black his boots to please him except himself,” he said; and he was not above saving the little maid’s labour, knowing, I doubt not, how hard a place a sick-room made of that little house.

Soon after the memorable dispute he tried to make peace with Miss Jenkyns by presenting her with a wooden fire-shovel of his own making, having heard how the grating of an iron one annoyed her. She received it with cool gratitude, and, when he was gone, bade me put it away in the lumber-room; for, to her thinking, no present from a man who preferred Mr Boz to Dr Johnson could be less jarring than iron.

I went to Drumble; but letters kept me au fait of Cranford. Miss Pole’s were all crochet commissions mixed with gossip; Miss Matilda Jenkyns (who did not mind being called Miss Matty when her sister was not by) wrote nice, kind, rambling letters, now and then venturing into an opinion of her own; but suddenly pulling herself up, and either begging me not to name what she had said, as Deborah thought differently, and she knew, or else putting in a postscript to the effect that, since writing the above, she had been talking over the subject with Deborah, and was quite convinced that, etc.—(here probably followed a recantation of every opinion she had given in the letter). Then came Miss Jenkyns—Debōrah, as she liked Miss Matty to call her, her father having once said that the Hebrew name ought to be so pronounced. I secretly think she took the Hebrew prophetess for a model in character; and, indeed, she was not unlike the stern prophetess in some ways, making allowance, of course, for modern customs and difference in dress. Miss Jenkyns wore a cravat, and a little bonnet like a jockey-cap, and altogether had the appearance of a strong-minded woman; although she would have despised the modern idea of women being equal to men. Equal, indeed! she knew they were superior. But to return to her letters. Everything in them was stately and grand like herself. I have been looking them over (dear Miss Jenkyns, how I honoured her!) and I will give an extract, more especially because it relates to our friend Captain Brown:—

“The Honourable Mrs Jamieson has only just quitted me; and, in the course of conversation, she communicated to me the intelligence that she had yesterday received a call from her revered husband’s quondam friend, Lord Mauleverer. You will not easily conjecture what brought his lordship within the precincts of our little town. It was to see Captain Brown, with whom, it appears, his lordship was acquainted in the ‘plumed wars,’ and who had the privilege of averting destruction from his lordship’s head when some great peril was impending over it, off the misnomered Cape of Good Hope. You know our friend the Honourable Mrs Jamieson’s deficiency in the spirit of innocent curiosity, and you will therefore not be so much surprised when I tell you she was quite unable to disclose to me the exact nature of the peril in question. I was anxious, I confess, to ascertain in what manner Captain Brown, with his limited establishment, could receive so distinguished a guest; and I discovered that his lordship retired to rest, and, let us hope, to refreshing slumbers, at the Angel Hotel; but shared the Brunonian meals during the two days that he honoured Cranford with his august presence. Mrs Johnson, our civil butcher’s wife, informs me that Miss Jessie purchased a leg of lamb; but, besides this, I can hear of no preparation whatever to give a suitable reception to so distinguished a visitor. Perhaps they entertained him with ‘the feast of reason and the flow of soul’; and to us, who are acquainted with Captain Brown’s sad want of relish for ‘the pure wells of English undefiled,’ it may be matter for congratulation that he has had the opportunity of improving his taste by holding converse with an elegant and refined member of the British aristocracy. But from some mundane failings who is altogether free?”

Miss Pole and Miss Matty wrote to me by the same post; the latter humbly apologised for writing at the same time as her sister, but, in spite of a little bad spelling, gave me the best idea of the commotion; for, except the people at the Angel, the Browns, Mrs Jamieson, and a little lad his lordship had sworn at for driving a dirty hoop against the aristocratic legs, I could not hear of any one with whom his lordship had held conversation.

When I returned in summer there had been neither births, deaths, nor marriages; the greatest event was a new drawing-room carpet at the Jenkynses’. Oh, the busy work Miss Matty and I had in chasing the sunbeams with newspapers, and in cutting and stitching little paper paths to every chair lest shoes should defile the purity of the carpet! Do you make paper paths for every guest to walk upon in London?

Captain Brown and Miss Jenkyns still smarted under their literary “raw.” He walked the streets so absorbed in Mr Boz that he all but ran against her; and though his apologies were sincere, she owned to me she had rather he had knocked her down—if only he had been reading a higher style of literature. He looked older, more worn; yet he was as bright and cheerful as ever, unless asked about his daughter. “She suffers a great deal, and she must suffer more: we do what we can to alleviate her pain;—God’s will be done!” and he took off his hat. I found, from Miss Matty, that everything had been done, in fact. A medical man, of high repute in that country neighbourhood, had been sent for, and every injunction he had given was attended to, regardless of expense. Miss Matty was sure they denied themselves many things in order to make the invalid comfortable; but they never spoke about it; and as for Miss Jessie!—“I really think she’s an angel,” said poor Miss Matty, quite overcome. “To see her way of bearing with Miss Brown’s crossness, and the bright face she puts on after she’s been sitting up a whole night and scolded above half of it, is quite beautiful. Yet she looks as neat and as ready to welcome the Captain at breakfast-time as if she had been asleep in the Queen’s bed all night. My dear! you could never laugh at her prim little curls or her pink bows again if you saw her as I have done.” I could only feel very penitent, and greet Miss Jessie with double respect when I met her next. She looked faded and pinched; and her lips began to quiver, as if she was very weak, when she spoke of her sister. But she brightened, and sent back the tears that were glittering in her pretty eyes, as she said—“But, to be sure, what a town Cranford is for kindness! I don’t suppose any one has a better dinner than usual cooked but the best part of all comes in a little covered basin for my sister. The poor people will leave their earliest vegetables at our door for her. They speak short and gruff, as if they were ashamed of it: but I am sure it often goes to my heart to see their thoughtfulness.”

“But why does not this Lord Mauleverer do something for the man who saved his life?” said I.

“Why, you see, unless Captain Brown has some reason for it, he never speaks about being poor; and he walked along by his lordship looking as happy and cheerful as a prince; and as they never called attention to their dinner by apologies, and as Miss Brown was better that day, and all seemed bright, I daresay his lordship never knew how much care there was in the background. He did send game in the winter pretty often, but now he is gone abroad.”

I had often occasion to notice the use that was made of fragments and small opportunities in Cranford; the rose-leaves that were gathered ere they fell to make into a potpourri for someone who had no garden; the little bundles of lavender flowers sent to strew the drawers of some town-dweller, or to burn in the chamber of some invalid. Things that many would despise, and actions which it seemed scarcely worth while to perform, were all attended to in Cranford. Miss Jenkyns stuck an apple full of cloves for Miss Brown’s room; and as she put in each clove she uttered a Johnsonian sentence.

One day Captain Brown called to thank her for many little kindnesses we had not known. He had suddenly become like an old man; twice he said, “What Jessie has been to us, God only knows!” then hastily shook hands and left. That afternoon we saw little groups in the street listening aghast. Jenny burst in: “Oh, ma’am! Captain Brown is killed by them nasty cruel railroads!” “How?—where—where? Good God! Jenny, don’t waste time in crying, but tell us something.” Miss Matty rushed out into the street at once, and collared the man who was telling the tale. “Come in—come to my sister at once, Miss Jenkyns, the rector’s daughter. Oh, man, man! say it is not true,” she cried, as she brought the affrighted carter, sleeking down his hair, into the drawing-room, where he stood with his wet boots on the new carpet, and no one regarded it. “The Captain was a-reading some new book as he was deep in, a-waiting for the down train; a little lass gave her sister the slip and came toddling across; he looked up at the sound, darted on the line, cotched it up; his foot slipped, and the train came over him in no time. The child’s safe, with only a bang on its shoulder.” The great rough carter puckered up his manly face, and turned away to hide his tears. I turned to Miss Jenkyns. She looked very ill, as if she were going to faint, and signed to me to open the window. “Matilda, bring me my bonnet. I must go to those girls. God pardon me, if ever I have spoken contemptuously to the Captain!”

They managed that Miss Brown, who could not live many days, should be spared the shock: she was told her father had been summoned for a short railway journey. Next day the paper said, “the gallant gentleman was deeply engaged in the perusal of a number of ‘Pickwick.’” “Poor, dear, infatuated man!” sighed Miss Jenkyns.

Miss Jessie would follow the corpse to the parish church. No dissuasive availed. She longed, poor thing, I have no doubt, to cry alone over the grave of the dear father to whom she had been all in all, and to give way, for one little half-hour, uninterrupted by sympathy and unobserved by friendship. At last Miss Jenkyns resolved to accompany her. She trimmed her little black silk bonnet with crape till it looked half helmet, half jockey-cap, and in that hybrid bonnet supported Miss Jessie with tender firmness, allowing her to weep her passionate fill.

When we came in the morning, there was that in Miss Brown’s room stronger than all nursing. She was dying. Miss Jessie knelt to catch the last soft awful whispers. “Oh, Jessie! Jessie! How selfish I have been! God forgive me!… And my father, my dear, dear father!… tell my father how I longed and yearned to see him at last, and to ask his forgiveness.” A light came into Miss Jessie’s face. “Would it comfort you, dearest, to think that he does know?… Mary! he has gone before you to the place where the weary are at rest.” Miss Brown’s lips formed, “Father, mother, Harry, Archy;—But you will be alone, Jessie!” The tears rolled down; she lifted her hands: “Though He slay me, yet will I trust in Him.” In a few moments more Miss Brown lay calm and still.

After this second funeral, Miss Jessie came to stay with Miss Jenkyns. She had something above twenty pounds a year, and the interest of the furniture-money; not enough to live on. She spoke quietly of sewing, nursing, housekeeping, even a shop. Miss Jenkyns declared she should do no such thing, and almost stormed on the rank due to a captain’s daughter—though she stood over her like a dragoon till the last spoonful of arrowroot was finished.

A caller was announced; a card given; Miss Jessie flushed scarlet. Major Gordon entered, a tall, frank-looking man. Years before, serving with Captain Brown, he had loved the blooming girl of eighteen; she had refused him, for her sister’s threatened sufferings and her father’s need left her none to nurse but herself. Angry, he had gone abroad, and, travelling, read at Rome in Galignani of Captain Brown’s death and had come straight to Cranford. Presently Miss Matty burst in, eyes large with terror. “Oh, goodness me! Deborah, there’s a gentleman sitting in the drawing-room with his arm round Miss Jessie’s waist!” “The most proper place in the world for his arm to be in. Go away, Matilda, and mind your own business,” said Miss Jenkyns.

Years afterwards I saw poor Miss Jenkyns again, old and feeble, something of her strong mind gone. Mrs Gordon had kept up a warm intercourse; happiness had brought back a touch of bloom and made her dimples in place. Little Flora Gordon was staying with the Misses Jenkyns, reading aloud the Rambler to the failing elder. “Ah! you find me changed, my dear. Did you ever read the Rambler?—wonderful!—and the most improving reading for Flora, better than that strange old book, with the queer name, poor Captain Brown was killed for reading—that book by Mr Boz, you know—‘Old Poz’; when I was a girl—but that’s a long time ago—I acted Lucy in ‘Old Poz.’” She babbled on long enough for Flora to get a good long spell at the Christmas Carol, which Miss Matty had left on the table.

On a yawl called the Nellie anchored in the Thames at dusk, Marlow begins telling his shipboard companions how “this also has been one of the dark places of the earth.” A boyhood passion for maps and the serpentine river through the blank center of Africa leads him, as an adult without a berth, to seek command of a Congo steamer for a European trading Company. In the sepulchral city he encounters two knitting women, a wry doctor who measures his skull, and the pieties of his well-connected aunt, all hinting at a mission dressed in light but run for profit. News that a captain named Fresleven has been killed gives Marlow his post, and soon he is sailing down a featureless African coast past a French warship absurdly shelling the bush. At the Company’s Outer Station he sees blasted cliffs, ruined machinery, a chain-gang in iron collars, and, in a shaded grove, men wasting away—the “helpers” quietly dying. Only the immaculate chief accountant, who keeps perfect books and collars amid chaos, offers a name that will haunt the tale: Kurtz, a remarkable agent deep upriver.

Marlow treks two hundred miles inland to the Central Station to find his steamer wrecked on stones and a Manager whose health, vagueness, and uneasiness govern a camp deadened by idleness and obsession with ivory. A brickmaker who makes no bricks tries to pump Marlow, hints at high patrons in Europe, and shows him a painting Kurtz left behind: a blindfolded woman carrying a torch in a field of darkness. The Eldorado Exploring Expedition—headed by the Manager’s uncle—arrives with donkeys and secret rapacity, then vanishes into the forest, as Marlow spends months salvaging and repairing his “tin-pot” boat while craving the rivets that never come. Rumors multiply: Kurtz is ill, his station imperiled, yet he sends in more ivory than all others combined and writes of stations as beacons to “humanize” the land. The wilderness presses around the listless pilgrims, whose only fervor is for percentages and precedence, while Marlow steadily fixes his gaze on the voice and figure he has not yet seen. At last, with cannibals for crew, a few armed pilgrims, and the Manager aboard, he pushes into the river’s choking silence toward the Inner Station.

The journey upriver is a regression to primordial time—shoals and snags, hippos and alligators, drumbeats at night, an oppressive stillness watched by inscrutable forest. In fog a mourning wail rises and, when it lifts, a volley of arrows showers the boat; Marlow rams the bank to save the crippled steamer, and his devoted helmsman dies with a spear in his side. They find a hut with stacked firewood, a penciled warning to “Hurry up. Approach cautiously,” and an old seamanship manual annotated in cipher, signs of another solitary white man within the green vastness. At the Inner Station a patched, ebullient Russian—part clown, part acolyte—greets them and confesses he has lived by Kurtz’s words; he also reveals the “symbols” Marlow spied on fenceposts are rotting human heads, evidence of a rule without restraint. The Russian says the tribes adore Kurtz, who raids with thunder and lightning and has become the object of rites; a superb, barbaric woman appears on the bank, a figure of the wilderness’s own fierce soul. The bearers bring out a skeletal, towering Kurtz whose voice still commands the multitude, and the Manager moves to spirit him downriver while muttering of “unsound method” that has ruined the district.

In the night Kurtz crawls toward the forest, drawn by drums and devotions, but Marlow confronts and coaxes him back, invoking success in Europe and threatening throttling rather than let him be lost. At departure the woman stretches her arms after the steamer as Marlow’s whistle scatters the crowd, and the river’s brown current bears them swiftly toward the sea while Kurtz’s voice pours out grandiose plans and hungry claims—“my Intended, my ivory, my station.” Among his papers is a rhapsodic report for the International Society for the Suppression of Savage Customs, ending with the scrawled directive, “Exterminate all the brutes!” As the steamer delays for repairs, Kurtz gives Marlow a packet of writings and a photograph, then, on the brink of extinction, looks into himself and whispers the verdict that becomes his epitaph: “The horror! The horror!” The pilgrims bury something in the mud, the Manager is satisfied the “affair” is settled, and Marlow, sick and pared to essentials, finds his loyalty fixed not on the Company but on the disturbing totality of Kurtz’s confession. To Marlow, the dying man had at least “something to say,” a judgment that pierced the veils of eloquence and civilization to face the darkness he had embraced.

Back in the sepulchral city, clerks and functionaries angle for Kurtz’s papers in the names of commerce and science; a cousin calls him a great musician, a journalist claims he was born to lead any extreme party, and each tries to fix a tidy label on a man who was always “more than a voice.” Marlow surrenders only the sanitized report and keeps the rest, then visits Kurtz’s Intended, whose mourning and unwavering belief preserve a luminous image of him as noble and good. She begs for his last words, and Marlow, unable to bear the full truth in the fragile temple of her faith, lies that the final utterance was her name. Leaving her in the glow of her sustaining illusion, he returns in memory to the river and to the recognition that the veneer of civilization is perilously thin and easily torn away. On the Nellie, his listeners sit in darkness as the ebb begins, and the Thames itself, gateway of empire, seems to lead into “the heart of an immense darkness.” The tale closes as it opened, on still water and lowering sky, having traced a passage through external wilderness and inward night to expose the hypocrisies of imperial “light,” the seduction of power, and the abyss within the human soul.

## Chapter 1

The Dashwoods had long been settled at Norland Park in Sussex, living with such decorum as to secure general good opinion. Its late owner, a single man much advanced in years, having lost the sister who had been his companion, invited into his house his nephew, Mr. Henry Dashwood, with Mrs. Dashwood and their three girls; and there, in the cheerfulness of children and the constant attentions of their elders, his declining days were comforted. He meant Norland for his nephew; but dying at last, he left it to Mr. Dashwood only for life, and so strictly entailed upon Mr. Dashwood’s son, and that son’s little boy, that no charge could be laid on the estate for the widow and daughters. To the three girls he left but a thousand pounds apiece.

Mr. Dashwood’s disappointment was soothed by hope; he might, by economy and time, save a sum. But he survived his uncle no longer than twelve-month; and ten thousand pounds, including the girls’ legacies, was all that remained for his widow and children. On his death-bed he recommended them earnestly to his son’s kindness. Mr. John Dashwood had not the strong feelings of the rest of the family; yet he promised to do everything in his power; and when he considered the matter at leisure, he meditated the liberal present of a thousand pounds a-piece.

He was not an ill-disposed young man, unless to be rather cold-hearted and rather selfish is to be ill-disposed; he conducted himself with propriety, and, had he married a more amiable woman, he might even have been made amiable himself. But Mrs. John Dashwood was a strong caricature of himself;—more narrow-minded and selfish. No sooner was the funeral over than she arrived, with child and attendants, without notice to her mother-in-law. The house was her husband’s; her right could not be disputed; but the indelicacy was great, and to Mrs. Dashwood—whose sense of honour was keen, and generosity romantic—highly unpleasing. She would have quitted the house for ever had not the entreaties of her eldest daughter prevailed.

Elinor, this eldest, at nineteen possessed a strength of understanding, and coolness of judgment, which qualified her to be her mother’s counsellor, and enabled her often to counteract, to advantage, that eagerness of mind in Mrs. Dashwood which must generally have led to imprudence. She had an excellent heart; her feelings were strong; but she knew how to govern them. Marianne’s abilities were, in many respects, quite equal to Elinor’s; she was sensible and clever; but eager in everything—generous, amiable, interesting—everything but prudent. The resemblance between her and her mother was strikingly great. Margaret, thirteen, was good-humoured and well-disposed, but had imbibed much of Marianne’s romance without much of her sense.

Their grief, at first overwhelming, was in the mother and Marianne renewed by choice, and heightened by every painful recollection; they resolved against consolation. Elinor, too, was deeply afflicted; but still she could exert herself—could consult with her brother, receive her sister-in-law with propriety, and strive to rouse her mother to similar forbearance.

## Chapter 2

Mrs. John Dashwood soon installed herself mistress of Norland; the widow and her daughters were reduced to visitors—treated with quiet civility by the lady, and with as much kindness by her husband as he could feel beyond himself, his wife, and their boy. He really pressed them to consider Norland as their home till they could fix elsewhere. But his intention of giving his sisters three thousand pounds a-piece did not at all approve itself to his wife.

“To take three thousand pounds from the fortune of their dear little boy,” she cried, “would be impoverishing him to the most dreadful degree. And what possible claim can the Miss Dashwoods, related to you only by half blood, have on your generosity to so large an amount?”

“It was my father’s last request,” said he, “that I should assist his widow and daughters.”

“He did not know what he was talking of, I dare say. He did not stipulate for any particular sum.”

“He only requested me,” he allowed, “to make their situation more comfortable. The promise was given, and must be performed. Something must be done for them whenever they leave Norland.”

“Well, then, let something be done for them; but that something need not be three thousand pounds. Consider that when the money is once parted with, it never can return. If, indeed, it could be restored to our poor little boy—”

“Why, to be sure,” said he, very gravely, “that would make great difference. Perhaps it would be better for all parties if the sum were diminished one half.—Five hundred pounds would be a prodigious increase to their fortunes!”

“Oh! beyond anything great! What brother on earth would do half so much! And as it is—only half blood!”

He wished not to do anything mean; rather too much than too little; and no one, at least, could think he had not done enough. But even five hundred a-piece began to appear more than prudent.

“They will each have about three thousand pounds on their mother’s death,” he argued—“a very comfortable fortune.”

“To be sure; indeed it strikes me they can want no addition at all. If they marry, they will be sure of doing well; if not, they may all live very comfortably together on the interest of ten thousand pounds.”

He shifted then to an annuity for their mother. “A hundred a year would make them perfectly comfortable.”

Fanny hesitated, then recoiled. “People always live for ever when there is an annuity to be paid them; and she is hardly forty. An annuity is a very serious business; it comes over and over every year, and there is no getting rid of it.”

“It is unpleasant,” he owned, “to have those yearly drains. One’s fortune is not one’s own.”

“Undoubtedly; and after all you have no thanks for it. Whatever is done should be at your own discretion entirely. Do not bind yourself to allow them anything yearly. It may be very inconvenient some years to spare a hundred, or even fifty pounds.”

He believed her right. Occasional presents would be of far greater assistance than a fixed allowance; they would prevent distress without enlarging style. “A present of fifty pounds, now and then, will amply discharge my promise.”

“To say the truth,” said she, “I am convinced your father had no idea of your giving them any money at all. He meant only such assistance as might be reasonably expected: looking out a comfortable small house, helping them to move their things, and sending them presents of fish and game.”

She computed, with cheerful economy, that, besides their own seven thousand, “the thousand pounds belonging to each girl brings them in fifty pounds a year a-piece; altogether, they will have five hundred a-year amongst them; and what on earth can four women want for more than that? They will live so cheap!—no carriage, no horses, hardly any servants, no company, no expenses of any kind!”

“Upon my word,” cried Mr. Dashwood, “I believe you are perfectly right.” The argument was irresistible; and he finally resolved to do no more than those neighbourly acts his wife had pointed out.

## Chapter 3

Months passed at Norland. When Mrs. Dashwood’s spirits revived enough for exertion, she was eager to fix near the place she loved; but Elinor’s prudence rejected many houses as too large for their income. Meanwhile she relied, as her husband had done, on her son’s promise; and his civility to themselves confirmed her belief in his liberality, even as her contempt for her daughter-in-law increased with knowledge.

A circumstance occurred to render their stay still more agreeable to Mrs. Dashwood: a growing attachment between Elinor and Mrs. John Dashwood’s brother. Edward Ferrars, the eldest son of a very rich man, was introduced soon after his sister’s establishment, and spent much of his time with them. Some mothers might have encouraged the intimacy for interest; others have repressed it for prudence, for except a trifling sum the whole of his fortune depended on his mother’s will. Mrs. Dashwood was alike uninfluenced by either: it was enough that he appeared amiable, that he loved her daughter, and that Elinor returned the partiality. That Elinor’s merit should not be acknowledged by everyone who knew her, was to her comprehension impossible.

Edward was not recommended by peculiar graces of person or address; he was not handsome, and his manners required intimacy to make them pleasing. He was too diffident to do justice to himself; but when his natural shyness was overcome, he showed an open, affectionate heart, good understanding, and solid improvement. He had no turn for great men or barouches; all his wishes centred in domestic comfort and the quiet of private life—fortunately he had a younger brother more promising for display.

Marianne, who loved all that was ardent, was less satisfied. “Edward is very amiable,” she allowed, “and I love him tenderly. But yet—he is not the kind of young man—there is something wanting. His eyes want all that spirit, that fire, which at once announce virtue and intelligence. I am afraid, Mama, he has no real taste. He admires Elinor’s drawings very much; but it is the admiration of a lover, not of a connoisseur. To satisfy me, those characters must be united. He must enter into all my feelings; the same books, the same music must charm us both.” His reading of Cowper “with such impenetrable calmness, such dreadful indifference!” almost drove her wild. “Elinor has not my feelings,” she concluded; “it would have broke my heart, had I loved him, to hear him read with so little sensibility. The more I know of the world, the more am I convinced that I shall never see a man whom I can really love. I require so much!”

Mrs. Dashwood, whose doctrines made difference of fortune nothing where dispositions resembled, was secure of Elinor’s happiness. “He is unlike Fanny—enough,” said she: “it implies everything amiable.”

“I have never yet known what it was to separate esteem and love,” she added playfully, when Elinor recommended only to esteem him.

## Chapter 4

“What a pity it is, Elinor,” said Marianne, “that Edward should have no taste for drawing.”

“No taste for drawing! He distrusts his own judgment, but he has an innate propriety and simplicity of taste which, in general, direct him perfectly right,” returned Elinor.

Marianne, unwilling to wound, professed the highest opinion of his goodness and sense; yet the rapture she required for “taste” could not be discovered in him. Elinor, smiling, was content with such commendation. She had seen most of him, and ventured to pronounce his mind well-informed, his enjoyment of books great, his observation just and correct, and his taste delicate and pure; his person hardly handsome till the sweetness of his countenance was perceived.

“I shall very soon think him handsome,” cried Marianne, “if I do not now. When you tell me to love him as a brother, I shall no more see imperfection in his face than I now do in his heart.”

Elinor, startled at her warmth, was sorry for her own. She believed the regard to be mutual; but she required greater certainty. “I do not attempt to deny,” said she, “that I think very highly of him—that I greatly esteem, that I like him—”

“Esteem him! Like him! Cold-hearted Elinor! Oh! worse than cold-hearted! Ashamed of being otherwise,” cried Marianne.

“Believe my feelings to be stronger than I have declared,” Elinor answered, laughing; “but farther than this you must not believe. I am by no means assured of his regard for me. He is very far from being independent. From Fanny’s occasional mention of her mother, we have never been disposed to think her amiable; and I am much mistaken if Edward is not aware there would be many difficulties, were he to wish to marry a woman who had not either a great fortune or high rank.”

Marianne was astonished to find how much imagination had outstripped truth. “And you really are not engaged to him! Yet it certainly soon will happen.” She even hoped delay would “improve that natural taste for your favourite pursuit,” perhaps so far as to make him learn to draw.

Elinor, though she could not consider her partiality as prosperous as Marianne believed, was more disturbed by Fanny’s behaviour than by Edward’s reserve. The first opportunity Mrs. John Dashwood had, she affronted her mother-in-law by expressive talk of her brother’s great expectations, of Mrs. Ferrars’s resolution that both her sons should marry well, and of the danger attending any young woman who attempted to draw him in. Mrs. Dashwood, neither unconscious nor calm, instantly resolved that Elinor should not be exposed another week to such insinuations, however inconvenient the removal.

In this state a letter arrived from a cousin, Sir John Middleton of Barton Park, Devonshire, offering, in the true spirit of friendly accommodation, a small house—Barton Cottage—on very easy terms, and pressing them to come to Barton Park and judge whether, by any alteration, it might be made comfortable. The offer was peculiarly well-timed. To quit the neighbourhood of Norland, once an evil, became a blessing compared with the misery of remaining the guest of such a mistress. Mrs. Dashwood’s resolution was formed as she read; she instantly accepted, and hastened to show both letters to her daughters.

Elinor had always thought it more prudent to settle at some distance than immediately among their present acquaintance; the house was on a simple scale, the rent uncommonly moderate; and though the removal beyond her wishes, she made no attempt to dissuade her mother.

## Chapter 5

Mrs. Dashwood lost no time in announcing to her son and daughter-in-law that she was provided with a house, and should incommode them no longer than till everything were ready. Mr. John Dashwood civilly hoped she would not be settled far; her reply—that she was going into Devonshire—drew from Edward a hasty, feeling exclamation. “Devonshire! Are you, indeed, going there? So far from hence! And to what part of it?” Learning it was within four miles north of Exeter, Mrs. Dashwood added, “It is but a cottage; but I hope to see many of my friends in it.” She invited Mr. and Mrs. John Dashwood, and Edward with still greater affection—wishing, by such marked civility to him, to show how little she regarded his sister’s disapprobation.

Mr. Dashwood repeated, again and again, his sorrow that her house should be so distant as to prevent his being of any service in removing her furniture—the very exertion to which he had, in fact, limited his promise. The furniture was sent by water: household linen, plate, china, and books, with a handsome pianoforte of Marianne’s. Mrs. John Dashwood saw the packages depart with a sigh; she felt it hard that, with so trifling an income, Mrs. Dashwood should have any handsome article.

The cottage was taken for a twelvemonth, ready furnished, and immediate possession offered. Rapid in everything that interested her, Mrs. Dashwood sold the carriage, already parted with the horses, and—at Elinor’s wise urging—limited their servants to three; a man and two maids, one of whom, with the man, was sent forward to prepare the house. As Lady Middleton was unknown to her, she chose to go directly to the cottage, relying undoubtingly on Sir John’s description.

Mr. Dashwood’s discourse now dwelt so often on the increasing expenses of housekeeping and the perpetual demands upon a man of any consequence, that Mrs. Dashwood gave over hope of pecuniary aid beyond their six months at Norland. In a very few weeks from Sir John’s first letter, everything was settled for their journey.

Many tears were shed in their last adieus to a place so much beloved. “Dear, dear Norland!” said Marianne, wandering before the house on the last evening; “when shall I cease to regret you!—when learn to feel a home elsewhere!—Oh! happy house… And you, ye well-known trees!—No leaf will decay because we are removed… you will continue the same; unconscious of the pleasure or the regret you occasion… But who will remain to enjoy you?”

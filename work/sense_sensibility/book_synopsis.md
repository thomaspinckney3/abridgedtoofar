When the amiable but improvident Mr. Dashwood dies at Norland Park, the estate passes—by entail and a dying uncle’s will—to his son John, leaving his widow and three daughters with little but good breeding. Pressed by his cold-hearted wife Fanny, John swiftly retracts any intention of aiding his half-sisters, and Mrs. Dashwood removes Elinor, Marianne, and Margaret from Sussex to the cottage of distant cousins, the Middletons, in Devonshire. At Barton Cottage, Elinor’s quiet reason tempers her mother’s and sister’s romantic impulses, even as she forms a deep, restrained attachment to Fanny’s brother, Edward Ferrars. Marianne, all feeling and music, scorns caution and captivates the neighborhood—and two men: the grave, compassionate Colonel Brandon, and the dashing, impetuous John Willoughby. While Brandon’s past sorrow circles him in reserve, Willoughby’s charisma seems to fulfill Marianne’s every poetic expectation. Elinor watches with concern, sensing the peril of sensibility ungoverned and the precariousness of women’s fates under English inheritance laws and social calculation.

The Barton circle expands with the indefatigable gossip Mrs. Jennings and her good-natured, sporting son-in-law Sir John, and into it sweep Lucy and Anne Steele, pert relations with sharp eyes for advantage. Elinor learns in confidence that Lucy has long been secretly engaged to Edward, a blow she absorbs with quiet fortitude while refusing her mother’s and Marianne’s heroics. Marianne’s romance meanwhile intensifies—shared songs, a lock of hair, a reckless tour of Allenham—until Willoughby abruptly departs for London on urgent “business,” leaving her appetite for rapture unsated. The Dashwood sisters follow Mrs. Jennings to town, where Elinor bears Lucy’s petty triumphs and the Ferrars family’s snobbery with composure. At a party, Marianne confronts Willoughby, who coldly disowns her before revealing, to Elinor later, that he has sold himself to a fortune. When Colonel Brandon uncovers Willoughby’s seduction of his ward Eliza, Marianne’s dream curdles into a hard lesson about charm divorced from character.

Letters and rumors tighten the net: Edward is cast off by his tyrannical mother for refusing to abandon Lucy; Willoughby marries Miss Grey for her thirty thousand pounds; and Marianne, languishing in mortification, is nursed and steadied by Elinor’s steadfast care. At Cleveland, the Palmers’ estate, Marianne’s imprudence culminates in a dangerous fever during a tempestuous night; Elinor sends for their mother as Colonel Brandon gallops through the dark to fetch her, and Willoughby, unexpectedly contrite, rushes to confess his selfishness and remorse to Elinor. Marianne survives, humbled and penitent, vowing to discipline feeling with principle, and at Barton she learns to value Brandon’s quiet constancy over theatrical passion. Edward arrives soon after with astonishing news: Lucy has jilted him and married his vain brother Robert, thus releasing him from a long, foolish engagement. Colonel Brandon, moved by esteem for Edward and Elinor, has already quietly offered Edward the Delaford living, and Mrs. Ferrars—after suitable theatrics—reluctantly restores her son.

Elinor—after months of silent trial—at last weeps for joy, and the couple marry, establishing themselves at the Delaford parsonage, where mutual respect and domestic frugality prove a truer romance than any opera duet. Marianne, no longer a captive of sensibility, discovers happiness in loving Brandon, whose patience, integrity, and history of compassion answer her matured heart; their marriage unites gratitude, esteem, and a more measured tenderness. Mrs. Jennings crows in contentment as her predictions come true; Sir John regains dinner partners; and Mrs. Ferrars transfers her favor, as fashion dictates, to the scheming Lucy, now Mrs. Robert Ferrars. Willoughby, haunted but not destroyed, finds a lesser felicity in horses and society, always measuring women against the wife he forfeited. The Dashwood women remain close—Barton Cottage alive with visits, music, and the quiet triumph of trials endured with dignity. In this balance of “sense” and “sensibility,” Austen suggests that feeling refined by judgment, and judgment warmed by feeling, offers the only durable foundation for love in a world ruled by money, manners, and accident.

Harriet Beecher Stowe’s Uncle Tom’s Cabin follows several enslaved people and the white families around them to expose the moral and spiritual evils of American slavery.  
In Kentucky, debt forces Mr. Shelby to sell the gentle, devout Uncle Tom and plans to sell little Harry, prompting Harry’s mother Eliza to flee north across the frozen Ohio River with her child.  
Eliza is aided by courageous Quakers and eventually reunites with her husband, the inventive George Harris, as the family presses toward freedom in Canada.  
Tom, refusing to run so he will not endanger others, is sent downriver by the trader Haley and on a Mississippi steamer saves angelic little Eva St. Clare, who persuades her father Augustine to buy him.  
In the St. Clare New Orleans household, Tom becomes beloved of Eva, whose pure Christian love softens hard hearts, while brusque New England cousin Miss Ophelia learns to see the humanity in mischievous Topsy.  
Eva’s illness and serene, luminous death awaken conscience in those around her, and St. Clare promises to free Tom, but he is suddenly killed before he can complete the paperwork.  
Tom is then sold to brutal Louisiana planter Simon Legree, whose plantation is a hell of violent coercion, despair, and calculated spiritual degradation.  
There Tom comforts and strengthens other enslaved people, including the despairing Cassy and the young Emmeline, whose lives Legree has crushed.  
Cassy engineers a ghostly ruse in the attic to terrify Legree and uses a swamp stratagem to help herself and Emmeline escape, while Tom steadfastly refuses to reveal their hiding place.  
Enraged, Legree has Tom savagely beaten; Tom forgives his tormentors and dies with unshaken faith, inspiring repentance in the men who harmed him.  
George Shelby, who has searched for his former servant, arrives too late, buries Tom with honor, and swears to oppose slavery in Tom’s name.  
Cassy and Emmeline evade capture, and in Montreal Cassy discovers that Eliza—now safe with George Harris and their son—is the daughter she lost to the trade.  
George Harris, Eliza, and their reunited relatives emigrate to Liberia to pursue education, freedom, and self-governance, while Topsy grows into a devoted Christian teacher in Africa.  
Throughout, Stowe contrasts the self-indulgent Christian hypocrisy that excuses bondage with the active, suffering love—often embodied by Black believers, little Eva, and the Quakers—that points to true religion.  
The novel closes as an explicit abolitionist appeal, insisting that slavery corrupts masters, destroys families, mocks the Gospel, and must be ended by the repentance, justice, and mercy of a roused Christian nation.

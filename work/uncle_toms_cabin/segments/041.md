The Martyr

“Deem not the just by Heaven forgot!
Though life its common gifts deny,—
Though, with a crushed and bleeding heart,
And spurned of man, he goes to die!
For God hath marked each sorrowing day,
And numbered every bitter tear,
And heaven’s long years of bliss shall pay
For all his children suffer here.”

The longest way must have its close; the gloomiest night wears on to a morning. We have walked with our humble friend through ease and indulgence, through heart-breaking separations, through a sunny island where flowers hid chains, and at last through blackness where the unseen firmament has blazed with stars. The morning-star now stands over the mountains; strange gales, not of earth, show that the gates of day are unclosing.

The escape of Cassy and Emmeline, two fugitive women, stung Legree, the plantation master, to fury; and, as was to be expected, it fell on the defenceless head of Tom, his slave. When he told the hands the tidings, he marked a light in Tom’s eye and that he did not join the muster to pursue. Remembering other times when Tom had proved inflexible in deeds of inhumanity, he forbore, in his haste, to grapple with him then.

Tom remained behind with a few who had learned of him to pray, and they prayed for the fugitives’ escape.

Legree’s baffled return gathered his long hatred into deadly form. Had not this man braved him—steadily, resistlessly—ever since he bought him? Was there not a spirit in him which, silent as it was, burned on him like fires of perdition?

“I hate him!” said Legree, that night, sitting up in his bed. “I hate him! And isn’t he MINE? Can’t I do what I like with him?” He clenched his fist, as if rending something.

But Tom was a faithful, valuable hand; and that, though it sharpened hatred, held him a little in. He would, next day, say nothing—yet; gather men with dogs and guns; surround the swamp; hunt systematically. If it failed, he would summon Tom before him; he would break him down—or—there was a dire whisper to which his soul assented.

“Well,” said Cassy, peering from the garret through the knot-hole, “the hunt’s going to begin again, today!”

Horsemen curveted before the house; two leashes of strange dogs strained and bayed. Overseers from neighboring plantations, and rough companions from a tavern-bar, a hard-favored set, drank Legree’s profuse brandy; negroes, detailed for the sport, were plied, that even this might be made a holiday.

Listening, the morning air blowing to her ear, Cassy heard the men divide the ground, discuss dogs, give orders about firing, and what to do in case of capture. She drew back, lifted her clasped hands. “O, great Almighty God! we are all sinners; but what have we done, more than all the rest of the world, that we should be treated so?”

“If it wasn’t for you, child,” she said to Emmeline, “I’d go out to them; and I’d thank any one that would shoot me down; for what use will freedom be to me? Can it give me back my children, or make me what I used to be?”

Emmeline, half afraid of these dark moods, took her hand with a gentle motion.

“Don’t!” said Cassy, trying to draw it away. “You’ll get me to loving you; and I never mean to love anything, again!”

“Poor Cassy! don’t feel so! If the Lord gives us liberty, perhaps he’ll give you back your daughter; at any rate, I’ll be like a daughter to you. I know I’ll never see my poor old mother again! I shall love you, Cassy, whether you love me or not!”

The child-like spirit conquered. Cassy put an arm round her, stroked her soft hair; and Emmeline wondered at the beauty of those magnificent eyes, now soft with tears.

“O, Em!” said Cassy, “I’ve hungered for my children, and thirsted for them! Here! here!”—she struck her breast—“it’s all desolate, all empty! If God would give me back my children, then I could pray.”

“You must trust him, Cassy,” said Emmeline; “he is our Father!”

“His wrath is upon us,” said Cassy. “He has turned away in anger.”

“No, Cassy! He will be good to us! Let us hope in Him,—I always have had hope.”

The hunt was long, animated, thorough, but unsuccessful; and, with grave, ironic exultation, Cassy looked down on Legree as, weary, he alighted from his horse.

“Now, Quimbo,” said Legree, flinging himself down in the sitting-room, “you jest go and walk that Tom up here, right away! The old cuss is at the bottom of this whole matter; and I’ll have it out of his old black hide!”

Sambo and Quimbo, his brutal drivers, hated Tom with a servile envy sharpened by their master’s displeasure, and the errand was welcome. Tom heard with a forewarning heart. He knew the fugitives’ concealment, knew the deadly character of the man and his despotic power; but he felt strong in God to meet death rather than betray the helpless.

He set down his basket, looked up, and said, “Into thy hands I commend my spirit! Thou hast redeemed me, oh Lord God of truth!” Then he quietly yielded to Quimbo’s brutal grasp.

“Ay, ay!” said the giant, dragging him. “Ye’ll cotch it, now! See how ye’ll look, now, helpin’ Mas’r’s niggers to run away!”

Those savage words never reached that ear; a Higher Voice said, “Fear not them that kill the body, and, after that, have no more that they can do.” Nerve and bone vibrated, as if touched by the finger of God. The huts, the rows, the whole scene of his degradation seemed to whirl by like landscape past a rushing car. His soul throbbed,—home in sight,—release at hand.

“Well, Tom!” said Legree, seizing him by the collar, teeth set with determined rage, “do you know I’ve made up my mind to KILL YOU?”

“It’s very likely, Mas’r,” said Tom, calmly.

“I have—done—just—that—thing, Tom, unless you’ll tell me what you know about these yer gals!”

Tom stood silent.

“D’ye hear?” roared Legree, stamping. “Speak!”

“I han’t got nothing to tell, Mas’r,” said Tom, with slow, firm utterance.

“Do you dare to tell me, ye old black Christian, ye don’t know?”

Tom was silent.

“Speak!” thundered Legree, striking him. “Do you know anything?”

“I know, Mas’r; but I can’t tell anything. I can die!”

Legree drew in a long breath; then, holding him by the arm, his face close, said, terribly, “Hark ’e, Tom!—ye think, ’cause I’ve let you off before, I don’t mean what I say; but, this time, I’ve made up my mind, and counted the cost. You’ve always stood it out again’ me: now, I’ll conquer ye, or kill ye!—one or t’ other. I’ll count every drop of blood there is in you, and take ’em, one by one, till ye give up!”

Tom looked up and answered, “Mas’r, if you was sick, or in trouble, or dying, and I could save ye, I’d give ye my heart’s blood; and, if taking every drop of blood in this poor old body would save your precious soul, I’d give ’em freely, as the Lord gave his for me. O, Mas’r! don’t bring this great sin on your soul! It will hurt you more than ’t will me! Do the worst you can, my troubles’ll be over soon; but, if ye don’t repent, yours won’t never end!”

For a moment—like heavenly music in a lull—there was silence; the tick of the old clock measured the last moments of mercy and probation to that hardened heart. One hesitating pause—one relenting thrill—and the evil spirit rushed back seven-fold. Foaming, Legree smote his victim to the ground.

Scenes of blood and cruelty are shocking to our ear and heart. What man has nerve to do, man has not nerve to hear; and yet, O my country! these things are done under the shadow of thy laws! But of old there was One whose suffering changed an instrument of torture and shame into a symbol of glory and immortal life; and, where His spirit is, neither stripes nor blood can make the Christian’s last struggle less than glorious.

Was Tom alone, that long night, while his brave, loving spirit bore up, in an old shed, against buffeting and brutal stripes?

Nay! There stood by him One,—seen by him alone,—“like unto the Son of God.”

The tempter stood by him, too,—furious, despotic will,—every moment pressing him to shun agony by betraying the innocent. But the brave, true heart was firm on the Eternal Rock. Like his Master, he knew that, if he saved others, himself he could not save; nor could utmost extremity wring from him words, save prayers and holy trust.

“He’s most gone, Mas’r,” said Sambo, touched, in spite of himself, by the patience of his victim.

“Pay away, till he gives up! Give it to him!—give it to him!” shouted Legree. “I’ll take every drop of blood he has, unless he confesses!”

Tom opened his eyes and looked on his master. “Ye poor miserable critter!” he said; “there ain’t no more ye can do! I forgive ye, with all my soul!” He fainted away.

“I b’lieve, my soul, he’s done for, finally,” said Legree, stooping to look. “Yes, he is! Well, his mouth’s shut up, at last,—that’s one comfort!”

Yes, Legree; but who shall shut up that voice in thy soul?—that soul, past repentance, past prayer, past hope?

Yet Tom was not quite gone. His wondrous words and pious prayers had struck upon the hearts of the imbruted blacks who had been instruments of cruelty. When Legree withdrew, they took him down, washed his wounds, made a rude bed of refuse cotton, and one, stealing to the house, begged a drink of brandy, as if for himself, and poured it down Tom’s throat.

“O, Tom!” said Quimbo, “we’s been awful wicked to ye!”

“I forgive ye, with all my heart!” said Tom, faintly.

“Sartin, we’s been doin’ a drefful wicked thing!” said Sambo. “Hopes Mas’r’ll have to ’count for it, and not we.”

“O, Tom! do tell us who is Jesus, anyhow?” said Sambo; “Jesus, that’s been a standin’ by you so, all this night!—Who is he?”

The word roused the failing spirit. He poured forth a few energetic sentences of that wondrous One,—his life, his death, his everlasting presence, and power to save.

They wept,—both the two savage men.

“Why didn’t I never hear this before?” said Sambo. “But I do believe!—I can’t help it! Lord Jesus, have mercy on us!”

“Poor critters!” said Tom. “I’d be willing to bar all I have, if it’ll only bring ye to Christ! O, Lord! give me these two more souls, I pray!”

That prayer was answered.

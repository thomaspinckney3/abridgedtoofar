Dark Places

“The dark places of the earth are full of the habitations of cruelty.”

Trailing wearily behind a rude wagon, and over a ruder road, Tom and his associates faced onward.

In the wagon sat Simon Legree; and the two women, still fettered together, were stowed in the back with the baggage. The whole company were seeking Legree’s plantation, which lay a good distance off.

It was a wild, forsaken road, now through dreary pine barrens, where the wind whispered mournfully, and now over log causeways, through long cypress swamps, the doleful trees rising out of slimy, spongy ground, hung with long wreaths of funeral black moss; ever and anon the loathsome moccasin slid among broken stumps and shattered branches rotting in the water. Dreary to the stranger with well-filled pocket and well-appointed horse; drearier to the man enthralled, whom every step bears further from all that man loves and prays for. So one might have thought, that saw the sunken, dejected faces and the wistful, patient weariness with which sad eyes rested on object after object that passed them in their sad journey.

Simon rode on, well pleased, pulling at a flask he kept in his pocket.

“I say, you!” he called back, catching a glance at the dispirited faces. “Strike up a song, boys,—come!”

The men looked at each other. The “come” was repeated with a smart crack of the driver’s whip. Tom began a Methodist hymn.

“Jerusalem, my happy home,
    Name ever dear to me!
When shall my sorrows have an end,
    Thy joys when shall—”

“Shut up, you black cuss!” roared Legree. “Did ye think I wanted any o’ yer infernal old Methodism? I say, tune up, now, something real rowdy,—quick!”

One of the men struck up an unmeaning song, such as are common among the slaves.

“Mas’r see’d me cotch a coon,
    High boys, high!
He laughed to split,—d’ye see the moon,
    Ho! ho! ho! boys, ho!
Ho! yo! hi—e! oh!”

The singer made up the rest to his own pleasure; the party took up the chorus at intervals,

“Ho! ho! ho! boys, ho!
High—e—oh! high—e—oh!”

It was boisterous, with a forced attempt at merriment; yet no wail of despair, no impassioned prayer, could have had such depth of woe as the wild notes of that chorus. As if the poor, dumb heart, threatened and prisoned, took refuge in that inarticulate sanctuary of music, and found there a language in which to breathe its prayer to God! There was a prayer in it which Simon could not hear. He only heard boys singing noisily, and was well pleased; he was making them “keep up their spirits.”

“Well, my little dear,” he said, turning to Emmeline and laying his hand on her shoulder, “we’re almost home!”

When he scolded and stormed, Emmeline was terrified; but when he laid his hand on her, and spoke as he now did, she felt as if she had rather he would strike her. The expression of his eyes made her soul sick, and her flesh creep. Involuntarily she clung closer to the mulatto woman by her side, as if she were her mother.

“You didn’t ever wear ear-rings,” he said, taking hold of her small ear with his coarse fingers.

“No, Mas’r!” said Emmeline, trembling and looking down.

“Well, I’ll give you a pair, when we get home, if you’re a good girl. You needn’t be so frightened; I don’t mean to make you work very hard. You’ll have fine times with me, and live like a lady,—only be a good girl.”

He had been drinking to that degree that he was inclined to be gracious; and now the enclosures of the plantation rose to view. The estate had formerly belonged to a gentleman of opulence and taste, who had bestowed much care on his grounds. Having died insolvent, it had been purchased, at a bargain, by Legree, who used it, as he did everything, merely as an implement for money-making. The place bore that ragged, forlorn look which shows that a former owner’s care has gone to utter decay.

What had been a smooth-shaven lawn was covered with frowsy, tangled grass, with horseposts set here and there, the turf trampled away, and the ground littered with broken pails, cobs of corn, and slovenly remains. A mildewed jessamine or honeysuckle hung raggedly from some ornamental support pushed aside and used as a horse-post. A large garden was grown over with weeds, through which here and there some solitary exotic reared its forsaken head. The conservatory stood with no shades; on mouldering shelves were dry, forsaken flower-pots with sticks in them, whose dried leaves showed they had once been plants. The house had been large and handsome, with a wide two-storied verandah running round it, the lower tier on brick pillars; but now windows were boarded, panes shattered, shutters hanging by a single hinge,—all telling of neglect and discomfort.

Bits of board, straw, old barrels and boxes garnished the ground; and three or four ferocious dogs, roused by the wagon-wheels, came tearing out, and were with difficulty restrained from laying hold of Tom and his companions by the ragged servants who came after them.

“Ye see what ye’d get!” said Legree, caressing the dogs with grim satisfaction, and turning to Tom and the others. “Ye see what ye’d get, if ye try to run off. These yer dogs has been raised to track niggers; and they’d jest as soon chaw one on ye up as eat their supper. So, mind yerself! How now, Sambo!” he said to a ragged fellow officious in his attentions. “How have things been going?”

“Fust rate, Mas’r.”

“Quimbo,” said Legree to another, who made zealous demonstrations, “ye minded what I telled ye?”

“Guess I did, didn’t I?”

These two were the principal hands on the place. Legree had trained them in savageness and brutality as systematically as his bull-dogs, till long practice in hardness had brought their nature to about the same range of capacities. Men say the negro overseer is ever more tyrannical than the white; it is but saying the slave’s mind has been more crushed and debased. This is true of every oppressed race, the world over: the slave is always a tyrant, if he can get a chance.

Legree governed by a resolution of forces. Sambo and Quimbo cordially hated each other; the hands hated them; and by playing one against another, he was sure, through one or the other of the three parties, to be informed of whatever was on foot. He encouraged his two black satellites to a coarse familiarity with him,—a familiarity always liable, on the slightest provocation, to bring down vengeance on one through the other.

As they stood by him now, they were an apt illustration that brutal men are lower even than animals: coarse, dark, heavy features; great eyes rolling enviously on each other; barbarous, guttural, half-brute intonations; dilapidated garments fluttering in the wind,—all in keeping with the vile, unwholesome character of everything about the place.

“Here, you Sambo,” said Legree, “take these yer boys down to the quarters; and here’s a gal I’ve got for you,” he added, separating the mulatto woman from Emmeline, and pushing her towards him; “I promised to bring you one, you know.”

The woman gave a start, drew back, and said, suddenly,

“O, Mas’r! I left my old man in New Orleans.”

“What of that, you—? Won’t you want one here? None o’ your words,—go long!” He raised his whip.

“Come, mistress,” he said to Emmeline; “you go in here with me.”

A dark, wild face glanced for a moment at the window; and, as he opened the door, a female voice spoke, quick and imperative. Tom, looking anxiously after Emmeline, heard Legree answer, angrily,

“You may hold your tongue! I’ll do as I please, for all you!”

Tom heard no more; he was following Sambo to the quarters,—a little street of rude shanties, far from the house, with a forlorn, brutal, forsaken air. Tom’s heart sunk. He had comforted himself with the thought of a cottage, rude indeed, but one he might make neat and quiet, with a shelf for his Bible and a place to be alone out of working hours. He looked into several; they were mere shells, destitute of furniture except a heap of straw, foul with dirt, spread over the bare ground, trodden hard by innumerable feet.

“Which of these will be mine?” he said submissively to Sambo.

“Dunno; ken turn in here, I spose,” said Sambo. “Spects thar’s room for ’nother thar; thar’s a pretty smart heap o’ niggers to each on ’em, now; sure, I dunno what I’s to do with more.”

It was late when the weary occupants came flocking home,—men and women in soiled, tattered garments, surly and uncomfortable, in no mood to look pleasantly on new-comers. The small village was alive with no inviting sounds: hoarse, guttural voices contending at the hand-mills, where their hard corn was yet to be ground into meal, for the cake that was to be their only supper. From earliest dawn they had been in the fields, pressed to work under the driving lash of the overseers; for it was the very heat and hurry of the season, and no means was left untried to press every one to the top of his capabilities. Work, not in itself the hardest, was turned to torment by being pressed hour after hour with unvarying, unrelenting sameness, without even the consciousness of free-will to take from its tediousness.

Tom looked in vain among the gang, as they poured along, for companionable faces. He saw only sullen, scowling, imbruted men, and feeble, discouraged women, or women that were not women,—the strong pushing away the weak,—the gross, unrestricted animal selfishness of those of whom nothing good was expected or desired; treated like brutes, they had sunk as nearly to that level as possible. To a late hour the sound of grinding was protracted; the mills were few, the grinders many; the weary and feeble were driven back by the strong, and came last to their turn.

“Ho yo!” said Sambo to the mulatto woman, throwing down a bag of corn before her; “what a cuss yo name?”

She made no answer.

“Wal, you my woman now. Yo grind dis yer corn, and get my supper baked, ye har?”

“I an’t your woman, and I won’t be!” she said, with the sharp, sudden courage of despair. “You go long!”

“I’ll kick yo, then!” said Sambo, raising his foot.

“Ye may kill me, if ye choose,—the sooner the better! Wish’t I was dead!”

“I say, Sambo, you go to spilin’ the hands, I’ll tell Mas’r o’ you,” said Quimbo, busy at the mill, from which he had viciously driven two or three tired women waiting to grind.

“And I’ll tell him ye won’t let the women come to the mills, yo old nigger!” said Sambo. “Yo jes keep to yo own row.”

Tom was hungry with the day’s journey, almost faint for want of food.

“Thar, yo!” said Quimbo, throwing down a coarse bag that held a peck of corn; “thar, nigger, grab, take car on ’t,—yo won’t get no more, dis yer week.”

Tom waited till a late hour for a place at the mills; then, moved by the utter weariness of two women trying to grind their corn, he ground for them, put together the decaying brands of the fire where many had baked before, and then went about getting his own supper. Small as it was, this deed of charity woke an answering touch in their hearts; an expression of womanly kindness came over their hard faces; they mixed his cake for him, and tended its baking. Tom sat by the firelight and drew out his Bible,—for he had need of comfort.

“What’s that?” said one of the women.

“A Bible,” said Tom.

“Good Lord! han’t seen un since I was in Kentuck.”

“Was you raised in Kentuck?” said Tom, with interest.

“Yes, and well raised, too; never ’spected to come to dis yer!” she sighed.

“What’s dat ar book, any way?” said the other.

“Why, the Bible.”

“Laws a me! what’s dat?” said the woman.

“Do tell! you never hearn on ’t?” said the first. “I used to har Missis a readin’ on ’t, sometimes, in Kentuck; but, laws o’ me! we don’t har nothin’ here but crackin’ and swarin’.”

“Read a piece, anyways!” said the first, curiously, seeing Tom attentively poring over it.

Tom read,—“Come unto Me, all ye that labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest.”

“Them’s good words, enough,” said the woman; “who says ’em?”

“The Lord,” said Tom.

“I jest wish I know’d whar to find Him,” said she. “I would go; ’pears like I never should get rested again. My flesh is fairly sore, and I tremble all over, every day, and Sambo’s allers a jawin’ at me, ’cause I doesn’t pick faster; and nights it’s most midnight ’fore I can get my supper; and den ’pears like I don’t turn over and shut my eyes, ’fore I hear de horn blow to get up, and at it agin in de mornin’. If I knew whar de Lor was, I’d tell him.”

“He’s here, he’s everywhere,” said Tom.

“Lor, you an’t gwine to make me believe dat ar! I know de Lord an’t here,” said the woman. “‘Tan’t no use talking, though. I’s jest gwine to camp down, and sleep while I ken.”

The women went off to their cabins, and Tom sat alone by the smouldering fire that flickered up redly in his face. The silver, fair-browed moon rose in the purple sky, and looked down, calm and silent, as God looks on the scene of misery and oppression,—looked calmly on the lone black man, as he sat, with his arms folded, and his Bible on his knee.

“Is God HERE?” Ah, how is it possible for the untaught heart to keep its faith, unswerving, in the face of dire misrule and palpable, unrebuked injustice? In that simple heart waged a fierce conflict; the crushing sense of wrong; the foreshadowing of a whole life of future misery; the wreck of all past hopes, like dead corpses of wife, and child, and friend, mournfully tossing before the soul, as the half-drowned mariner sees the dead rise from the dark wave! Ah, was it easy here to believe and hold fast the great password of Christian faith, that “God IS, and is the REWARDER of them that diligently seek Him”?

Tom rose, disconsolate, and stumbled into the cabin allotted to him. The floor was strewn with weary sleepers; the foul air almost repelled him; but the heavy night-dews were chill, his limbs weary; wrapping about him a tattered blanket, his only bed-clothing, he stretched himself in the straw and fell asleep.

In dreams, a gentle voice came over his ear. He was sitting on the mossy seat in the garden by Lake Pontchartrain; and Eva, with her serious eyes bent downward, was reading to him from the Bible; and he heard her read.

“When thou passest through the waters, I will be with thee, and the rivers they shall not overflow thee; when thou walkest through the fire, thou shalt not be burned, neither shall the flame kindle upon thee; for I am the Lord thy God, the Holy One of Israel, thy Saviour.”

Gradually the words seemed to melt and fade, as in a divine music; the child raised her deep eyes, and fixed them lovingly on him, and rays of warmth and comfort seemed to go from them to his heart; and, as if wafted on the music, she rose on shining wings, from which flakes and spangles of gold fell off like stars, and she was gone.

Tom woke. Was it a dream? Let it pass for one. But who shall say that that sweet young spirit, which in life so yearned to comfort the distressed, was forbidden of God to assume this ministry after death?

It is a beautiful belief,
    That ever round our head
Are hovering, on angel wings,
    The spirits of the dead.

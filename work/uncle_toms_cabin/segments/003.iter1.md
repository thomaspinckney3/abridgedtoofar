The Mother

Eliza had been brought up by her mistress from girlhood—petted, indulged, neatly trained; she embodied that refinement, that softness of voice and manner often remarked in quadroon women, joined, in her case, to dazzling beauty and a prepossessing address. Eliza, such as we have described her, is not a fancy sketch, but taken from remembrance, as we saw her, years ago, in Kentucky. Safe under Mrs. Shelby’s protection, her beauty had not been made a fatal inheritance.

She had been married to George Harris, a bright and talented young mulatto, hired to a bagging factory, where, by adroitness and ingenuity, he became “the first hand,” and invented a hemp-cleaning machine—quite as much mechanical genius, considering his circumstances, as Whitney’s gin. Handsome and pleasing, a general favorite, he was nevertheless, in the eye of the law, not a man, but a thing—subject to a vulgar, narrow-minded, tyrannical master. Hearing of George’s invention, this gentleman rode over to see what “this intelligent chattel had been about,” and was waited upon over the factory, shown the machinery by George, who, in high spirits, talked so fluently, held himself so erect, looked so handsome and manly, that his master began to feel an uneasy consciousness of inferiority. “What business had his slave to be marching round the country, inventing machines, and holding up his head among gentlemen? He’d soon put a stop to it—take him back, put him to hoeing and digging, and see if he’d step about so smart.” Accordingly, he demanded George’s wages, and announced his intention of taking him home. The manufacturer remonstrated; he would increase compensation. “No object at all, sir. I don’t need to hire any of my hands out, unless I’ve a mind to.” “But only think of his inventing this machine,” interposed a workman. “O yes!—a machine for saving work, is it? Let a nigger alone for that. They are all labor-saving machines themselves.”

George had stood like one transfixed, at hearing his doom thus suddenly pronounced by a power that he knew was irresistible. He folded his arms, tightly pressed in his lips, but a whole volcano of bitter feelings burned in his bosom, and sent streams of fire through his veins. He breathed short, and his large dark eyes flashed like live coals; and he might have broken out into some dangerous ebullition, had not the kindly manufacturer touched him on the arm, and said, in a low tone, “Give way, George; go with him for the present. We’ll try to help you, yet.” The tyrant observed the whisper, and conjectured its import, though he could not hear what was said; and he inwardly strengthened himself in his determination to keep the power he possessed over his victim.

George was taken home, and put to the meanest drudgery of the farm. He had been able to repress every disrespectful word; but the flashing eye, the gloomy and troubled brow, were part of a natural language that could not be repressed,—indubitable signs, which showed too plainly that the man could not become a thing.

It was during the happy period of his employment in the factory that George had seen and married his wife. Much trusted and favored by his employer, he had liberty to come and go; the marriage was highly approved by Mrs. Shelby, who, with a little womanly complacency in match-making, united her handsome favorite with one of her own class; and they were married in her mistress’ great parlor, the beautiful hair adorned with orange-blossoms, the bridal veil thrown over the fair head. For a year or two Eliza saw her husband frequently, and there was nothing to interrupt their happiness, except the loss of two infant children, to whom she was passionately attached, and whom she mourned with a grief so intense as to call for gentle remonstrance from her mistress. After the birth of little Harry, she gradually became tranquillized and settled; every bleeding tie and throbbing nerve entwined once more with that little life; and Eliza was a happy woman up to the time that her husband was rudely torn from his kind employer, and brought under the iron sway of his legal owner.

True to his word, the manufacturer visited Mr. Harris after George had been taken away, and tried every inducement to restore him to his former employment. The master, dogged, cut him short: “It’s a free country, sir; the man’s mine, and I do what I please with him,—that’s it!” And so fell George’s last hope;—nothing before him but a life of toil and drudgery, sharpened by every smarting vexation and indignity which tyrannical ingenuity could devise.

A very humane jurist once said, The worst use you can put a man to is to hang him. No; there is another use that a man can be put to that is WORSE!

In Which Property Gets into an Improper State of Mind

It was late in a drizzly afternoon when a traveler alighted at the door of a small country hotel, in the village of N——, in Kentucky. Within, a miscellany of storm-drifted company lounged about the barroom; rifles stood in the corner; shot-pouches and game-bags lay with hunting-dogs and little negroes rolled together in the angles. At each end of the wide fireplace a long-legged gentleman tipped back in his chair, hat on head, muddy heels enthroned upon the mantel, in that posture of meditation peculiar to western taverns.

Mine host, great of stature and good nature, with a shock of hair and a great hat above that, presided behind the bar. In truth, every man in the place bore on his head this emblem of sovereignty—felt hats rakishly tipped for your jolly fellows; jammed down for your hard characters; set far back for the wide-awake; and shaken about by the careless—quite a Shakespearean study. Divers negroes, in very free-and-easy pantaloons and scant of shirt, scuttled to and fro, producing less result than zealous stir, while a rollicking fire went rejoicingly up the great chimney, the outer door and every window set open to a raw damp air, the calico curtain flapping.

Your Kentuckian, descendant of mighty hunters, keeps his house as camp; wears his hat, tumbles himself about, puts heels on mantels as his fathers on logs; keeps doors and windows open winter and summer, calls everybody "stranger," and is the frankest, easiest, most jovial creature living.

Into such company our traveller came—a short, thick-set man, carefully dressed, round and good-natured of countenance, but fussy and particular in his ways. He brought in valise and umbrella with his own hands, repulsed officious offers, nestled both under the chair nearest the fire, and sat down, looking apprehensively at the gentleman whose artillery of tobacco-juice played right and left.

"I say, stranger, how are ye?" said that worthy, firing an honorary salute.

"Well, I reckon," returned the little man, dodging the threatening honor.

"Any news?"

"Not that I know of."

"Chaw?" said the first speaker, offering a brotherly slice of tobacco.

"No, thank ye—it don't agree with me," said the new-comer, edging off.

The long-sided brother good-naturedly slewed his battery to bear on a fire-iron, and proceeded to storm it with zeal sufficient to take a city. The old gentleman started at every discharge; but his attention was soon drawn to a group around a large handbill.

"Nigger advertised!" said one.

Mr. Wilson—for such was the old gentleman's name—erected his spectacles, and read, slowly, as if studying:

"Ran away from the subscriber, my mulatto boy, George. Said George six feet in height, a very light mulatto, brown curly hair; is very intelligent, speaks handsomely, can read and write, will probably try to pass for a white man, is deeply scarred on his back and shoulders, has been branded in his right hand with the letter H.
    "I will give four hundred dollars for him alive, and the same sum
    for satisfactory proof that he has been _killed."

The long-legged veteran took down his length, strode up, and, with deliberation, discharged a full mouthful on the paper.

"There's my mind upon that!" said he, and sat down again.

"Why, now, stranger, what's that for?" asked mine host.

"I'd do it all the same to the writer o' that ar' paper, if he was here," said the long man, resuming his tobacco. "Any man that owns a boy like that, and can't find no better way o' treatin' on him, _deserves_ to lose him. Such papers is a shame to Kentucky; that's my mind right out, if anybody wants to know!"

"Well, now, that's a fact," said the landlord, entering it in his book as if it were a charge.

"I've got a gang o' boys," continued the long man, "and I jest tells 'em—'Boys,' says I,—'_run_ now! dig! put! jest when ye want to! I never shall come to look arter ye!' That's the way I keeps mine. Let 'em know they're free to run any time, and it jest breaks up their wantin' to. More 'n all, I've got free papers recorded for 'em, in case I gets keeled up; and I tell ye, stranger, there an't a fellow in our parts gets more out o' his niggers than I do. Treat 'em like dogs, and you'll have dogs' works and dogs' actions. Treat 'em like men, and you'll have men's works." And, in warmth, he fired a perfect feu de joie at the fireplace.

"I think you're altogether right, friend," said Mr. Wilson. "And this boy described here _is_ a fine fellow—no mistake. He worked for me half-a-dozen years in my bagging-factory—my best hand. He invented a machine for cleaning hemp; a valuable affair; gone into use in several factories. His master holds the patent."

"I'll warrant ye," said the drover, "holds it, makes money out on 't, and then turns round and brands the boy's right hand! If I had a fair chance, I'd mark _him_ so he'd carry it one while."

"These yer knowin' boys is allers aggravatin' and sarcy," said a coarse fellow, entrenched in his own obtuseness; "that's why they gets cut up and marked. If they behaved, they wouldn't."

"That is to say," returned the drover, dryly, "the Lord made 'em men, and it's a hard squeeze gettin' 'em down into beasts."

"Bright niggers isn't no kind o' 'vantage," persisted the other; "what's the use o' talents if you can't get the use on 'em? All they make on 't is to get round ye. I jest sold 'em down river. I knew I'd got to lose 'em, first or last."

"Better send orders up to the Lord to make ye a set, and leave out their souls entirely," said the drover.

Here a small one-horse buggy drew up. A tall, gentlemanly man, dark and Spanish-looking, with glossy black curls, aquiline nose, straight thin lips, and admirable contour, entered, a colored servant driving. He sauntered to the bar with hat in hand, gave his name—Henry Butler, Oaklands, Shelby County—then turned, with indifference, to read the handbill.

"Jim," he said to his man, "seems to me we met a boy something like this, up at Beman's, didn't we?"

"Yes, Mas'r," said the servant, "only I an't sure 'bout the hand."

"Well, I didn't look, of course," said the stranger, yawning; and then requested a private apartment.

The landlord was all obsequious. A covey of negroes, old and young, bustled and tumbled over each other to get Mas'r's room ready; the new-comer seated himself easily, and conversed with the man next him.

Mr. Wilson had from the first regarded the stranger with disturbed curiosity. He had seen him somewhere. When the man spoke, or smiled, he started, fixed his eyes, and withdrew them as the bright black eyes met his with unconcern. At last a flash of recollection sent alarm through his good old face; the stranger rose and came to him.

"Mr. Wilson, I think," said he, extending his hand. "I beg your pardon—I didn't recollect you before. I see you remember me,—Mr. Butler, of Oaklands, Shelby County."

"Ye—yes—yes, sir," said Mr. Wilson, like one in a dream.

A negro boy announced that Mas'r's room was ready.

"Jim, see to the trunks," said the gentleman, negligently. Then to Mr. Wilson: "I should like a few moments' business in my room, if you please."

Mr. Wilson followed as a sleep-walker. In the large upper chamber, when the servants had done and gone, the young man locked the door, put the key in his pocket, folded his arms, and faced him.

"George!" said Mr. Wilson.

"Yes—George," said the young man, smiling.

"I couldn't have thought it!"

"I am pretty well disguised, I fancy. A little walnut-bark for my yellow skin, dyed hair, and—behold—no answer to the advertisement."

"O, George! this is a dangerous game. I could not have advised you."

"I can do it on my own responsibility," returned George, with the same proud smile.

He was, by his father's side, of white descent; from one of Kentucky's proudest families he had inherited fine European features and a high, indomitable spirit. From his mother, one of those unfortunates marked by beauty to be the slave of passion, he had received only a slight mulatto tinge, redeemed by a rich dark eye. Grace of movement and gentlemanly manners sat naturally upon him; a change of tint and hair had metamorphosed him into the Spanish-looking fellow who now played boldly the gentleman travelling with his domestic.

Mr. Wilson, good-natured, fidgety, cautious, shuffled up and down, much "tumbled up and down in his mind," divided between helping George and maintaining law and order.

"Well, George, I s'pose you're running away—leaving your lawful master—(I don't wonder)—but I'm sorry, George—decidedly—I think I must say that—it's my duty."

"Why are you sorry, sir?" said George, calmly.

"Why, to see you setting yourself in opposition to the laws of your country."

"_My_ country!" said George, with strong, bitter emphasis. "What country have I but the grave,—and I wish to God I were laid there!"

"Why, no—no—this way of talking is wicked—unscriptural. You've got a hard master—reprehensible, I can't defend him. But you know the angel bade Hagar return and submit; and the apostle sent back Onesimus to his master."

"Don't quote Bible at me that way, Mr. Wilson," said George, his eye flashing. "Don't! My wife is a Christian, and I mean to be, if ever I get where I can; but to quote Bible to a fellow in my circumstances is enough to make him give it up. I appeal to God Almighty. I'm willing to go with the case to Him, and ask if I do wrong to seek my freedom."

"These feelings are natural," said the good man, blowing his nose. "Yes; but it is my duty not to encourage 'em. The apostle says, 'Let every one abide in the condition wherein he is called.' We must submit to Providence, George,—don't you see?"

George drew his head back, folded his arms over his broad breast, a bitter smile on his lips.

"I wonder, Mr. Wilson, if the Indians should come and take you a prisoner from your wife and children, and keep you all your life hoeing corn for them, if you'd think it your duty to abide in the condition wherein you were called. I rather think the first stray horse would look like an indication of Providence—shouldn't you?"

The little old gentleman stared with both eyes; and, having the sense of saying nothing where nothing could be said, stroked his umbrella and patted its creases, and exhorted in a general way.

"You see, George, I've always stood your friend; and whatever I've said, I've said for your good. You're running an awful risk. You can't hope to carry it out. If you're taken, it will be worse than ever; they'll abuse you, half kill you, and sell you down river."

"I know all this," said George. "I _do_ run a risk; but—" He threw open his overcoat, and showed two pistols and a bowie-knife. "There! I'm ready for 'em! Down south I never _will_ go. No! if it comes to that, I can earn myself at least six feet of free soil,—the first and last I shall ever own in Kentucky!"

"Why, George, this state of mind is awful; really desperate. Going to break the laws of your country!"

"My country, again! You have a country; but what country have _I_, or any one like me, born of slave mothers? What laws are there for us? We don't make them,—we don't consent to them; all they do is to crush us, and keep us down. Haven't I heard your Fourth-of-July speeches? Don't you tell us, once a year, that governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed? Can't a fellow _think_ that hears such things? Can't he put this and that together, and see what it comes to?"

Mr. Wilson's mind might be fairly represented by a bale of cotton—downy, soft, benevolently fuzzy and confused. He pitied George with all his heart, had a dim perception of the feeling that agitated him; but deemed it duty to go on talking _good_ to him, with infinite pertinacity.

"George, this is bad. As a friend, you'd better not meddle with such notions; bad for boys in your condition,—very," and he sat and nervously chewed the umbrella-handle.

"See here, now," said George, drawing a chair resolutely before him. "Look at me. Don't I sit here every way just as much a man as you are? Look at my face—my hands—my body." He drew himself up proudly. "Why am I _not_ a man, as much as anybody? Hear me. I had a father—one of your Kentucky gentlemen—who didn't think enough of me to keep me from being sold with his dogs and horses to satisfy the estate when he died. I saw my mother put up at sheriff's sale with her seven children; they were sold before her eyes, one by one, all to different masters; I was the youngest. She kneeled to old Mas'r to buy her with me, that she might have one child; he kicked her away with his heavy boot. I saw him do it; and the last I heard was her moans and screams, when I was tied to his horse's neck to be carried off.

"Well, then? My master traded with a man and bought my oldest sister. She was pious—a member of the Baptist church—and handsome as my poor mother had been. I was glad at first, for I had one friend near me. I was soon sorry. Sir, I have stood at the door and heard her whipped, when every blow cut into my naked heart, and I couldn't help her; and she was whipped, sir, for wanting to live a decent Christian life, such as your laws give no slave girl a right to live; and at last I saw her chained with a trader's gang, to be sent to Orleans—sent for nothing else but that—and that's the last I know of her.

"I grew up—years and years—no father, no mother, no sister, not a living soul that cared for me more than a dog; nothing but whipping, scolding, starving. I've been so hungry I was glad to take the bones thrown to the dogs; and yet, when I was a little fellow, and lay awake whole nights and cried, it wasn't hunger nor whipping I cried for. No, sir—it was for _my mother_ and _my sisters_; because I hadn't a friend to love me on earth. I never knew peace or comfort. I never had a kind word till I came to your factory. You treated me well; encouraged me to learn to read and write, to make something of myself; and God knows how grateful I am. Then I found my wife; you've seen her—you know how beautiful she is. When I found she loved me, when I married her, I scarcely believed I was alive, I was so happy; and she is as good as she is beautiful. But now what? My master takes me from my work, my friends, all I like, and grinds me into the dirt! Why? Because, he says, I forgot who I was; he says, to teach me that I am only a nigger! After all, and last of all, he comes between me and my wife, and says I shall give her up, and live with another woman. And all this your laws give him power to do, in spite of God or man.

"Look at it! There isn't _one_ of all these things, that have broken the hearts of my mother, my sister, my wife, and myself, but your laws allow, and give every man power to do, in Kentucky, and none can say nay! Do you call these the laws of _my_ country? Sir, I haven't any country, any more than I have any father. But I'm going to have one. I don't want anything of _your_ country, except to be let alone—to go peaceably out of it; and when I get to Canada, where the laws will own me and protect me, _that_ shall be my country, and its laws I will obey. But if any man tries to stop me, let him take care; for I am desperate. I'll fight for my liberty to the last breath. Your fathers did it; if it was right for them, it is right for me!"

This, delivered partly seated, partly walking with tears, flashing eyes, and despairing gesture, was too much for the good old soul, who mopped his face with a great yellow silk handkerchief.

"Blast 'em all!" he suddenly broke out. "Haven't I always said so—the infernal old cusses! I hope I an't swearing. Well! go ahead, George, go ahead; but be careful; don't shoot anybody, unless—well—you'd _better_ not shoot, I reckon; at least, I wouldn't _hit_ anybody, you know. Where is your wife?"

"Gone—gone, with her child in her arms; gone after the north star; the Lord only knows where; and whether we ever meet on earth, no creature can tell."

"Astonishing! from such a kind family?"

"Kind families get in debt," said George, bitterly, "and the laws of _our_ country allow them to sell the child out of its mother's bosom to pay its master's debts."

"Well, well," said the honest old man, fumbling. "I s'pose I an't followin' my judgment—hang it, I _won't_ follow my judgment! Here, George." He drew out a roll of bills. "Take 'em."

"No, my kind, good sir! You have done much for me, and this might bring you into trouble. I have money enough to take me as far as I need."

"No; but you must. Money's a great help everywhere; can't have too much, if you get it honestly. Take it,—_do_ take it,—do, my boy!"

"On condition I may repay it," said George, taking it.

"And how long are you going to travel so?" said Mr. Wilson. "Not long, I hope. It's well carried on, but too bold. And this black fellow—who is he?"

"A true fellow, who went to Canada a year ago. He heard, after, that his master, in anger, whipped his poor old mother; and he has come all the way back to comfort her, and get a chance to carry her off."

"Has he got her?"

"Not yet. He has hung about, and found no chance. He goes with me as far as Ohio, to put me among friends that helped him, and then comes back."

"Dangerous, very dangerous!" said the old man.

George drew himself up, and smiled disdainfully.

"Something has brought you out wonderfully," said Mr. Wilson, eyeing him with innocent wonder. "You hold up your head, and speak and move like another man."

"Because I'm a _freeman_!" said George, proudly. "Yes, sir; I've said Mas'r for the last time to any man. _I'm free!_"

"Take care! You are not sure—you may be taken."

"All men are free and equal _in the grave_, if it comes to that," said George.

"I'm dumb-founded with your boldness! To come right here to the nearest tavern!"

"It is _so_ bold, and this tavern _so_ near, that they will never think of it. They will look for me on ahead; and you yourself wouldn't know me. Jim's master don't live in this county; he isn't known here. He's given up; nobody's looking after him; and nobody will take me up from the advertisement, I think."

"But the mark in your hand?"

George drew off his glove, and showed a newly-healed scar.

"That is a parting proof of Mr. Harris' regard," he said, scornfully. "A fortnight ago, he took it into his head to give it to me, because he believed I should try to get away. Looks interesting, doesn't it?" He drew on his glove.

"I declare, my very blood runs cold when I think of it—your condition and your risks!" said Mr. Wilson.

"Mine has run cold many years; at present it's about up to the boiling point," said George.

"Well, my good sir," he added after a pause, "I saw you knew me; I thought I'd have this talk, lest your surprise should betray me. I leave early to-morrow, before daylight; to-morrow night I hope to sleep safe in Ohio. I shall travel by daylight, stop at the best hotels, go to dinner with the lords of the land. So, good-by, sir; if you hear I'm taken, you may know I'm dead!"

He stood like a rock, and put out his hand with the air of a prince. The friendly little old man shook it heartily; after a shower of caution, took his umbrella, and fumbled out.

George looked after him; a thought flashed; he opened the door.

"Mr. Wilson—one word more."

The old gentleman reëntered; George locked the door, and stood looking down, irresolute. At last, with a sudden effort—

"Mr. Wilson, you have shown yourself a Christian to me; I want one last deed of Christian kindness."

"Well, George."

"What you said was true. I _am_ running a dreadful risk. There isn't, on earth, a living soul to care if I die,"—he drew his breath hard,—"I shall be kicked out and buried like a dog, and nobody'll think of it a day after,—_only my poor wife!_ Poor soul! she'll mourn; and if you'd contrive to send this little pin to her." He took it out. "She gave it to me for a Christmas present, poor child! Give it to her, and tell her I loved her to the last. Will you? _Will_ you?"

"Yes, certainly—poor fellow!" said the old gentleman, with watery eyes.

"Tell her one thing," said George. "It's my last wish: if she _can_ get to Canada, to go there. No matter how kind her mistress is, no matter how she loves her home; beg her not to go back,—for slavery always ends in misery. Tell her to bring up our boy a free man, and he won't suffer as I have. Tell her this—will you?"

"Yes, George. I'll tell her; but I trust you won't die; take heart,—you're a brave fellow. Trust in the Lord. I wish in my heart you were safe through,—that's what I do."

"_Is_ there a God to trust in?" said George, in a tone of bitter despair that arrested speech. "O, I've seen things all my life that make me feel there can't be a God. You Christians don't know how these things look to us. There's a God for you; but is there any for us?"

"O, now, don't—don't, my boy!" said the old man, almost sobbing. "There is—there is; clouds and darkness are round about him, but righteousness and judgment are the habitation of his throne. There's a _God_, George—believe it; trust in Him, and I'm sure He'll help you. Everything will be set right,—if not in this life, in another."

The simple piety and benevolence of the old man clothed him, for the moment, with dignity. George stopped his distracted walk, stood a moment thoughtfully, and then said, quietly,

"Thank you for saying that, my good friend; I'll _think of that_."

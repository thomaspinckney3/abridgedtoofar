George Shelby had written home merely a line, naming the day he might be expected. Of the death-scene of his old friend he had not the heart to write. He tried, choked, tore up the sheet, wiped his eyes, and fled to be alone. Meanwhile there was a pleased bustle through the Shelby mansion in expectation of young Mas’r George. Mrs. Shelby sat in her comfortable parlor, a cheerful hickory fire dispelling the late autumn chill; a supper-table, glittering with plate and cut glass, was set out, over which Aunt Chloe, Tom’s wife, presided.

Arrayed in a new calico, with clean white apron and high, well-starched turban, her black face glowing, she lingered with needless punctiliousness about the table.

“Laws, now! won’t it look natural to him?” she said. “Thar,—I set his plate just whar he likes it, round by the fire. Mas’r George allers wants de warm seat. O, go way!—why didn’t Sally get out de best tea-pot,—de little new one, Mas’r George got for Missis, Christmas? I’ll have it out! And Missis has heard from Mas’r George?”

“Yes, Chloe; but only a line, just to say he would be home to-night, if he could,—that’s all.”

“Didn’t say nothin’ ’bout my old man, s’pose?” she went on, fidgeting with the tea-cups.

“No. He said he would tell all when he got home.”

“Jes like Mas’r George,—he’s allers so ferce for tellin’ everything hisself,” said Chloe. “I’s got jist de very pattern my old man liked so much, a bakin’; jist sich as I gin him de mornin’ he was took off.—Lord bless us!”

The rattling of wheels was heard.

“Mas’r George!” cried Aunt Chloe, starting to the window.

Mrs. Shelby ran to the door, and was folded in her son’s arms. Aunt Chloe strained her eyes into the darkness.

“O, poor Aunt Chloe!” said George, stopping compassionately, and taking her hard, black hand between both his; “I’d have given all my fortune to have brought him with me, but he’s gone to a better country.”

There was a passionate exclamation from Mrs. Shelby; Aunt Chloe said nothing.

In the supper-room the money, of which Chloe was so proud, still lay on the table. “Thar,” said she, gathering it up with a trembling hand, and holding it to her mistress, “don’t never want to see nor hear on ’t again. Jist as I knew ’t would be,—sold, and murdered on dem ar’ old plantations!” She turned to go. Mrs. Shelby followed softly, drew her down, and sat beside her.

“My poor, good Chloe!” said she.

Chloe leaned her head on her mistress’ shoulder, and sobbed, “O Missis! ’scuse me, my heart’s broke,—dat’s all!”

“I know it is,” said Mrs. Shelby, as her tears fell fast; “and I cannot heal it, but Jesus can. ‘He healeth the broken-hearted, and bindeth up their wounds.’”

They sat long, all weeping together; and George, sitting down beside the mourner, took her hand, and with simple pathos repeated the triumphant scene of her husband’s death, and his last messages of love.

About a month after, one morning, all the servants on the estate were convened in the great hall to hear a few words from their young master. To the surprise of all, he appeared with a bundle of papers, and read successively a certificate of freedom to every one on the place, presenting each, amid sobs, tears, and shouts.

Many pressed around, earnestly begging not to be sent away, tendering back their free papers.

“We don’t want to be no freer than we are. We’s allers had all we wanted. We don’t want to leave de ole place, and Mas’r and Missis, and de rest!”

“My good friends,” said George, when he could be heard, “there’ll be no need for you to leave me. The place wants as many hands to work it as it did before. We need the same about the house that we did before. But you are now free men and free women. I shall pay you wages for your work, such as we shall agree on. The advantage is, that in case of my getting in debt, or dying,—things that might happen,—you cannot now be taken up and sold. I expect to carry on the estate, and to teach you what, perhaps, it will take you some time to learn,—how to use the rights I give you as free men and women. I expect you to be good, and willing to learn; and I trust in God that I shall be faithful, and willing to teach. And now, my friends, look up, and thank God for the blessing of freedom.”

An aged, patriarchal negro, gray and blind, now rose, and, lifting his trembling hand, said, “Let us give thanks unto the Lord!” All kneeled by one consent; a more touching and hearty _Te Deum_ never ascended to heaven.

On rising, another struck up a Methodist hymn, of which the burden was,

“The year of Jubilee is come,—
Return, ye ransomed sinners, home.”

“One thing more,” said George, staying the congratulations; “you all remember our good old Uncle Tom?”

He gave a short narration of the scene of his death, and of his loving farewell to all on the place, and added, “It was on his grave, my friends, that I resolved, before God, that I would never own another slave, while it was possible to free him; that nobody, through me, should ever run the risk of being parted from home and friends, and dying on a lonely plantation, as he died. So, when you rejoice in your freedom, think that you owe it to that good old soul, and pay it back in kindness to his wife and children. Think of your freedom, every time you see UNCLE TOM’S CABIN; and let it be a memorial to put you all in mind to follow in his steps, and be honest and faithful and Christian as he was.”

In Which It Appears That a Senator Is But a Man

The light of the cheerful fire shone on the rug and carpet of a cosey parlor, and glittered on the tea-cups and well-brightened tea-pot, as Senator Bird drew off his boots to put on a pair of new handsome slippers his wife had been working for him while he was away. Mrs. Bird, the very picture of delight, superintended the table and, ever and anon, admonished a set of frolicsome juveniles who effervesced in untold gambol and mischief.

“Tom, let the door-knob alone,—there’s a man! Mary! Mary! don’t pull the cat’s tail,—poor pussy! Jim, you mustn’t climb on that table,—no, no!—You don’t know, my dear, what a surprise it is to us all, to see you here tonight!” she cried at last, finding space to speak to her husband.

“Yes, yes, I thought I’d just make a run down, spend the night, and have a little comfort at home. I’m tired to death, and my head aches!”

Mrs. Bird cast a glance at the camphor-bottle in the half-open closet, but he interposed.

“No, no, Mary, no doctoring! A cup of your good hot tea, and some of our good home living, is what I want. It’s a tiresome business, this legislating!”

He smiled, as if he rather liked to consider himself a sacrifice to his country.

“Well,” said his wife, when the tea-table business grew slack, “and what have they been doing in the Senate?”

Mr. Bird opened his eyes in surprise, for it was unusual that gentle little Mrs. Bird should trouble her head with state matters. “Not very much of importance.”

“Well; but is it true they have been passing a law forbidding people to give meat and drink to those poor colored folks that come along? I heard they were talking of some such law, but I didn’t think any Christian legislature would pass it!”

“Why, Mary, you are getting to be a politician, all at once.”

“No, nonsense! I wouldn’t give a fig for all your politics, generally; but I think this is something downright cruel and unchristian. I hope, my dear, no such law has been passed.”

“There has been a law passed forbidding people to help off the slaves that come over from Kentucky, my dear; so much of that thing has been done by these reckless Abolitionists, that our brethren in Kentucky are very strongly excited, and it seems necessary, and no more than Christian and kind, that something should be done by our state to quiet the excitement.”

“And what is the law? It don’t forbid us to shelter those poor creatures a night, does it, and to give ’em something comfortable to eat, and a few old clothes, and send them quietly about their business?”

“Why, yes, my dear; that would be aiding and abetting, you know.”

Mrs. Bird was a timid, blushing little woman, with mild blue eyes and the gentlest voice in the world; as for courage, a cock-turkey could put her to rout at the first gobble, and a stout house-dog would bring her into subjection by the mere show of his teeth. Yet there was one thing that could rouse her,—anything in the shape of cruelty. Then the general softness of her nature flashed into something vehement and resolute.

On the present occasion, her cheeks went very red, which improved her appearance; she walked up to her husband with a resolute air.

“Now, John, I want to know if you think such a law as that is right and Christian?”

“You won’t shoot me, now, Mary, if I say I do!”

“I never could have thought it of you, John; you didn’t vote for it?”

“Even so, my fair politician.”

“You ought to be ashamed, John! Poor, homeless, houseless creatures! It’s a shameful, wicked, abominable law, and I’ll break it, for one, the first time I get a chance; and I hope I shall have a chance, I do! Things have got to a pretty pass, if a woman can’t give a warm supper and a bed to poor, starving creatures, just because they are slaves, and have been abused and oppressed all their lives, poor things!”

“But, Mary, just listen to me. Your feelings are all quite right, dear, and interesting, and I love you for them; but, then, dear, we mustn’t suffer our feelings to run away with our judgment; you must consider it’s a matter of private feeling,—there are great public interests involved,—there is such a state of public agitation rising, that we must put aside our private feelings.”

“Now, John, I don’t know anything about politics, but I can read my Bible; and there I see that I must feed the hungry, clothe the naked, and comfort the desolate; and that Bible I mean to follow.”

“But in cases where your doing so would involve a great public evil—”

“Obeying God never brings on public evils. I know it can’t. It’s always safest, all round, to do as He bids us.”

“Now, listen to me, Mary, and I can state to you a very clear argument, to show—”

“O, nonsense, John! you can talk all night, but you wouldn’t do it. I put it to you, John,—would you now turn away a poor, shivering, hungry creature from your door, because he was a runaway? Would you, now?”

He hemmed and coughed, and wiped his glasses, and did various things that gave time to a humane nature thus pressed in a sore point, for this was not his forte, as his wife well knew.

“I should like to see you doing that, John—I really should! Turning a woman out of doors in a snow-storm, for instance; or maybe you’d take her up and put her in jail, wouldn’t you? You would make a great hand at that!”

“Of course, it would be a very painful duty,” began Mr. Bird.

“Duty, John! don’t use that word! You know it isn’t a duty—it can’t be a duty! If folks want to keep their slaves from running away, let ’em treat ’em well,—that’s my doctrine. I tell you folks don’t run away when they are happy; and when they do run, poor creatures! they suffer enough with cold and hunger and fear, without everybody’s turning against them; and, law or no law, I never will, so help me God!”

“Mary! Mary! My dear, let me reason with you.”

“I hate reasoning, John,—especially reasoning on such subjects. There’s a way you political folks have of coming round and round a plain right thing; and you don’t believe in it yourselves, when it comes to practice. I know you well enough, John. You don’t believe it’s right any more than I do; and you wouldn’t do it any sooner than I.”

At this critical juncture, old Cudjoe, the black man-of-all-work, put his head in at the door and wished “Missis would come into the kitchen;” and our senator, tolerably relieved, looked after his little wife with a whimsical mixture of amusement and vexation, and seated himself to read the papers.

“John! John! I do wish you’d come here, a moment,” his wife’s quick, earnest voice called presently.

He started at the sight in the kitchen:—A young, slender woman, garments torn and frozen, one shoe gone, the stocking torn from a cut and bleeding foot, lay back in a deadly swoon on two chairs. There was the despised race in her face, yet none could help feeling its mournful, pathetic beauty; its stony sharpness and cold, fixed aspect struck a chill over him. Mrs. Bird and their only colored domestic, old Aunt Dinah, were busy with restoratives; and old Cudjoe had got the boy on his knee, chafing his cold feet.

“Sure, now, if she an’t a sight to behold!” said Dinah, compassionately. “‘Pears like ’t was the heat made her faint. She was tol’able peart when she cum in, and asked if she couldn’t warm herself a spell; and I was jest a-askin’ her where she cum from, and she fainted right down.”

“Poor creature!” said Mrs. Bird, as the woman slowly unclosed large, dark eyes and looked vacantly about; then, with agony crossing her face, sprang up.

“O, my Harry! Have they got him?”

The boy leaped from Cudjoe’s knee and ran to her. “O, he’s here! he’s here!” she cried, gathering him.

“O, ma’am!” she said wildly to Mrs. Bird, “do protect us! don’t let them get him!”

“Nobody shall hurt you here, poor woman,” said Mrs. Bird, encouragingly. “You are safe; don’t be afraid.”

“God bless you!” said the woman, covering her face and sobbing, while the boy tried to climb into her lap. She would not yield him to any hand; even in sleep, when, at last, a bed was made by the fire and she sank down with him, her arm encircled him with an unrelaxing clasp, as if scarcely in dreams would she be beguiled of her hold.

Mr. and Mrs. Bird returned to the parlor and, strange as it may appear, made no reference to their previous conversation. She busied herself with her knitting; he pretended to read.

“I wonder who and what she is!” said Mr. Bird at last.

“When she wakes up and feels a little rested, we will see,” said Mrs. Bird.

“I say, wife!”

“Well, dear!”

“She couldn’t wear one of your gowns, could she, by any letting down, or such matter? She seems to be rather larger than you are.”

Mrs. Bird smiled. “We’ll see.”

“And there’s that old bombazin cloak you keep to put over me in my naps; you might as well give her that,—she needs clothes.”

Dinah came to say the woman was awake and wished to see “Missis.”

The woman sat by the fire, looking into the blaze with a calm, heart-broken expression.

“Did you want me?” said Mrs. Bird, gently. “I hope you feel better now, poor woman!”

A shivering sigh was the only answer; then the dark eyes were lifted with such a forlorn, imploring expression that tears came into the little woman’s eyes.

“You needn’t be afraid of anything; we are friends here. Tell me where you came from, and what you want,” said she.

“I came from Kentucky,” said the woman.

“When?” asked Mr. Bird.

“Tonight.”

“How did you come?”

“I crossed on the ice.”

“Crossed on the ice!” all exclaimed.

“Yes. God helping me, I crossed on the ice; for they were behind me—right behind—and there was no other way!”

“Law, Missis,” said Cudjoe, “the ice is all in broken-up blocks, a swingin’ and a teterin’ up and down in the water!”

“I know it was—I know it!” she cried; “but I did it! I could but die, if I didn’t. The Lord helped me; nobody knows how much the Lord can help ’em, till they try.”

“Were you a slave?” said Mr. Bird.

“Yes, sir; I belonged to a man in Kentucky.”

“Was he unkind?”

“No, sir; he was a good master.”

“And your mistress?”

“No, sir—no! my mistress was always good to me.”

“What could induce you to leave a good home, then, and run away, and go through such dangers?”

The woman looked at Mrs. Bird and saw she was in deep mourning.

“Ma’am,” she said suddenly, “have you ever lost a child?”

Mrs. Bird burst into tears. “Why do you ask that? I have lost a little one.”

“Then you will feel for me. I have lost two, one after another,—left ’em buried there when I came away; and I had only this one left. I never slept a night without him; he was all I had. And, ma’am, they were going to take him away from me,—to sell him,—sell him down south, ma’am, to go all alone,—a baby that had never been away from his mother in his life! I couldn’t stand it, ma’am. I knew I never should be good for anything, if they did; and when I knew the papers were signed, and he was sold, I took him and came off in the night; and they chased me,—the man that bought him, and some of Mas’r’s folks,—and they were coming right behind me, and I heard ’em. I jumped right on to the ice; and how I got across, I don’t know,—but, first I knew, a man was helping me up the bank.”

“How came you to tell me you had a kind master?” the senator burst out, resolutely gulping down a rising in his throat.

“Because he was a kind master; I’ll say that of him, any way;—and my mistress was kind; but they couldn’t help themselves. They were owing money; and there was some way, I can’t tell how, that a man had a hold on them, and they were obliged to give him his will. I listened, and heard him telling mistress that, and she begging and pleading for me,—and he told her he couldn’t help himself, and that the papers were all drawn;—and then it was I took him and left my home, and came away.”

“Have you no husband?”

“Yes, but he belongs to another man. His master is real hard to him, and won’t let him come to see me, hardly ever; and he threatens to sell him down south;—it’s like I’ll never see him again!”

“And where do you mean to go, my poor woman?” said Mrs. Bird.

“To Canada, if I only knew where that was. Is it very far off, is Canada?” she said, with a simple, confiding air.

“Much further than you think, poor child!” said Mrs. Bird. “But we will try to think what can be done for you. Dinah, make her up a bed in your own room, close by the kitchen, and I’ll think what to do in the morning. Meanwhile, never fear; put your trust in God; He will protect you.”

Back in the parlor, Mrs. Bird sat swaying thoughtfully in her little rocker, while the senator strode and grumbled, “Pish! pshaw! confounded awkward business!” At length, striding up, he said,

“I say, wife, she’ll have to get away from here, this very night. That fellow will be down on the scent bright and early tomorrow morning: if ’t was only the woman, she could lie quiet till it’s over; but that little chap can’t be kept still by a troop of horse and foot. A pretty kettle of fish it would be for me to be caught with them both here, just now! No; they’ll have to be got off tonight.”

“Tonight! How is it possible?—where to?”

“Well, I know pretty well where to,” said the senator, drawing on his boots, and embracing his knee with both hands, going off in deep meditation. “There’s my old client, Van Trompe, come over from Kentucky, set all his slaves free; he’s bought a place seven miles up the creek, back in the woods, where nobody goes unless they go on purpose. There she’d be safe enough; but nobody could drive a carriage there tonight but me.”

“Why not? Cudjoe is an excellent driver.”

“Ay, ay; but the creek has to be crossed twice; the second crossing is dangerous unless one knows it as I do. Cudjoe must put in the horses as quietly as may be, about twelve o’clock, and I’ll take her over; and then, to give color to the matter, he must carry me on to the next tavern to take the stage for Columbus, that comes by about three or four, and so it will look as if I’d had the carriage only for that. I shall feel rather cheap there, after all that’s been said and done; but, hang it, I can’t help it!”

“Your heart is better than your head, in this case, John,” said the wife, laying her little white hand on his. “Could I ever have loved you, had I not known you better than you know yourself?”

At the door he stopped, hesitated, and came back.

“Mary, I don’t know how you’d feel about it, but there’s that drawer full of things—of—of—poor little Henry’s.”

She took the candle into the little bed-room, unlocked the drawer, and paused. Ah! mother, has there never been in your house a drawer, the opening of which was like the opening again of a little grave? There were little coats, piles of aprons, rows of small stockings, even a pair of little shoes rubbed at the toes; a toy horse and wagon, a top, a ball,—memorials gathered with many a tear. She sat down and wept until the tears fell into the drawer; then, with nervous haste, she selected the plainest and most substantial articles, and gathered them in a bundle.

“Mamma,” said one of the boys, gently, “you going to give away those things?”

“My dear boys,” she said softly, “if our dear, loving little Henry looks down from heaven, he would be glad to have us do this. I could not give them to any common person,—to anybody that was happy; but I give them to a mother more heart-broken than I am; and I hope God will send His blessings with them!”

From a wardrobe she took a plain, serviceable dress or two, and, with needle, scissors, and thimble, quietly commenced the “letting down” process, and continued till the old clock struck twelve and wheels rattled at the door.

“Mary,” said her husband, “you must wake her up now; we must be off.”

Mrs. Bird hastily deposited the articles in a small plain trunk; Mr. Bird took it to the carriage; and the woman—Eliza, the fugitive mother—appeared at the door, arrayed in cloak, bonnet, and shawl that had belonged to her benefactress, her child in her arms. Mr. Bird hurried her in; Mrs. Bird pressed out after. Eliza leaned from the carriage and put out her hand,—a hand as soft and beautiful as that given in return. She fixed large, dark eyes, full of earnest meaning, on Mrs. Bird’s face; her lips moved, once or twice,—there was no sound; she pointed upward, with a look never to be forgotten, fell back, and covered her face. The door shut; the carriage drove on.

What a situation, now, for a patriotic senator, that had been all the week before spurring up the legislature of his state to pass more stringent resolutions against escaping fugitives, their harborers and abettors! He had been as bold as a lion about it; the image of a fugitive had been but the letters that spelt the word—or, at the most, a little newspaper picture of a man with a stick and bundle, with “Ran away from the subscriber” under it. The magic of the real presence of distress,—the imploring human eye, the frail, trembling hand, the despairing appeal of helpless agony,—these he had never tried. He had never thought a fugitive might be a hapless mother, a defenceless child, like that one now wearing his lost boy’s little well-known cap. And as our poor senator was not stone or steel,—as he was a man, and a downright noble-hearted one, too,—he was, as everybody must see, in a sad case for his patriotism.

Be that as it may, if our good senator was a political sinner, he was in a fair way to expiate it by his night’s penance. There had been long rainy weather; the soft, rich earth of Ohio is admirably suited to the manufacture of mud; and the road was an Ohio railroad of the good old times,—a corduroy causeway of round rough logs, with chasms and ruts of black mud between. Over such a road our senator went stumbling along, making moral reflections as continuously as might be,—the carriage proceeding thus,—bump! bump! bump! slush!—down in the mud!—the senator, woman, and child reversed in a twinkling against the down-hill window; carriage sticks fast; Cudjoe outside musters the horses; after pullings and twitchings, just as patience fails, the carriage rights with a bounce,—two front wheels down into another abyss, and all tumble on the front seat,—the senator’s hat jammed over eyes and nose; child cries; Cudjoe cracks the whip; carriage springs up with another bounce,—down go the hind wheels; elbows encounter bonnet; both her feet are jammed into his hat, which flies off; then a pause,—the “slough” is passed; horses pant; the senator finds his hat; the woman straightens her bonnet and hushes her child; they brace themselves anew.

At last, with a square plunge that sets all on their feet and then down again with incredible quickness, the carriage stops. After much commotion, Cudjoe appears.

“Please, sir, it’s powerful bad spot, this ’yer. I don’t know how we’s to get clar out. I’m a thinkin’ we’ll have to be a gettin’ rails.”

The senator steps out gingerly; down goes one foot immeasurably; he tries to pull it up, loses balance, tumbles over into the mud, and is fished out by Cudjoe. But we forbear, out of sympathy to our readers’ bones.

It was full late in the night when the carriage emerged, dripping and bespattered, out of the creek, and stood at the door of a large farmhouse. It took perseverance to arouse the inmates; at last the proprietor appeared and undid the door,—a great, tall, bristling Orson of a fellow, full six feet and some inches in his stockings, in a red flannel hunting-shirt; a heavy mat of sandy hair and a beard of some days, giving him an appearance not prepossessing. He stood, candle aloft, blinking on our travellers with a mystified expression that was truly ludicrous.

“Are you the man that will shelter a poor woman and child from slave-catchers?” said the senator, explicitly.

“I rather think I am,” said honest John Van Trompe, with emphasis.

“I thought so,” said the senator.

“If there’s anybody comes,” said the good man, stretching his tall form, “why here I’m ready for him: and I’ve got seven sons, each six foot high, and they’ll be ready for ’em. Give our respects to ’em. Tell ’em it’s no matter how soon they call,—make no kinder difference to us,” said John, running his fingers through the shock that thatched his head, and bursting into a great laugh.

Weary, jaded, spiritless, Eliza dragged herself up to the door, with her child lying in a heavy sleep on her arm. The rough man held the candle to her face; uttering a compassionate grunt, he opened a small bed-room off the kitchen, set a light upon the table, and addressed her.

“Now, I say, gal, you needn’t be a bit afeard, let who will come here. I’m up to all that sort o’ thing,” he said, pointing to two or three goodly rifles over the mantel; “and most people that know me know that ’t wouldn’t be healthy to try to get anybody out o’ my house when I’m agin it. So now you jist go to sleep, as quiet as if yer mother was a rockin’ ye,” said he, shutting the door.

“Why, this is an uncommon handsome un,” he said to the senator. “Ah, well; handsome uns has the greatest cause to run, sometimes, if they has any kind o’ feelin’, such as decent women should. I know all about that.”

The senator briefly explained Eliza’s history.

“O! ou! aw! now, I want to know?” said the good man, pitifully; “sho! now sho! That’s natur now, poor crittur! hunted down now like a deer,—hunted down, jest for havin’ natural feelin’s, and doin’ what no kind o’ mother could help a doin’! I tell ye what, these yer things make me come the nighest to swearin’, now, o’ most anything,” said honest John, wiping his eyes with the back of a great, freckled hand. “I tell yer what, stranger, it was years and years before I’d jine the church, ’cause the ministers round in our parts used to preach that the Bible went in for these ’ere cuttin’s up,—and I couldn’t be up to ’em with their Greek and Hebrew, and so I took up agin ’em, Bible and all. I never jined till I found a minister that was up to ’em all in Greek and all that, and he said right the contrary; and then I took right hold, and jined,—fact,” said John, uncorking some very frisky bottled cider, which he presented.

“Ye’d better jest put up here, now, till daylight,” said he, heartily; “I’ll call up the old woman, and have a bed ready for you in no time.”

“Thank you, my good friend,” said the senator, “I must be along, to take the night stage for Columbus.”

“Ah! well, then, if you must, I’ll go a piece with you, and show you a cross road that’ll take you there better than the road you came on. That road’s mighty bad.”

Honest John’s history might be told in a word. Once a considerable land-owner and slave-owner in Kentucky, having “nothing of the bear about him but the skin,” and a great, honest, just heart equal to his frame, he had for years watched, with repressed uneasiness, a system equally bad for oppressor and oppressed. At last his heart swelled too big for its bonds; so he took his pocket-book, crossed into Ohio, bought a quarter township of good land, made out free papers for all his people—men, women, and children—packed them in wagons, sent them off to settle, and then turned up the creek to a snug farm, to enjoy his conscience and reflections.

He equipped himself now with a lantern and guided the senator’s carriage toward a road that ran down in a hollow, back of his dwelling. When they parted, the senator put into his hand a ten-dollar bill.

“It’s for her,” he said, briefly.

“Ay, ay,” said John, with equal conciseness.

They shook hands, and parted.

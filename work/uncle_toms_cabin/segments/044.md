As ghost rumors spread, Cassy and Emmeline slip from the garret in disguise—Cassy as a Creole lady, Emmeline as her maid—reach the riverboats, and there encounter George Shelby and a French lady, Madame de Thoux, who reveals herself as George Harris’s sister; hearing names and papers, Cassy first suspects that Eliza may be her long-lost child. Legree’s terror intensifies, and that night the house is found open as two white figures vanish down the avenue.

The rest of our story is soon told. George Shelby, interested, as any other young man might be, by the romance of the incident, no less than by feelings of humanity, sent to Cassy the bill of sale of Eliza; its date and name all corresponded with her knowledge of facts, and she felt no doubt of her child’s identity. It remained only to trace the fugitives’ path.

Madame de Thoux and Cassy, thus curiously joined by fortune, went straightway to Canada, making inquiry among the stations where fugitives were located. At Amherstberg they found the missionary who had sheltered George and Eliza on their first landing, and through him traced the family to Montreal.

George and Eliza had now been five years free. George, in a worthy machinist’s shop, had found constant occupation, earning a competent support; the family had been increased by another daughter. Little Harry—a fine, bold boy—was at a good school, making rapid progress.

So much was the Amherstberg pastor moved by the statements of Madame de Thoux and Cassy, that, at the former’s solicitation, he accompanied them to Montreal in their search,—she bearing all the expense.

The scene changes to a small, neat tenement on the outskirts of Montreal; evening. A cheerful fire on the hearth; a tea-table spread; in one corner a green-covered table, with an open desk, pens and paper, and a shelf of well-chosen books. This was George’s study. The same zeal for improvement which had led him to snatch the arts of reading and writing, amid toil and discouragement, still made him devote leisure to self-cultivation. He sat making notes from a volume of the family library.

“Come, George,” says Eliza, “you’ve been gone all day. Do put down that book, and let’s talk, while I’m getting tea,—do.”

And little Eliza seconds it by toddling up and striving to pull away the book, to install herself on his knee.

“O, you little witch!” says George, yielding, as, in such circumstances, man always must.

“That’s right,” says Eliza, with a matronly air, yet contented and happy as woman need be.

“Harry, my boy, how did you come on in that sum, to-day?” says George, laying his hand on his son’s head.

“I did it, every bit of it, myself, father; and nobody helped me!”

“That’s right,” says his father; “depend on yourself, my son. You have a better chance than ever your poor father had.”

There is a rap at the door. Eliza opens it; the delighted “Why! this you?” calls up her husband; and the good pastor of Amherstberg is welcomed. Two ladies are with him, to whom Eliza offers seats.

Truth to say, the honest pastor had arranged a little programme for the development of this affair, and all, on the way, had prudently exhorted each other to keep to order. Conceive, then, his consternation, when, just as he had motioned the ladies to be seated, and was wiping his lips for his introductory speech, Madame de Thoux threw her arms round George’s neck, and, letting all out at once, cried,

“O, George! don’t you know me? I’m your sister Emily.”

Cassy had seated herself more composedly, and would have carried her part well, had not little Eliza suddenly appeared before her in exact shape and curl as her own child had been when last she saw her. The little thing peered up; Cassy caught her to her bosom, saying, in what, at the moment, she really believed,

“Darling, I’m your mother!”

In fine, it was troublesome to do it all in order; but the good pastor at last succeeded in composing them, and delivered the speech he had intended; and so well, that his whole audience were sobbing about him in a manner to satisfy any orator.

They knelt together; and the good man prayed,—for there are feelings so agitated and tumultuous that they can find rest only by being poured into the bosom of Almighty love,—and, rising, the new-found family embraced with a holy trust in Him who, through perils and by unknown ways, had brought them together.

The missionary’s note-book among the Canadian fugitives contains truth stranger than fiction. When a system prevails that whirls and scatters families as the wind the autumn leaves, these shores of refuge, like the eternal shore, often unite in glad communion hearts long mourning each other as lost. Deeds of heroism are wrought, when, defying torture and braving death, the fugitive threads back to that dark land to bring away sister, mother, or wife. One young man, twice recaptured and shamefully scourged, escaped again, and wrote that he would go a third time to bring his sister. Is this man a hero, or a criminal? Would not you do as much?

But to return to our friends, now seated about the social board, decidedly companionable; only that Cassy, who keeps little Eliza on her lap, now and then squeezes her in a way that astonishes the child, and obstinately refuses to stuff her with cake, alleging, to the little one’s wonder, that she has something better than cake.

Indeed, in two or three days such a change passed over Cassy that our readers would scarce know her. The despairing, haggard look gave place to gentle trust. She sank at once into the bosom of the family, took the little ones into her heart, as that for which it long had waited. Her love flowed even more naturally to the little Eliza than to her own daughter, for she was the very image of the lost child,—a flowery bond through whom grew up acquaintanceship and affection. Eliza’s steady, consistent piety, formed by constant reading of the sacred word, made her a proper guide to her mother’s shattered and wearied mind. Cassy yielded, with her whole soul, to every good influence, and became a devout and tender Christian.

After a day or two, Madame de Thoux told her brother more particularly of her affairs. The death of her husband had left her an ample fortune, which she generously offered to share. When she asked George how best to apply it, he answered,

“Give me an education, Emily; that has always been my heart’s desire. Then, I can do all the rest.”

On mature deliberation, it was decided that the whole family should go, for some years, to France; whither they sailed, carrying Emmeline with them. The latter’s good looks won the affection of the first mate; shortly after entering port she became his wife. George remained four years at a French university, where, with unintermitted zeal, he obtained a thorough education. Political troubles at last led the family again to seek an asylum in this country.

George’s feelings and views, as an educated man, may be best gathered from a letter to a friend:

“I feel somewhat at a loss as to my future course. True, as you have said, I might mingle in the circles of the whites; my shade of color is slight, and that of my wife and children scarce perceptible. Well—perhaps, on sufferance, I might. But, to tell you the truth, I have no wish to.

“My sympathies are not for my father’s race, but for my mother’s. To him I was no more than a fine dog or horse: to my poor heart-broken mother I was a child. When I think of all she suffered; of my own early sufferings; of the distresses and struggles of my heroic wife; of my sister, sold in the New Orleans market,—though I hope to have no unchristian sentiments, yet I may be excused for saying, I have no wish to pass for an American, or to identify myself with them.

“The desire and yearning of my soul is for an African nationality. I want a people that shall have a tangible, separate existence of its own; and where am I to look for it? Not in Hayti; for in Hayti they had nothing to start with. A stream cannot rise above its fountain. The race that formed the character of the Haytiens was a worn-out, effeminate one; the subject race will be centuries in rising.

“Where, then, shall I look? On the shores of Africa I see a republic,—formed of picked men, who by energy and self-education have individually raised themselves above slavery. Having gone through feebleness, this republic has become an acknowledged nation, owned by France and England. There it is my wish to go, and find myself a people.

“I grant Liberia may have been played off, in the hands of our oppressors, against us; may have been used unjustifiably to retard emancipation. But is there not a God above man’s schemes? May He not have overruled their designs, and founded for us a nation by them?

“In these days, a nation is born in a day. A nation starts now with the great problems of republican life wrought out to its hand;—it has not to discover, but only to apply. Let us then all take hold, with all our might, and see what we can do; and the whole splendid continent of Africa opens before us and our children. Our nation shall roll the tide of civilization and Christianity along its shores, and plant there mighty republics, that, growing with tropical rapidity, shall be for coming ages.

“Do you say I am deserting my enslaved brethren? I think not. If I forget them one hour, one moment of my life, so may God forget me! But what can I do for them here? Can I break their chains? No—not as an individual; but let me go and form part of a nation that shall have a voice in the councils of nations, and then we can speak. A nation has a right to argue, remonstrate, implore, and present the cause of its race,—which an individual has not.

“If Europe becomes a grand council of free nations,—as I trust in God it will,—and if, as France and England have done, they acknowledge our position; then, in the great congress of nations, we will present the cause of our enslaved and suffering race; and it cannot be that free, enlightened America will not then desire to wipe from her escutcheon that bar sinister which disgraces her among nations, and is as truly a curse to her as to the enslaved.

“You will tell me, our race have equal rights to mingle in the American republic as the Irishman, the German, the Swede. Granted, they have; and they who deny it are false to professed principles of human equality. We have more than the rights of common men—we have the claim of an injured race for reparation. But then, I do not want it. I want a country, a nation, of my own. I think the African race has peculiarities, yet to be unfolded in the light of civilization and Christianity, which, if not the same with those of the Anglo-Saxon, may prove, morally, of even a higher type.

“I trust the development of Africa is to be essentially Christian. If not a dominant and commanding race, they are, at least, an affectionate, magnanimous, forgiving one. Called in the furnace of oppression, they must bind to their hearts that sublime doctrine of love and forgiveness, through which alone they are to conquer, and which it is to be their mission to spread over Africa.

“In myself I am feeble for this,—full half the blood in my veins is the hot and hasty Saxon; but I have an eloquent preacher ever by my side, in my beautiful wife. When I wander, her gentler spirit restores me, and keeps before my eyes the Christian calling of our race. As a Christian patriot, as a teacher of Christianity, I go to my country,—my chosen, my glorious Africa!—and to her, in my heart, I sometimes apply those splendid words: ‘Whereas thou hast been forsaken and hated, so that no man went through thee; I will make thee an eternal excellence, a joy of many generations!’

“You will call me an enthusiast; say I have not counted the cost. But I have considered it. I go to Liberia, not as an Elysium of romance, but as to a field of work. I expect to work with both hands,—to work hard; against all sorts of difficulties; and to work till I die. This is what I go for; and in this I am quite sure I shall not be disappointed.

“Whatever you may think of my determination, do not divorce me from your confidence; and think that, in whatever I do, I act with a heart wholly given to my people.

“GEORGE HARRIS.”

George, with his wife, children, sister, and mother, embarked for Africa a few weeks after. If we are not mistaken, the world will yet hear from him there.

Of our other characters we have little to add, save a word of Miss Ophelia and Topsy, and a farewell chapter to George Shelby. Miss Ophelia took Topsy home to Vermont,—to the surprise of that grave deliberative body, “our folks.” They thought it an odd addition to a well-trained household; but so thorough was Miss Ophelia in conscientious duty to her élève, that the child grew rapidly in grace and favor with family and neighborhood. At womanhood, by her own request, she was baptized and became a member of the Christian church; and showed such intelligence, activity, zeal, and desire to do good, that she was approved as a missionary to a station in Africa; and we have heard that the same ingenuity which, when a child, made her multiform and restless, is now employed, in safer and wholesomer ways, in teaching the children of her own country.

P.S.—Some inquiries, set on foot by Madame de Thoux, have recently resulted in the discovery of Cassy’s son. Being a young man of energy, he had escaped, years before his mother, and been received and educated by friends of the oppressed in the North. He will soon follow his family to Africa.

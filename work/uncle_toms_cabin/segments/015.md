Evangeline

“A young star! which shone
O’er life—too sweet an image, for such glass!
A lovely being, scarcely formed or moulded;
A rose with all its sweetest leaves yet folded.”

The Mississippi! Once a river of mighty solitudes, it now bears along its broad bosom to the sea the wealth and hurry of a land whose products run from tropic to pole. Turbid waters, tearing, foaming, headlong,—an image of the vehement race that drives its business on their wave; and, alas! they bear, too, a darker freight,—the tears of the oppressed, the sighs of helpless hearts to an unknown God—unknown, unseen, and silent, yet who will “come out of his place to save all the poor of the earth!”

The slanting sun kindled the sea-like sweep; the shivery cane and tall cypress, hung with funereal moss, glowed in the golden ray, as the heavily-laden steamboat marched onward. Piled with cotton, up over deck and sides, she loomed like a square, gray block, moving slowly toward the mart.

High on the upper deck, in a nook among the bales, sat our humble friend Tom. Partly through Mr. Shelby’s letters, partly through Tom’s quiet, inoffensive ways, even Haley, the trader, had ceased to fetter him at night, and, trusting his parole of honor, let him come and go on the boat. Ever obliging, with a hearty good will in any need among the hands, he had won general favor; and when there was nothing to do, he climbed to his nook and bent over his Bible.

For a hundred miles above New Orleans, the river, higher than the land, rolls between levees like walls. From the deck, as from a floating castle, one overlooks miles of country; and so, plantation after plantation, Tom read in silence the life he was approaching. He saw gangs at their toil; villages of huts, long and low, beyond the stately house and pleasure-grounds; and, as the moving picture slid by, his poor, foolish heart turned back to the Kentucky farm,—the old shadowy beeches, the master’s cool halls, and, near by, the little vine-overgrown cabin; the familiar faces of those who grew with him; his busy wife; the merry laugh of his boys; the chirrup of the baby at his knee,—till, with a start, all faded again into canebrakes and cypress, the creak and groan of engines, and the stern certainty that all that phase of life was gone forever.

He could not write—no letter, no sign bridged the gulf. Is it strange, then, that some tears fell on his Bible, as with patient finger he threaded his slow way from word to word? He had learned late, and read laboriously; but here was a book whose words are ingots of gold, best weighed singly. Pointing and pronouncing half aloud, he read:

“Let—not—your—heart—be—troubled.
In—my—Father’s—house—are—many—mansions.
I—go—to—prepare—a—place—for—you.”

His Bible, though lacking learned notes, was scored with bold marks of his own devising—way-marks that Master’s children had helped him set as they read to him; so he could seize his favorites without spelling wearily through all between. Every passage breathed of some old home-scene; it was to him all of this life that remained, and the promise of another.

Among the passengers was a young gentleman of fortune from New Orleans, St. Clare by name, with a little daughter of five or six, and a lady connected with them who had the charge of the child.

Tom had often caught glimpses of the little girl,—one of those tripping creatures no more to be contained than a sunbeam. Her form had the airy grace of a myth. Not her features so much as the dreamy earnestness of expression held the dullest; her head and neck were nobly set; long golden-brown hair floated like a cloud; deep, violet-blue eyes, shaded with heavy fringes, made all turn and look as she glided here and there. Yet she was not grave; an innocent playfulness flickered, summer-like, over her, and she seemed always in motion, singing to herself, moving as in a happy dream. Dressed in white, she passed like a shadow without spot, and there was not a nook above or below where her fairy foot had not glided and that visionary head fleeted by. Rough voices blessed her; rough hands reached to smooth her path; the fireman paused, seeing those eyes look into the raging furnace with pity, as if for him; the steersman smiled when the picture-like head gleamed through his window.

Tom, with his soft, impressible nature, yearned toward the childlike, and watched her with growing interest. He half believed her an angel out of his New Testament, whenever that golden head and those deep eyes peeped at him from behind the dusky bales. Often she walked mournfully round where Haley’s gang sat in chains; she would glide in, lift a chain with her little hands, sigh woefully, and pass on; and, again, she came with hands full of candy, nuts, and oranges, distributing them joyfully, and vanishing.

He knew many simple arts to invite little people—baskets cut from cherry-stones, faces carved on hickory-nuts, jumping figures from elder-pith, whistles of many sorts,—treasures he had hoarded for his master’s children, and now, with prudent economy, displayed, one by one. For all her busy interest, she was shy; a while she perched like a canary on some box near him, gravely taking what he offered; but at last they were on confidential terms.

“What’s little missy’s name?” said Tom, when the moment seemed ripe.

“Evangeline St. Clare,” said the little one; “though papa and everybody else call me Eva. Now, what’s your name?”

“My name’s Tom; the little chil’en used to call me Uncle Tom, way back thar in Kentuck.”

“Then I mean to call you Uncle Tom, because, you see, I like you,” said Eva. “So, Uncle Tom, where are you going?”

“I don’t know, Miss Eva.”

“Don’t know?” said Eva.

“No, I am going to be sold to somebody. I don’t know who.”

“My papa can buy you,” said Eva, quickly; “and if he buys you, you will have good times. I mean to ask him, this very day.”

“Thank you, my little lady,” said Tom.

The boat stopped at a wooding-place, and hearing her father’s voice, Eva bounded away. Tom went forward to help in wooding, and was busy among the hands. Presently, as the boat started again, Eva and her father stood by the rail; the great wheel churned twice, and, by some sudden movement, the child lost her balance and fell sheer over the side. Her father sprang, but strong arms held him; more efficient help had followed the child.

Tom, standing beneath on the lower deck, saw her strike the water and sink. He was after her in a moment. Broad-chested and strong-armed, he kept afloat easily till she rose; then he caught her, and, swimming to the side, handed her up, dripping, to the grasp of a hundred hands, which, as if one man, were stretched to receive her. A few moments more and her father bore her senseless to the ladies’ cabin, where ensued, as is usual, a kindly, bustling strife of well-meaning hands that did everything but help.

The next day was sultry and close, as the steamer drew near New Orleans. Expectation and preparation stirred through the boat; in cabin and gangway things were gathered and set in order. On the lower deck sat Tom, arms folded, looking anxiously, from time to time, toward a group across the way.

There stood fair Eva, a little paler, but otherwise unhurt; and at her side a graceful, elegantly-formed young man, leaning carelessly on a bale, a large pocket-book open before him. Plainly he was her father. The same noble head, the same blue eyes and golden-brown hair; but the expression was wholly other. The eyes, though in form and color similar, wanted that dreamy depth; all was clear, bold, bright, with a light of this world; the beautifully cut mouth had a proud, somewhat sarcastic turn; a free-and-easy superiority sat not ungracefully on his fine person. He listened, with good-humored negligence, half comic, half contemptuous, to Haley, the trader, who volubly expatiated on the quality of the article for which they bargained.

“All the moral and Christian virtues bound in black Morocco, complete!” he said, when Haley had finished. “Well, now, my good fellow, what’s the damage, as they say in Kentucky; in short, what’s to be paid out for this business? How much are you going to cheat me, now? Out with it!”

“Wal,” said Haley, “if I should say thirteen hundred dollars for that ar fellow, I shouldn’t but just save myself; I shouldn’t, now, re’ly.”

“Poor fellow!” said the young man, fixing his keen, mocking eye on him; “but I suppose you’d let me have him for that, out of a particular regard for me.”

“Well, the young lady here seems to be sot on him, and nat’lly enough.”

“O! certainly; there’s a call on your benevolence, my friend. Now, as a matter of Christian charity, how cheap could you afford to let him go, to oblige a young lady that’s particular sot on him?”

“Wal, now, just think on ’t,” said the trader; “look at them limbs,—broad-chested, strong as a horse. Look at his head; them high forrads allays shows calculatin niggers. Now, a nigger of that heft is worth considerable, just for his body, supposin he’s stupid; but come to put in his calculatin faculties, which I can show he has oncommon, why, ’course, it makes him come higher. Why, that ar fellow managed his master’s hull farm. He has a strornary talent for business.”

“Bad, bad,—very bad; knows altogether too much!” said the other, with the same smile. “Never will do. Your smart fellows are always running off, stealing horses, and raising the devil generally. I think you’ll have to take off a couple of hundred for his smartness.”

“Wal, there might be suthin in that ar, if ’t warnt for his character; but I can show recommends from his master and others, to prove he is one o’ your real pious,—the most humble, prayin, pious crittur ye ever did see. Why, he’s been called a preacher in them parts he came from.”

“And I might use him for a family chaplain, possibly,” added the young man, dryly. “That’s quite an idea. Religion is a remarkably scarce article at our house.”

“You’re joking, now.”

“How do you know I am? Didn’t you just warrant him for a preacher? Has he been examined by any synod or council? Come, hand over your papers.”

Haley, secure in the good-humored twinkle that promised cash at last, laid down a greasy pocket-book and fumbled among papers. The young man looked down with easy drollery.

“Papa, do buy him! It’s no matter what you pay,” whispered Eva, softly, climbing upon a package and putting her arm about her father’s neck. “You have money enough, I know. I want him.”

“What for, pussy? Are you going to use him for a rattle-box, or a rocking-horse, or what?”

“I want to make him happy.”

“An original reason, certainly.”

Here the trader handed up a certificate signed by Mr. Shelby, Tom’s former master. The young man took it with the tips of his long fingers and glanced over it carelessly.

“A gentlemanly hand,” he said, “and well spelt, too. Well—now, I’m not sure, after all, about this religion,” he added, the wicked light returning to his eye; “the country is almost ruined with pious white people; such pious politicians just before elections,—such pious goings on in church and state, that a fellow doesn’t know who’ll cheat him next. I don’t know, either, about religion’s being up in the market, just now. How many hundred dollars, now, do you put on for this religion?”

“You like to be jokin, now,” said the trader; “but there’s sense under that ar. I know there’s differences in religion. Some kinds is mis’rable: your meetin pious; your singin, roarin pious; them ar an’t no account. But there’s your rail softly, quiet, stiddy, honest, pious, that the hull world couldn’t tempt to do nothing they thinks is wrong; and ye see in this letter what Tom’s old master says.”

“Now,” said the young man, stooping gravely over his bills, “if you can assure me that I really can buy this kind of pious, and that it will be set down to my account in the book up above as something belonging to me, I wouldn’t care if I did go a little extra for it. How d’ye say?”

“Wal, raily, I can’t do that,” said the trader. “I’m a thinkin every man’ll have to hang on his own hook in them ar quarters.”

“Rather hard on a fellow that pays extra on religion, and can’t trade with it in the state where he wants it most, an’t it, now?” said St. Clare, making out a roll of bills. “There, count your money, old boy!”

“All right,” said Haley, beaming, and, pulling out an old inkhorn, he filled a bill of sale and handed it to the purchaser.

“I wonder, now, if I was divided up and inventoried,” said the latter, running over the paper, “how much I might bring. So much for the shape of my head, so much for a high forehead, so much for arms and legs, and so much for education, talent, honesty, religion! Bless me! there would be small charge on that last, I’m thinking. But come, Eva.”

He took his daughter’s hand, stepped across the boat, and, carelessly putting the tip of his finger under Tom’s chin, said, good-humoredly, “Look up, Tom, and see how you like your new master.”

Tom looked up. It was not in nature to see that gay, handsome face without pleasure. Tears started, and he said, heartily, “God bless you, Mas’r!”

“Well, I hope he will. What’s your name? Tom? Quite as likely to do it for your asking as mine, from all accounts. Can you drive horses, Tom?”

“I’ve been allays used to horses,” said Tom. “Mas’r Shelby raised heaps of ’em.”

“Well, I think I shall put you in coachy, on condition you won’t be drunk more than once a week, unless in cases of emergency, Tom.”

Tom looked surprised and hurt. “I never drink, Mas’r.”

“I’ve heard that story before, Tom; but then we’ll see. It will be a special accommodation to all concerned, if you don’t. Never mind, my boy,” he added, good-humoredly, seeing Tom still grave; “I don’t doubt you mean to do well.”

“I sartin do, Mas’r,” said Tom.

“And you shall have good times,” said Eva. “Papa is very good to everybody, only he always will laugh at them.”

“Papa is much obliged to you for his recommendation,” said St. Clare, laughing, as he turned on his heel and walked away.

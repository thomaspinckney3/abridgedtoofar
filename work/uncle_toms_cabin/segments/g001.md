VOLUME I.

## Chapter 2
In Which the Reader Is Introduced to a Man of Humanity

Late on a chilly February afternoon, in a handsomely furnished dining-parlor in P——, Kentucky, Mr. Shelby sat close with a visitor over wine. The visitor, Mr. Haley, was a short, thick-set, over-dressed trader whose rings, seals, and blue neckerchief jingled and flashed as he talked in easy defiance of Murray’s Grammar, seasoning speech with oaths. Opposite him, Mr. Shelby bore the look of a gentleman, the air of his house testifying to comfort and ease.

“That is the way I should arrange the matter,” said Shelby—meaning to cover a debt by the sale of Uncle Tom.

“I can’t make trade that way,” returned Haley, holding his glass to the light. Shelby urged Tom’s uncommon worth: “steady, honest, capable, manages my whole farm like a clock.” “You mean honest, as niggers go,” said Haley. No—Shelby meant truly pious and trustworthy. He told how Tom, after “getting religion” at a camp-meeting, had been sent alone to Cincinnati with five hundred dollars and come back faithful. Others had tempted him toward Canada; Tom replied, “Ah, master trusted me, and I couldn’t.”

Haley praised the “valeyable” market of such piety—if it was the genuine article. He had “realized six hundred” on one such, and, with jocular candor, professed the conscience a man in business could afford. He pressed for “a boy or gal to throw in with Tom.” Shelby demurred—he could spare none.

Here a little quadroon boy of four or five entered—black hair like floss silk in glossy curls, great dark eyes under long lashes, scarlet-and-yellow plaid frock—comic assurance and bashfulness mingled. “Hulloa, Jim Crow!” whistled Shelby, tossing raisins. The child danced and sang in wild, grotesque fashion, then mimicked old Uncle Cudjoe and Elder Robbins, to roars of laughter. “That chap’s a case,” cried Haley, flipping him orange, and suddenly clapping Shelby’s shoulder: “Fling in that chap, and I’ll settle the business.”

At that instant his mother entered—a young quadroon woman of about twenty-five, every feature proclaiming maternity to the boy. Her rich, full eye, long lashes, and ripples of silky hair flushed under Haley’s bold admiration. Neatly dressed, finely moulded, she withdrew at Shelby’s word with the child on her arm. “By Jupiter,” said Haley, “there’s an article, now! You might make your fortune on that ar gal in Orleans.” “I don’t want to make my fortune on her,” Shelby said dryly, and turned to fresh wine.

Haley pressed: “Come, how will you trade about the gal?” “She is not to be sold,” said Shelby. “My wife would not part with her for her weight in gold.” “Women always say such things,” Haley laughed; yet he persisted: “You’ll let me have the boy, though?” He proposed to raise “handsome boys for the market—fancy articles entirely,” to stand at rich men’s doors and wait table. Shelby balked: “I hate to take the boy from his mother, sir.” Haley had a humane system: get the girl “off for a day,” do it quietly; buy earrings and a gown to make up. “These critters an’t like white folks, you know; they gets over things, only manage right.”

He enlarged, with becoming diffidence, on the “humanity” of his methods—how other traders pulled screaming infants from mothers’ arms, “very bad policy—damages the article.” He told of “a real handsome gal” in Orleans who went “ravin’ mad” and died in a week when her baby was torn away: “Clear waste, sir, of a thousand dollars, just for want of management; it’s always best to do the humane thing, sir; humanity, sir, I may say, is the great pillar of my management.” Mr. Shelby, half in disgust, half in bitter amusement, found himself laughing with this piquant parody of virtue.

They spoke of Tom Loker—“a clever fellow, only the very devil with niggers—on principle”—and of Haley’s quieter, more “paying” system: avoid “onpleasant parts,” keep women “out of sight, out of mind.” He criticized Kentucky masters for “spilin’” their people with expectations: “’Tan’t no kindness to be givin’ on him notions.” Shelby, ill at ease, promised an answer by evening and cautioned secrecy. Alone, he thought: he would have “kicked the fellow down the steps,” but the man had him at advantage. “If anybody had ever said to me that I should sell Tom down south to one of those rascally traders, I should have said, ‘Is thy servant a dog, that he should do this thing?’ And now it must come.” Eliza’s child, too. “So much for being in debt—heigho!”

The narrator turns to Kentucky, “perhaps the mildest form” of slavery: quiet agriculture; indulgent masters; affectionate slaves; a scene to tempt one to dream the patriarchal legend. “But over and above the scene there broods a portentous shadow—the shadow of law.” So long as the law counts “all these human beings, with beating hearts and living affections, only as so many things belonging to a master,” so long as misfortune can at any moment turn indulgence to “hopeless misery and toil,” nothing beautiful or desirable can be made in the best administration of slavery.

Mr. Shelby, a fair, kindly man, had speculated loosely; his notes had fallen into Haley’s hands—that was the key to all. Meanwhile, Eliza, passing the door, caught enough to know a trader was making offers—for somebody. She thought she heard an offer for her boy; her heart swelled; she strained him tight, so that he looked up in wonder. In the chamber, her tremor made her upset a pitcher and offer a nightgown for a silk dress. “Eliza, child, what ails you?” asked her mistress. Bursting into tears, Eliza stammered that a trader was with master. “Suppose there has,” said Mrs. Shelby. Would master “sell my Harry?” “Sell him! No, you foolish girl!” Mrs. Shelby assured her husband never dealt with southern traders, never meant to sell servants who behaved well. “Who do you think would want to buy your Harry? Do you think all the world are set on him as you are?” Reassured, Eliza laughed at her fears and braided her mistress’s hair.

Mrs. Shelby, a woman of high moral and religious sensibility, generous and energetic, had been sincere in her incredulity. Her husband respected, even stood a little in awe of, her consistent piety, indulging a shadowy hope, perhaps, of “getting into heaven through her superabundance.” The heaviest load on him now was to break to her the contemplated arrangement. She, ignorant of his embarrassments, went out for an evening visit, the matter passed from her mind.

## Chapter 3
The Mother

Eliza had been brought up by her mistress from girlhood—petted, indulged, neatly trained; she embodied that refinement, softness of voice and manner often remarked in quadroon women, joined in her case to dazzling beauty and prepossessing address. Under Mrs. Shelby’s protection, her beauty had not been made a fatal inheritance. She married George Harris, a bright young mulatto hired to a bagging factory, where, by ingenuity and adroitness, he became “the first hand,” and invented a hemp-cleaning machine—quite as much genius, considering his circumstances, as Whitney’s gin.

Handsome, pleasing, a general favorite, George prospered until the day his legal owner rode over to see “what this intelligent chattel had been about.” Shown the machinery by a man who talked fluently, held himself erect, looked manly, the master felt an uneasy inferiority. “What business had his slave to be marching round the country, inventing machines, and holding up his head among gentlemen?” He would “put a stop to it,” take him home, set him to hoeing and digging, make him “step about so smart.”

The manufacturer remonstrated—would increase compensation; George was peculiarly adapted to the business. “No object at all, sir. I don’t need to hire any of my hands out, unless I’ve a mind to.” “But only think of his inventing this machine,” said an unlucky workman. “O yes!—a machine for saving work, is it? Let a nigger alone for that. They are all labor-saving machines themselves.” Then the clincher, the American irony: “It’s a free country, sir; the man’s mine, and I do what I please with him,—that’s it!”

George, transfixed, folded his arms; his eyes flashed like live coals; kindly hands whispered, “Give way, George,” or they would help yet. Taken home, he was put to mean drudgery. He held back disrespectful words, but the gloomy brow and flashing eye spoke the language that “the man could not become a thing.”

In the happy factory time he had wooed and married Eliza, a union made in Mrs. Shelby’s parlor, the mistress herself setting orange-blossoms and veil on the bride’s fair head. For a year or two, they met freely; they buried two infants; then little Harry came, and Eliza’s bleeding heart was bound up. After George’s recall, the manufacturer, true to his word, tried inducements again. Mr. Harris, dogged, repeated: “It’s a free country, sir; the man’s mine, and I do what I please with him,—that’s it!”

A very humane jurist once said, The worst use you can put a man to is to hang him. No; there is another use that a man can be put to that is WORSE!

## Chapter 4
The Husband and Father

Mrs. Shelby gone out for the afternoon, Eliza stood in the verandah when a hand touched her shoulder. “George, is it you?” She drew him into her neat little room. “How glad I am!—why don’t you smile?—and look at Harry—how he grows.” “I wish he’d never been born!” George burst out. “I wish I’d never been born myself!”

Frightened, Eliza leaned on his shoulder and wept. He checked himself, kissed the boy’s long curls—“just like you, Eliza”—yet groaned that loving them was only to drag them down with him. “What’s the use of living? I wish I was dead!” She begged patience—he had a hard master, had lost the factory, but perhaps. “Patient!” He had been patient—torn from the place where all were kind, though he had paid every cent of wages. “Well, it is dreadful,” she admitted; “but, after all, he is your master, you know.” “My master! and who made him my master?” George’s litany of worth spilled out: he knew more of business, managed better, read and wrote better—self-taught, in spite of the man—and was now made a dray-horse, put to work any horse could do. Young Mas’r Tom slashed a whip near the horse; George begged him to stop; the boy struck him; George held the hand; the boy screamed; his father tied George to a tree and handed the switches to the child: “whip him till he is tired.” George’s eyes burned. “Who made this man my master?”

Eliza’s gentle ethics wavered. He told of Carlo, the little dog she had given him—“about all the comfort that I’ve had.” Feeding him scraps, he was ordered to “tie a stone to his neck and throw him in the pond.” George refused; master and boy pelted the drowning creature with stones. “I had to take a flogging because I wouldn’t do it myself.” “O, George, don’t do anything wicked,” Eliza pleaded; “trust in God.” “I an’t a Christian like you; my heart’s full of bitterness; I can’t trust in God. Why does he let things be so?” She urged faith—her mistress said God is doing the very best. “That’s easy to say for people sitting on sofas and riding in carriages.”

Then the blow that made Eliza gasp: Mr. Harris would not allow him to come to Shelby’s any more; he must “take Mina for a wife and settle down,” or be sold down river. “Why—but you were married to me, by the minister, as much as if you’d been a white man!” “Don’t you know a slave can’t be married? There is no law in this country for that; I can’t hold you for my wife, if he chooses to part us.” He wished he’d never seen her; better for them both—better for the child—who might yet be sold. “O, but master is so kind!” “Yes, but who knows?—he may die—and then” the sword would pierce her soul “for every good and pleasant thing” her child is or has; it would make him worth too much for her to keep.

Eliza glanced to the verandah, where the boy rode triumphantly up and down on Mr. Shelby’s walking-stick. She checked the words trembling to tell George of the trader. “No, he has enough to bear.” George straightened. “I’m going to Canada; and when I’m there, I’ll buy you. I’ll buy you and the boy;—God helping me, I will!” “Dreadful! if you should be taken?” “I won’t be taken; I’ll die first! I’ll be free, or I’ll die!” “You won’t kill yourself!” “No need of that. They will kill me, fast enough; they never will get me down the river alive!”

She begged prudence: “go carefully, pray God.” He had a plan: master had sent him past the Shelbys with a note; he believed it would please the man to think he had “aggravated ‘Shelby’s folks’”; George would go home “quite resigned,” make preparations, and in a week be “among the missing.” “Pray for me, Eliza; perhaps the good Lord will hear you.” There were last words and sobs, “such parting as those may make whose hope to meet again is as the spider’s web,” and husband and wife were parted.

## Chapter 5
An Evening in Uncle Tom’s Cabin

Uncle Tom’s cabin nestled close by “the house,” the master’s dwelling. A neat garden-patch sprang strawberries, raspberries, vegetables; a scarlet bignonia and multiflora rose curtained the rough logs; Aunt Chloe’s annuals—marigolds, petunias, four-o’clocks—unfolded their splendors to her pride. Enter: the house-supper over, Aunt Chloe, head cook, had come to “get her ole man’s supper.” A round, black, shining face beneath a checked turban; a soul that was cook to the very bone. Barnyard fowl grew grave at her approach, reflecting on their latter end. Corn-cake in all its kinds—hoe-cake, dodgers, muffins—was her sublime mystery.

Within, one corner held a snowy bed with a piece of carpet—Aunt Chloe’s upper walks—the sacred drawing-room; the other corner, a humbler bed for use. Over the fireplace glittered scriptural prints and a portrait of General Washington that would have much astonished that hero. Two woolly-headed boys guided the baby’s first tottering failures, cheering each tumble as cleverness; the table, somewhat rheumatic in its limbs, bore cups and saucers of brilliant pattern, with symptoms of a meal.

At the table sat Uncle Tom, Mr. Shelby’s best hand—large, broad-chested, glossy black; African features softened by steady good sense and benevolence; self-respecting dignity united with confiding simplicity. He patiently copied letters, big fingers moving slowly over a slate, overseen by young Mas’r George, bright boy of thirteen. “Not that way, Uncle Tom,—that makes a q, you see.” “La sakes, now, does it?” Tom admired the flourishing row of g’s and q’s, and recommenced.

“How easy white folks al’us does things!” said Aunt Chloe, greasing a griddle with a scrap of bacon, eyeing George with pride. “The way he can write, now! and read, too! and come out here evenings and read his lessons to us,—mighty interestin’!” “Aunt Chloe, I’m getting mighty hungry,” said George. “Mose done, Mas’r George,” she peeped—“browning beautiful—a real lovely brown.” She scorned Sally’s green hand at cake-making—“cake ris all to one side—no shape at all”—and whisked from the bake-kettle a pound-cake no city confectioner need be ashamed of.

They heaped sausages, batter-cakes. “They wanted me to come to supper in the house,” said George, “but I knew what was what.” “So you did, honey,” Aunt Chloe nudged, bustling. He reached for the cake with a great knife; “La bless you! you wouldn’t be for cuttin’ it wid dat ar great heavy knife! Smash all down—spile all de pretty rise.” Producing a thin old knife “I keeps sharp a purpose,” she feathered the slices. “Tom Lincon says their Jinny is a better cook than you,” George teased. Aunt Chloe tossed her head: “Dem Lincons an’t much count, no way! As to gettin’ up anything in style, they don’t begin. Good Lor! can Missis Lincon sweep into a room like my missis?” Still, in the “good, plain, common cookin’,” Jinny would “make a good pone o’ bread,” “bile her taters far,” but “come to de higher branches”—the flecky paste “as melts in your mouth”—“she can’t.” Aunt Chloe recalled General Knox praising her chicken pie—how she and Missis had “come pretty near quarrelling about dat ar crust,” till she’d said, with saucy reverence, that the Lord “must have meant me to make de pie-crust, and you to stay in de parlor.” Missis had “larfed in her eyes” and let the cook cook.

When even a boy can eat no more, George noticed the pile of glistening eyes from the corner and tossed them bits. Aunt Chloe baked a goodly pile for the children, seated Tom and George by the chimney, settled the baby on Tom’s big shoulder while Mose and Pete roared like bears and Aunt Chloe declared they “fairly took her head off.” She pulled out a trundle-bed—“we’s goin’ to have the meetin’”—then, at George’s word, shoved it under and let the boys sit up.

Chairs were lacking. “Old Uncle Peter sung both de legs out of dat oldest cheer, last week,” Mose said. Barrels were rolled in, wedged with stones, spanned with boards; tubs and pails were turned; the rickety chairs drawn to the wall. “Mas’r George is such a beautiful reader, now, I know he’ll stay to read for us,” Aunt Chloe said. The room filled—from gray patriarchs to lads and girls. There was harmless gossip—Aunt Sally’s new red headkerchief; how Missis would give Lizzy the spotted muslin when the new barège was made; a new sorrel colt Mr. Shelby thought of buying. Then singing commenced—fine, wild voices, even through nasal intonations. They sang:

“Die on the field of battle,
Die on the field of battle,
    Glory in my soul.”

And oft repeated:

“O, I’m going to glory,—won’t you come along with me?
Don’t you see the angels beck’ning, and a calling me away?
Don’t you see the golden city and the everlasting day?”

They sang of Jordan’s banks, Canaan’s fields, the New Jerusalem; some laughed, some cried, some clapped hands, as if they had crossed the river. An old gray-headed woman, leaning on her staff, testified: “Well, chil’en! I’m mighty glad to hear ye and see ye once more, ’cause I don’t know when I’ll be gone to glory; but I’ve done got ready—my little bundle all tied up, and my bonnet on, jest a waitin’ for the stage to come along and take me home. Sometimes, in the night, I think I hear the wheels a rattlin’. Now, you jest be ready too; for dat ar glory is a mighty thing!” The circle struck up: “O Canaan, bright Canaan, I’m bound for the land of Canaan.”

Mas’r George read the last chapters of Revelation, interrupted by “The sakes now!” “Only hear that!” “Jest think on ’t!” Well trained by his mother, he ventured sober expositions; “a minister couldn’t lay it off better,” they said. Uncle Tom, patriarch in religious matters, exhorted with simple, hearty sincerity; but it was in prayer that he excelled—touching simplicity, childlike earnestness—Scripture “dropping from his lips unconsciously”; he “prayed right up,” so that responses threatened to drown him.

While devotion breathed in the cabin of the man, another scene passed in the master’s hall. The trader and Mr. Shelby sat at a table spread with papers and pens. Mr. Shelby counted bundles of bills, pushing them across; Haley counted likewise. “All fair,” said the trader. “And now for signing these yer.” Shelby hastily signed the bills of sale “like a man that hurries over some disagreeable business,” and pushed them over with the money. Haley drew a parchment from his well-worn valise, glanced, handed it to Shelby, who took it with suppressed eagerness. “Wal, now, the thing’s done!” said the trader, rising. “It’s done!” repeated Mr. Shelby, musingly; fetching a long breath, “It’s done!”

“Yer don’t seem to feel much pleased with it,” Haley observed. “Haley,” said Shelby, “I hope you’ll remember that you promised, on your honor, you wouldn’t sell Tom, without knowing what sort of hands he’s going into.” “Why, you’ve just done it, sir,” said the trader. “Circumstances, you well know, obliged me,” Shelby returned, haughtily. “Wal, you know, they may ’blige me, too,” said Haley. “Howsomever, I’ll do the very best I can in gettin’ Tom a good berth; as to my treatin’ on him bad, you needn’t be a grain afeard. If there’s anything that I thank the Lord for, it is that I’m never noways cruel.” After such expositions of “humanity,” Mr. Shelby was not reassured; but, as it was the best comfort the case admitted, he let the trader go and betook himself to a solitary cigar.

The Stratagem

“The way of the wicked is as darkness; he knoweth not at what he stumbleth.”

The garret of the house that Legree occupied was a great, desolate space, dusty, hung with cobwebs, and littered with cast-off lumber. A former opulent family had left behind tall, high-backed chairs and dusty tables in mouldering rooms, and one or two immense packing-boxes in which the furniture had come stood against the sides of the garret. A small window, with dingy panes, let in a scant, uncertain light. Ghostly enough in itself, it wanted not for legends to increase its terrors. Some years before, a woman who had incurred Legree’s displeasure had been confined there for weeks. What passed there, the negroes whispered darkly; but it was known her body was one day taken down and buried; and afterward, oaths and cursings, and the sound of violent blows, were said to ring through that old garret, mingled with wailings and groans. Once, when Legree overheard something of this, he flew into a passion, and swore the next teller of tales should have a chance to know what was there; he would chain them there a week. The hint suppressed talk, though it did not disturb the story’s credit in the least.

Gradually the staircase to the garret, and even the passage to it, were avoided; the legend was falling into desuetude. It suddenly occurred to Cassy to make use of the superstitious excitability, so great in Legree, for the purpose of her liberation and that of her fellow-sufferer, Emmeline.

Cassy’s sleeping-room was directly under the garret. One day, without consulting Legree, she took it on herself, with some ostentation, to change all the furniture of the room to one at a distance. The under-servants, called to effect the movement, were bustling, when Legree returned from a ride.

“Hallo! you Cass! what’s in the wind now?”

“Nothing; only I choose to have another room,” said Cassy, doggedly.

“And what for, pray?”

“I choose to.”

“The devil you do! and what for?”

“I’d like to get some sleep, now and then.”

“Sleep! well, what hinders your sleeping?”

“I could tell, I suppose, if you want to hear,” said Cassy, dryly.

“Speak out, you minx!”

“O! nothing. I suppose it wouldn’t disturb you! Only groans, and people scuffing, and rolling round on the garret floor, half the night, from twelve to morning!”

“People up garret!” said Legree, uneasily, forcing a laugh. “Who are they, Cassy?”

She raised her sharp, black eyes, and looked him in the face with an expression that went through his bones. “To be sure, Simon, who are they? I’d like to have you tell me. You don’t know, I suppose!”

With an oath, Legree struck at her with his riding-whip; she glided aside, passed through the door, and, looking back, said, “If you’ll sleep in that room, you’ll know all about it. Perhaps you’d better try it!” She shut and locked the door.

Legree blustered, swore, threatened to break it down; then walked uneasily into the sitting-room. Cassy saw her shaft had struck home, and, from that hour, with exquisite address, she never ceased to continue the train of influences she had begun.

In a knot-hole of the garret, she inserted the neck of an old bottle, so that, with the least wind, most doleful wailing sounds proceeded from it, increasing, in a high wind, to a perfect shriek—such as, to credulous, superstitious ears, might well seem the voice of horror and despair.

From time to time the servants heard these sounds, and the memory of the old legend revived. A creeping horror seemed to fill the house; and, though none dared breathe it to Legree, he found himself encompassed by it, as by an atmosphere.

The influence of Cassy over him was strange. He was her owner, tyrant, tormentor; she, wholly in his hands: yet the most brutal man cannot live constantly within a strong female influence and not be greatly controlled by it. When he first bought her, delicately bred, he crushed her, without scruple, beneath his foot. But time, debasing influences, and despair hardened womanhood within her; fiercer fires waked; she had become in a measure his mistress, and he alternately tyrannized over and dreaded her. Of late, a partial insanity had given a weird, unsettled cast to her words and manner that harassed him the more.

A night or two after, Legree sat in the old sitting-room by a flickering wood fire that threw uncertain glances round. It was a stormy, windy night; windows rattling, shutters flapping, wind carousing down the chimney and puffing out smoke and ashes, as if a legion of spirits were after them. He had been casting up accounts and reading newspapers, while Cassy sat in the corner, sullenly looking into the fire. Seeing an old book on the table, which he had noticed in her hands earlier, he took it up,—one of those coarse collections of bloody murders, ghostly legends, and supernatural visitations, strangely fascinating to any one that once begins to read. He poohed and pished, yet turned page after page, till, finally, he threw it down with an oath.

“You don’t believe in ghosts, do you, Cass?” said he, taking the tongs and settling the fire. “I thought you’d more sense than to let noises scare you.”

“No matter what I believe,” said Cassy, sullenly.

“Fellows used to try to frighten me with their yarns at sea,” said Legree. “Never come it round me that way. I’m too tough for any such trash, tell ye.”

Cassy sat looking intensely at him from the shadow. There was that strange light in her eyes which always impressed him with uneasiness.

“Them noises was nothing but rats and the wind,” said Legree. “Rats will make a devil of a noise; I used to hear ’em sometimes down in the hold; and wind,—Lord’s sake! ye can make anything out o’ wind.”

She made no answer; she fixed her glittering eyes on him with that unearthly expression.

“Come, speak out, woman,—don’t you think so?”

“Can rats walk down stairs, and come walking through the entry, and open a door when you’ve locked it and set a chair against it?” said Cassy; “and come walk, walk, walking right up to your bed, and put out their hand, so?”

She kept her eyes on him; he stared like a man in a nightmare, till—when she finished by laying her hand, icy cold, on his—he sprung back, with an oath.

“Woman! what do you mean? Nobody did?”

“O, no,—of course not,—did I say they did?” she answered, with a smile of chilling derision.

“But—did—have you really seen?—Come, Cass, what is it, now,—speak out!”

“You may sleep there, yourself, if you want to know.”

“Did it come from the garret, Cassy?”

“It,—what?”

“Why, what you told of—”

“I didn’t tell you anything,” said Cassy, doggedly.

He walked up and down, uneasily. “I’ll have this yer thing examined. I’ll look into it, this very night. I’ll take my pistols—”

“Do,” said Cassy; “sleep in that room. I’d like to see you doing it. Fire your pistols,—do!”

He stamped and swore.

“Don’t swear,” said Cassy; “nobody knows who may be hearing you. Hark! What was that?”

“What?” said Legree, starting.

A heavy old Dutch clock in the corner began, and slowly struck twelve. For some reason, he neither spoke nor moved; a vague horror fell on him; Cassy, with a keen, sneering glitter in her eyes, counted the strokes.

“Twelve o’clock; well now we’ll see,” said she, turning, opening the door into the passage, and standing as if listening. “Hark! What’s that?” said she, raising her finger.

“It’s only the wind,” said Legree. “Don’t you hear how cursedly it blows?”

“Simon, come here,” said Cassy, in a whisper, laying her hand on his and leading him to the foot of the stairs: “do you know what that is? Hark!”

A wild shriek came pealing down the stairway—from the garret. His knees knocked together; his face grew white.

“Hadn’t you better get your pistols?” said Cassy, with a sneer that froze his blood. “It’s time this thing was looked into, you know. I’d like to have you go up now; they’re at it.”

“I won’t go!” said Legree, with an oath.

“Why not? There an’t any such thing as ghosts, you know! Come!” And she flitted up the winding stairway, laughing, and looking back. “Come on.”

“I believe you are the devil!” said Legree. “Come back you hag,—come back, Cass! You shan’t go!”

She laughed wildly and fled on. He heard her open the entry doors that led to the garret. A wild gust of wind swept down, extinguishing the candle he held, and with it,—fearful, unearthly,—the screams; they seemed shrieked in his very ear. He fled frantically into the parlor. In a few moments Cassy followed,—pale, calm, cold as an avenging spirit, and with that same fearful light in her eye.

“I hope you are satisfied,” said she.

“Blast you, Cass!”

“What for? I only went up and shut the doors. What’s the matter with that garret, Simon, do you suppose?”

“None of your business!”

“O, it an’t? Well,” said Cassy, “at any rate, I’m glad I don’t sleep under it.”

Anticipating the rising wind, that evening Cassy had opened the garret window; of course, the moment the doors were opened, the draft had swept down and extinguished the light. This may serve as a specimen of the game she played till he would sooner have put his head into a lion’s mouth than explore that garret.

Meanwhile, in the night, when all else slept, she slowly and carefully accumulated there provisions sufficient for some time; she transferred, article by article, the greater part of her own and Emmeline’s wardrobe. All being arranged, they only waited a fitting opportunity.

By cajoling Legree, and taking advantage of a good-natured interval, she had got him to take her to a neighboring town on the Red River. With memory sharpened to preternatural clearness, she marked every turn in the road and formed an estimate of the time to traverse it.

When all was matured, we look behind the scenes and see the final coup d’état. Near evening: Legree had been absent on a ride. For many days Cassy had been unusually gracious and accommodating; they had seemed on the best of terms. Now behold her and Emmeline in the latter’s room, busy over two small bundles.

“There, these will be large enough,” said Cassy. “Now put on your bonnet, and let’s start; it’s just about the right time.”

“Why, they can see us yet,” said Emmeline.

“I mean they shall,” said Cassy, coolly. “Don’t you know they must have their chase, at any rate? The way is this:—we will steal out by the back door, and run down by the quarters. One of the overseers will be sure to see us. They will give chase, and we will get into the swamp; then they can’t follow us further till they go and give the alarm, and turn out the dogs, and so on; and, while they are blundering round and tumbling over each other, as they always do, you and I will slip along to the creek that runs back of the house, and wade in it till we get opposite the back door. That will put the dogs all at fault; scent won’t lie in the water. Every one will run out to look after us, and then we’ll whip in at the back door, and up into the garret, where I’ve a nice bed in one of the great boxes. We must stay there a good while; he will raise heaven and earth after us. He’ll muster old overseers from the plantations, and have a great hunt; they’ll go over every inch of ground in that swamp. He makes it his boast that nobody ever got away from him. So let him hunt at his leisure.”

“Cassy, how well you have planned it!” said Emmeline. “Who ever would have thought of it, but you?”

There was neither pleasure nor exultation in Cassy’s eyes,—only a despairing firmness.

“Come,” she said, reaching her hand.

The two fugitives glided noiselessly from the house, and flitted, through gathering shadows, along by the quarters. A crescent moon delayed night a little. As Cassy expected, when near the verge of the swamps that encircled the plantation, they heard a voice calling them to stop. It was not an underling, but Legree himself, pursuing with violent execrations. At the sound, the feebler spirit of Emmeline gave way; she clutched Cassy’s arm. “O, Cassy, I’m going to faint!”

“If you do, I’ll kill you!” said Cassy, drawing a small, glittering stiletto, and flashing it before the girl’s eyes.

The diversion wrought its purpose. Emmeline did not faint, but plunged, with Cassy, into a part of the labyrinth so deep and dark that it was perfectly hopeless for Legree to think of following without assistance.

“Well,” said he, chuckling brutally, “at any rate, they’ve got themselves into a trap now—the baggage! They’re safe enough. They shall sweat for it!”

He came to the quarters as the hands were returning from work. “There’s two runaways in the swamps. I’ll give five dollars to any nigger as catches ’em. Turn out the dogs! All hands!”

The sensation was immediate. Many sprang forward, officiously, for the reward or from cringing habit. Pine-knots were kindled; the dogs were uncoupled; their hoarse, savage bay added to the animation.

“Shall we shoot ’em, if can’t cotch ’em?” asked one, to whom the master brought out a rifle.

“You may fire on Cass, if you like; it’s time she was gone to the devil; but the gal, not,” said Legree. “And now, boys, be spry and smart. Five dollars for him that gets ’em; and a glass of spirits to every one of you, anyhow.”

With the glare of blazing torches, with whoop and shout and savage yell of man and beast, the band proceeded down to the swamp, followed, at some distance, by every servant in the house. The establishment was, in consequence, wholly deserted, when Cassy and Emmeline glided in the back way. The whooping and shouts still filled the air; and, looking from the sitting-room windows, they could see the troop, with their flambeaux, just dispersing along the swamp’s edge.

“See there!” said Emmeline. “The hunt is begun! Hark! the dogs! If we were only there, our chances wouldn’t be worth a picayune. O, for pity’s sake, do let’s hide ourselves. Quick!”

“There’s no occasion for hurry,” said Cassy, coolly; “they are all out after the hunt,—that’s the amusement of the evening! We’ll go up stairs, by and by. Meanwhile,”—and she took deliberately a key from the pocket of a coat Legree had thrown down in his hurry,—“meanwhile I shall take something to pay our passage.”

She unlocked the desk, took out a roll of bills, and counted rapidly.

“O, don’t let’s do that!” said Emmeline.

“Don’t! why not? Would you have us starve in the swamps, or have that that will pay our way to the free states? Money will do anything, girl.” She put the money in her bosom.

“It would be stealing,” said Emmeline, in a distressed whisper.

“Stealing!” said Cassy, with a scornful laugh. “They who steal body and soul needn’t talk to us. Every one of these bills is stolen,—stolen from poor, starving, sweating creatures, who must go to the devil at last, for his profit. Let him talk about stealing! But come, we may as well go up garret; I’ve got a stock of candles there, and some books to pass away the time. You may be pretty sure they won’t come there to inquire after us. If they do, I’ll play ghost for them.”

When Emmeline reached the garret, she found an immense box, in which some heavy pieces of furniture had once been brought, turned on its side, the opening facing the eaves. Cassy lit a small lamp; creeping round under the slant, they established themselves in it. It was spread with a couple of small mattresses and some pillows; a box near by was stored with candles, provisions, and all the clothing necessary, which Cassy had arranged into bundles of an astonishingly small compass.

“There,” said Cassy, fixing the lamp into a small hook she had driven into the side; “this is to be our home for the present. How do you like it?”

“Are you sure they won’t come and search the garret?”

“I’d like to see Simon Legree doing that,” said Cassy. “No, indeed; he’ll be too glad to keep away. As to the servants, they would any of them stand and be shot, sooner than show their faces here.”

Somewhat reassured, Emmeline settled back on her pillow.

“What did you mean, Cassy, by saying you would kill me?” she asked, simply.

“I meant to stop your fainting,” said Cassy, “and I did do it. And now I tell you, Emmeline, you must make up your mind not to faint, let what will come; there’s no sort of need of it. If I had not stopped you, that wretch might have had his hands on you now.”

Emmeline shuddered.

They remained some time in silence; Cassy busied herself with a French book; Emmeline, overcome, fell into a doze and slept. She was awakened by loud shouts and outcries, the tramp of horses’ feet, and the baying of dogs. She started up with a faint shriek.

“Only the hunt coming back,” said Cassy, coolly; “never fear. Look out of this knot-hole. Don’t you see ’em all down there? Simon has to give up, for this night. Look, how muddy his horse is, flouncing about in the swamp; the dogs, too, look crestfallen. Ah, my good sir, you’ll have to try the race again and again,—the game isn’t there.”

“O, don’t speak a word!” said Emmeline; “what if they should hear you?”

“If they do hear anything, it will make them very particular to keep away,” said Cassy. “No danger; we may make any noise we please, and it will only add to the effect.”

At length the stillness of midnight settled down over the house. Legree, cursing his ill luck, and vowing dire vengeance on the morrow, went to bed.

The Victory

"Thanks be unto God, who giveth us the victory."

Have not many of us, in the weary way of life, felt how far easier it were to die than to live? Death may come with a thrill that braces courage; but to live—to wear on, day by day, in mean, bitter, harassing servitude; to have every nerve dampened, every feeling smothered—this slow heart-martyrdom is the searching test of man and woman.

When Tom first stood face to face with his persecutor and thought his hour come, the vision of Jesus and heaven seemed but a step beyond, and he felt he could bear anything. But, when the excitement passed, came back the pain of his bruised limbs, the sense of his degraded, hopeless estate; and the day went heavily.

Long before his wounds were healed, Legree drove him to the field. Day after day of pain and weariness followed, with every indignity malice could devise. There was no leisure for the Bible; in the height of the season Legree pressed all hands through Sundays and week-days alike. At first Tom read a verse or two by the fire's flicker; but soon he came home so exhausted his head swam, his eyes failed, and he sank down with the rest.

Is it strange that the peace and trust which had upborne him should give way to darkness? The gloomiest problem met him ever—souls crushed, evil triumphant, and God silent. Weeks and months he wrestled so. He prayed that God would send deliverance, watched day after day in vague hope of some hand stretched to redeem; and, when none came, bitter thoughts rose that it was vain to serve God, that God had forgotten him. Sometimes he saw Cassy, and now and then, when summoned to the house, the dejected Emmeline; but there was no time to commune with any.

One evening, in utter dejection, he sat by a few decaying brands, his coarse supper baking. He fed the flicker, drew his worn Bible; the marked passages that had so often thrilled him lay there—the courage of patriarchs, seers and witnesses. But the failing eye and weary sense answered not to the mighty touch. Heavily sighing, he put it in his pocket.

A coarse laugh roused him. Legree stood opposite.

"Well, old boy," he said, "you find your religion don't work, it seems! I thought I should get that through your wool, at last!"

Tom was silent.

"You were a fool," said Legree. "I meant to do well by you when I bought you. You might have been better off than Sambo, had easy times; instead of being cut up and thrashed, you might have lorded it round, and cut up the other niggers; had now and then a good warming of whiskey punch. Come, Tom, don't you think you'd better be reasonable?—heave that ar old pack of trash in the fire, and join my church!"

"The Lord forbid!" said Tom, fervently.

"You see the Lord an't going to help you; if he had been, he wouldn't have let me get you! This yer religion is a mess of lying trumpery, Tom. Ye'd better hold to me; I'm somebody, and can do something!"

"No, Mas'r," said Tom; "I'll hold on. The Lord may help me, or not help; but I'll hold to him, and believe him to the last!"

"The more fool you!" Legree spat, spurned him with his foot. "Never mind; I'll chase you down, yet, and bring you under—you'll see!" And he turned away.

When a heavy weight presses the soul to the lowest level it can endure, there comes a desperate effort of every nerve to throw it off; and the heaviest anguish often precedes a return tide of courage. So now. The atheistic taunts drove Tom's desponding soul to the ebb; faith still held, but numb and despairing. He sat, as one stunned. Suddenly all around faded, and a vision rose—One crowned with thorns, buffeted and bleeding. Tom gazed in awe at the majestic patience; the deep pathetic eyes thrilled him. With floods of emotion he stretched his hands and fell upon his knees—when, gradually, the vision changed: the thorns became rays of glory; and, in splendor inconceivable, he saw that same face bending compassionately toward him, and a voice said, "He that overcometh shall sit down with me on my throne, even as I also overcame, and am set down with my Father on his throne."

How long he lay he knew not. When he came to himself, the fire was out; the chill dews had drenched him. But the dread crisis was past. Hunger, cold, degradation, disappointment—they were as nothing. From his deepest soul he loosed every earthly hope, and offered his will a sacrifice to the Infinite. He looked up to the silent stars, and the solitude rang with the words of a hymn he had sung often, but never so:

"The earth shall be dissolved like snow,
    The sun shall cease to shine;
But God, who called me here below,
    Shall be forever mine.

"And when this mortal life shall fail,
    And flesh and sense shall cease,
I shall possess within the veil
    A life of joy and peace.

"When we've been there ten thousand years,
    Bright shining like the sun,
We've no less days to sing God's praise
    Than when we first begun."

Such relations are common among the religious histories of the slaves. Who shall measure what the All-pervading Spirit may do to encourage desponding souls of the desolate? If the poor forgotten one believes Jesus hath appeared and spoken to him, who shall contradict? Did He not proclaim to bind up the broken-hearted, and set at liberty them that are bruised?

When the dim gray of dawn woke them to the field, among the tattered and shivering was one who walked with exultant tread; firmer than the ground was his faith in almighty, eternal love. Ah, Legree, try all your forces now! Agony, want, loss of all things, shall but hasten him to be made a king and a priest unto God.

From that time, a sphere of peace encompassed the lowly heart. Past bleeding regrets; past hope's fluctuations; the bent and struggling will was merged in the Divine. Life's remaining voyage seemed short; eternal blessedness near and vivid; and life's uttermost woes fell unharming.

All noticed the change. Cheerfulness and alertness returned; no insult ruffled him.

"What the devil's got into Tom?" said Legree to Sambo. "A while ago he was down in the mouth, and now he's peart as a cricket."

"Dunno, Mas'r; gwine to run off, mebbe."

"Like to see him try that," said Legree, with a savage grin.

That night, riding home, he turned his horse to the quarters. It was a superb moonlight; China-tree shadows lay pencilled on the turf; the very stillness seemed almost unholy to disturb. He paused: some one was singing. A musical tenor rose:

"When I can read my title clear
    To mansions in the skies,
I'll bid farewell to every fear,
    And wipe my weeping eyes.

"Should earth against my soul engage,
    And hellish darts be hurled,
Then I can smile at Satan's rage,
    And face a frowning world.

"Let cares like a wild deluge come,
    And storms of sorrow fall,
May I but safely reach my home,
    My God, my Heaven, my All."

"So ho!" muttered Legree. "He thinks so, does he? How I hate these cursed Methodist hymns!" He burst out, raised his riding-whip. "How dare you get up this row, when you ought to be in bed? Shut yer old black gash, and get along in with you!"

"Yes, Mas'r," said Tom, with ready cheerfulness, rising.

Provoked beyond measure at his evident happiness, Legree belabored him over head and shoulders.

"There, you dog," he said, "see if you'll feel so comfortable, after that!"

But the blows fell only on the outer man. Tom stood submissive; yet Legree felt his power was somehow gone. As Tom disappeared, a vivid flash crossed his dark mind: he understood it was GOD who stood between him and his victim, and he blasphemed. That submissive, silent man, whom taunts and stripes could not disturb, roused within him the old demoniac cry, "What have we to do with thee, thou Jesus of Nazareth?—art thou come to torment us before the time?"

Tom's soul overflowed with compassion for the poor wretches around him. It seemed his own sorrows were over; out of a strange treasury of peace he longed to pour relief. Opportunities were scant; yet on the way to the fields, at the rows, home again, chances came to help the weary and disheartened. At first, the brutalized scarce comprehended; but, week after week, month after month, the strange, silent, patient man—who bore everyone's burden, sought help from none; stood aside for all and came last; shared his little all; in cold nights gave his tattered blanket to some shivering, sick woman; filled the baskets of the weaker at risk of coming short himself; and, though pursued with unrelenting cruelty, never uttered a word of reviling—this man began to have power over them. When the pressing season passed, and Sundays were allowed, many gathered to hear of Jesus. They would gladly have met to pray and sing; but Legree forbade, and broke up attempts with oaths and execrations; so the blessed news crept from lip to lip.

Who shall speak the simple joy with which some, to whom life was a joyless journey to a dark unknown, heard of a compassionate Redeemer and a heavenly home? The poor mulatto, whose faith had been all but crushed by cruelty and wrong, felt her soul raised by the hymns and Scriptures Tom breathed in her ear between tasks; and even the half-crazed, wandering mind of Cassy was soothed by his unobtrusive influence.

Stung to madness by life's agonies, Cassy had often resolved on retribution. One night, after the cabin slept, Tom saw her face at the chink that served for a window. She made a silent gesture.

Tom came out. It was broad, calm moonlight. The light, as it fell on Cassy's large, black eyes, showed a wild glare unlike her wonted fixed despair.

"Come here, Father Tom," she said, laying her small hand on his wrist, drawing him with a force like steel. "Come here,—I've news for you."

"What, Misse Cassy?" said Tom, anxiously.

"Tom, wouldn't you like your liberty?"

"I shall have it, Misse, in God's time," said Tom.

"Ay, but you may have it tonight," she said, with sudden energy. "Come on. He's asleep—sound. I put enough in his brandy to keep him so. I wish I'd had more,—I shouldn't have wanted you. But come; the back door is unlocked; there's an axe there,—his room door is open; I'll show you the way. I'd a done it myself, only my arms are so weak. Come along!"

"Not for ten thousand worlds, Misse!" said Tom, firmly, stopping and holding her back.

"But think of all these poor creatures," said Cassy. "We might set them all free, go to the swamps, find an island, live by ourselves. Any life is better than this."

"No!" said Tom. "No! good never comes of wickedness. I'd sooner chop my right hand off!"

"Then I shall do it," said Cassy, turning.

"O, Misse Cassy!" cried Tom, flinging himself before her, "for the dear Lord's sake that died for ye, don't sell your precious soul to the devil, that way! Nothing but evil will come of it. The Lord hasn't called us to wrath. We must suffer, and wait his time."

"Wait!" said Cassy. "Haven't I waited?—waited till my head is dizzy and my heart sick? What has he made me suffer? What has he made hundreds suffer? Isn't he wringing the life-blood out of you? I'm called on; they call me! His time's come, and I'll have his heart's blood!"

"No, no, no!" said Tom, holding her small hands clenched with spasmodic violence. "No, ye poor, lost soul, that ye mustn't do. The dear, blessed Lord never shed no blood but his own; and that he poured out for us when we was enemies. Lord, help us to follow his steps, and love our enemies."

"Love!" said Cassy, with a fierce glare; "love such enemies! It isn't in flesh and blood."

"No, Misse, it isn't," said Tom, looking up; "but He gives it to us, and that's the victory. When we can love and pray over all and through all, the battle's past, and the victory's come,—glory be to God!"

And this, O Africa! latest called of nations—called to the crown of thorns, the scourge, the bloody sweat—this is to be thy victory; by this shalt thou reign with Christ when his kingdom shall come on earth.

The deep fervor of Tom's feeling, the softness of his voice, his tears, fell like dew on the wild, unsettled spirit. A softness gathered over the lurid fires of her eye; she looked down; the muscles of her hands relaxed.

"Evil spirits follow me," she said, in a low voice; "O! Father Tom, I can't pray,—I wish I could. I never have prayed since my children were sold! What you say must be right; but when I try, I can only hate and curse. I can't pray!"

"Poor soul!" said Tom, compassionately. "Satan desires to have ye, and sift ye as wheat. I pray the Lord for ye. O! Misse Cassy, turn to the dear Lord Jesus. He came to bind up the broken-hearted, and comfort all that mourn."

Cassy stood silent; large, heavy tears dropped from her eyes.

"Misse Cassy," said Tom, hesitating, "if ye only could get away from here,—if the thing was possible,—I'd 'vise ye and Emmeline to do it; that is, if ye could go without blood-guiltiness,—not otherwise."

"Would you try it with us, Father Tom?"

"No," said Tom; "time was when I would; but the Lord's given me a work among these yer poor souls, and I'll stay with 'em, and bear my cross with 'em, till the end. It's different with you; it's a snare to you,—it's more'n you can stand,—and you'd better go, if you can."

"I know no way but through the grave," said Cassy. "There's no beast or bird but finds a home somewhere; even snakes and alligators have their places to lie down and be quiet; but there's no place for us. Down in the darkest swamps their dogs will hunt us out. Everybody and everything is against us; even the very beasts side against us,—and where shall we go?"

Tom was silent; at length he said,

"Him that saved Daniel in the den of lions,—that saved the children in the fiery furnace,—Him that walked on the sea, and bade the winds be still,—He's alive yet; and I've faith to believe he can deliver you. Try it, and I'll pray, with all my might, for you."

By what strange law of mind is it that an idea long trodden under foot suddenly sparkles out as a discovered jewel? Cassy had often revolved schemes of escape and dismissed them as hopeless; but now, in a flash, a plan arose, so simple and feasible as to awaken instant hope.

"Father Tom, I'll try it!" she said, suddenly.

"Amen!" said Tom; "the Lord help ye!"

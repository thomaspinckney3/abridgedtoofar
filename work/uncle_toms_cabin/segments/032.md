The Middle Passage

“Thou art of purer eyes than to behold evil, and canst not look upon iniquity: wherefore lookest thou upon them that deal treacherously, and holdest thy tongue when the wicked devoureth the man that is more righteous than he?”—HAB. 1: 13.

On the lower part of a small, mean boat, on the Red River, Tom sat,—chains on his wrists, chains on his feet, and a weight heavier than chains lay on his heart. All had faded from his sky,—moon and star; all had passed by him, as the trees and banks were now passing, to return no more. Kentucky home, with wife and children, and indulgent owners; St. Clare home, with its refinements and splendors; the golden head of Eva, with its saint-like eyes; the proud, gay, handsome, seemingly careless, yet ever-kind St. Clare; hours of ease and indulgent leisure,—all gone! and in place thereof, what remains?

It is one of the bitterest apportionments of slavery, that the negro, sympathetic and assimilative, after acquiring, in a refined family, the tastes and feelings of such a place, is no less liable to become the bond-slave of the coarsest and most brutal,—just as a chair or table, which once decorated the superb saloon, comes, at last, battered and defaced, to some low haunt of vulgar debauchery. The great difference is, that the table and chair cannot feel, and the man can; for, though “taken, reputed, adjudged in law, to be a chattel personal,” the soul and its little world of memories, hopes, loves, fears, and desires cannot so be blotted out.

Mr. Simon Legree, Tom’s master, had purchased slaves, here and there in New Orleans, to the number of eight, and driven them, handcuffed, down to the steamer Pirate for a trip up the Red River. Having got them fairly on board, and the boat being off, he came round, with that air of efficiency which ever characterized him, to review his property. Stopping opposite to Tom,—who had been attired for sale in his best broadcloth, with well-starched linen and shining boots,—he briefly said:

“Stand up.”

Tom stood up.

“Take off that stock!” And, as Tom, hampered by fetters, proceeded, he helped him by pulling it, with no gentle hand, from his neck, and putting it in his own pocket. He turned to Tom’s trunk, which he had been ransacking, and, taking from it a pair of old pantaloons and a dilapidated coat, said, freeing Tom’s hands a moment and pointing among the boxes, “You go there, and put these on.”

Tom obeyed.

“Take off your boots,” said Mr. Legree. Tom did so.

“There,” said the former, throwing him a pair of coarse, stout shoes, such as were common among the slaves, “put these on.”

In all this hurried exchange, Tom had not forgotten to transfer his cherished Bible to his pocket. It was well he did so; for Mr. Legree, having refitted the handcuffs, proceeded to investigate the contents of his pockets. He drew out a silk handkerchief, and put it into his own. Several little trifles, which Tom had treasured, chiefly because they had amused Eva, he looked upon with a contemptuous grunt, and tossed over his shoulder into the river. Tom’s Methodist hymn-book, which, in his hurry, he had forgotten, he now held up and turned over.

“Humph! pious, to be sure. So, what’s yer name,—you belong to the church, eh?”

“Yes, Mas’r,” said Tom, firmly.

“Well, I’ll soon have that out of you. I have none o’ yer bawling, praying, singing niggers on my place; so remember. Now, mind yourself,” he said, with a stamp and a fierce glare of his gray eye, “I’m your church now! You understand,—you’ve got to be as I say.”

Something within the silent black man answered No! and, as if repeated by an invisible voice, came the words of an old prophetic scroll, as Eva had often read them,—“Fear not! for I have redeemed thee. I have called thee by name. Thou art MINE!”

But Simon Legree heard no voice. He only glared a moment on Tom’s downcast face and walked off. Tom’s neat and abundant wardrobe he carried to the forecastle, where it was soon surrounded by various hands of the boat; with much laughing, at the expense of “niggers that tried to be gentlemen,” the articles were readily sold to one and another, and the empty trunk, at last, put up at auction. The bystanders thought it a good joke; Tom’s look after his things, as they went here and there, and then the auction of the trunk,—that was funnier than all, and occasioned abundant witticisms.

“Now, Tom,” said Simon, sauntering back, “I’ve relieved you of any extra baggage, you see. Take mighty good care of them clothes. It’ll be long enough ’fore you get more. I go in for making niggers careful; one suit has to do for one year, on my place.”

He next walked to where Emmeline, the young girl he had bought, sat chained to another woman.

“Well, my dear,” he said, chucking her under the chin, “keep up your spirits.”

The involuntary look of horror and aversion with which she regarded him did not escape his eye. He frowned.

“None o’ your shines, gal! you’s got to keep a pleasant face, when I speak to ye,—d’ye hear? And you, you old yellow poco moonshine!”—giving a shove to the mulatto woman—“don’t you carry that sort o’ face! You’s got to look chipper, I tell ye!”

“I say, all on ye,” he added, retiring a pace, “look at me,—look at me,—look me right in the eye,—straight, now!” and he stamped, at every pause. By a sort of fascination, every eye turned to his glaring greenish-gray one.

“Now,” said he, doubling his great, heavy fist into something like a blacksmith’s hammer, “d’ye see this fist? Heft it!” and he brought it down on Tom’s hand. “Look at these yer bones! Well, I tell ye this yer fist has got as hard as iron knocking down niggers. I never see the nigger, yet, I couldn’t bring down with one crack,”—and he struck so near Tom’s face that Tom winked and drew back. “I don’t keep none o’ yer cussed overseers; I does my own overseeing; and I tell you things is seen to. You’s every one on ye got to toe the mark,—quick,—straight,—the moment I speak. That’s the way to keep in with me. Ye won’t find no soft spot in me, nowhere. So, now, mind yerselves; for I don’t show no mercy!”

The women involuntarily drew in their breath, and the whole gang sat with downcast, dejected faces. Meanwhile, Simon turned on his heel, and marched up to the bar for a dram.

“That’s the way I begin with my niggers,” he said to a gentlemanly man who had stood by him. “It’s my system to begin strong,—just let ’em know what to expect.”

“Indeed!” said the stranger, regarding him as a naturalist might an out-of-the-way specimen.

“Yes, indeed. I ain’t none o’ yer gentlemen planters, with lily fingers, to slop round and be cheated by some old cuss of an overseer! Just feel o’ my knuckles, now; look at my fist. Tell ye, sir, the flesh on ’t has come jest like a stone, practising on nigger—feel on it.”

The stranger applied his fingers, and simply said,

“’T is hard enough; and, I suppose,” he added, “practice has made your heart just like it.”

“Why, yes, I may say so,” returned Simon, with a hearty laugh. “I reckon there’s as little soft in me as in any one going. Tell you, nobody comes it over me! Niggers never gets round me, neither with squalling nor soft soap,—that’s a fact.”

“You have a fine lot there.”

“Real,” said Simon. “There’s that Tom,—they telled me he was suthin’ uncommon. I paid a little high for him, tendin’ him for a driver and a managing chap; only get the notions out that he’s larnt by bein’ treated as niggers never ought to be, he’ll do prime! The yellow woman I got took in on. I rayther think she’s sickly, but I shall put her through for what she’s worth; she may last a year or two. I don’t go for savin’ niggers. Use up, and buy more, ’s my way;—makes you less trouble, and I’m quite sure it comes cheaper in the end.” And Simon sipped.

“And how long do they generally last?” said the stranger.

“Well, donno; ’cordin’ as their constitution is. Stout fellers last six or seven years; trashy ones gets worked up in two or three. I used to, when I fust begun, have considerable trouble fussin’ with ’em,—doctorin’ on ’em when they’s sick, and givin’ on ’em clothes and blankets, and what not, tryin’ to keep ’em decent and comfortable. Law, ’t warn’t no sort o’ use; I lost money on ’em, and ’t was heaps o’ trouble. Now, you see, I just put ’em straight through, sick or well. When one nigger’s dead, I buy another; and I find it comes cheaper and easier, every way.”

The stranger turned away, and seated himself beside a gentleman, who had been listening with repressed uneasiness.

“You must not take that fellow to be any specimen of Southern planters,” said he.

“I should hope not,” said the young gentleman, with emphasis.

“He is a mean, low, brutal fellow!”

“And yet your laws allow him to hold any number of human beings subject to his absolute will, without even a shadow of protection; and, low as he is, you cannot say there are not many such.”

“Well,” said the other, “there are also many considerate and humane men among planters.”

“Granted,” said the young man; “but, in my opinion, it is you considerate, humane men, that are responsible for all the brutality and outrage wrought by these wretches; because, if it were not for your sanction and influence, the whole system could not keep foothold for an hour. If there were no planters except such as that one,”—he pointed to Legree’s back,—“the whole thing would go down like a millstone. It is your respectability and humanity that licenses and protects his brutality.”

“You certainly have a high opinion of my good nature,” said the planter, smiling; “but I advise you not to talk quite so loud. There are people on board who might not be quite so tolerant to opinion as I am. You had better wait till I get up to my plantation, and there you may abuse us all, quite at your leisure.”

The young gentleman colored and smiled; they were soon busy at backgammon.

Meanwhile, in the lower part of the boat, Emmeline and the mulatto woman with whom she was confined exchanged, as was natural, some particulars of their history.

“Who did you belong to?” said Emmeline.

“Well, my Mas’r was Mr. Ellis,—lived on Levee-street. P’raps you’ve seen the house.”

“Was he good to you?” said Emmeline.

“Mostly, till he tuk sick. He’s lain sick, off and on, more’n six months, and been orful oneasy. ’Pears like he warn’t willin’ to have nobody rest, day nor night; and got so curous, there couldn’t nobody suit him. ’Pears like he just growed crosser, every day; kep me up nights till I got farly beat out, and couldn’t keep awake no longer; and ’cause I got to sleep, one night, Lors, he talk so orful to me, and he tell me he’d sell me to just the hardest master he could find; and he’d promised me my freedom, too, when he died.”

“Had you any friends?” said Emmeline.

“Yes, my husband,—he’s a blacksmith. Mas’r gen’ly hired him out. They took me off so quick, I didn’t even have time to see him; and I’s got four children. O, dear me!” She covered her face with her hands.

Emmeline wanted to say something, but could not think of anything that would not mock the case; and, by a common consent, they both avoided, with fear and dread, all mention of the horrible man who was now their master.

True, there is religious trust for even the darkest hour. The mulatto woman was a member of the Methodist church, and had an unenlightened but very sincere piety. Emmeline had been educated more intelligently,—taught to read and write, and diligently instructed in the Bible, by a faithful and pious mistress; yet would it not try the faith of the firmest Christian to find themselves, seemingly abandoned of God, in the grasp of ruthless violence? How much more must it shake Christ’s poor little ones, weak in knowledge and tender in years!

The boat moved on,—freighted with its weight of sorrow,—up the red, muddy, turbid current, through the abrupt, tortuous windings of the Red River; and sad eyes gazed wearily on the steep red-clay banks, as they glided by in dreary sameness. At last the boat stopped at a small town, and Legree, with his party, disembarked.

In Which the Reader Is Introduced to a Man of Humanity

Late on a chilly February afternoon, in a handsomely furnished dining-parlor in P——, Kentucky, Mr. Shelby sat close with a visitor over wine. The visitor, Mr. Haley, was a short, thick-set, over-dressed trader whose rings, seals, and blue neckerchief jingled and flashed as he talked in easy defiance of Murray’s Grammar, seasoning speech with oaths. Opposite him, Mr. Shelby bore the look of a gentleman, the air of his house testifying to comfort and ease.

“That is the way I should arrange the matter,” said Shelby—meaning to cover a debt by the sale of Uncle Tom.

“I can’t make trade that way,” returned Haley, holding his glass to the light. Shelby urged Tom’s uncommon worth: “steady, honest, capable, manages my whole farm like a clock.” “You mean honest, as niggers go,” said Haley. No—Shelby meant truly pious and trustworthy. He told how Tom, after “getting religion” at a camp-meeting, had been sent alone to Cincinnati with five hundred dollars and come back faithful. Others had tempted him toward Canada; Tom replied, “Ah, master trusted me, and I couldn’t.”

Haley praised the “valeyable” market of such piety—if it was the genuine article. He had “realized six hundred” on one such, and said, “Well, I’ve got just as much conscience as any man in business can afford to keep,—just a little, you know, to swear by, as ’t were”; then pressed for “a boy or gal to throw in with Tom.” Shelby demurred—he could spare none.

Here a little quadroon boy of four or five entered—black hair like floss silk in glossy curls, great dark eyes under long lashes, scarlet-and-yellow plaid frock—comic assurance and bashfulness mingled. “Hulloa, Jim Crow!” whistled Shelby, tossing raisins. The child danced and sang in wild, grotesque fashion, then mimicked old Uncle Cudjoe and Elder Robbins, to roars of laughter. “That chap’s a case,” cried Haley, flipping him orange, and suddenly clapping Shelby’s shoulder: “Fling in that chap, and I’ll settle the business.”

At that instant his mother entered—a young quadroon woman of about twenty-five, every feature proclaiming maternity to the boy. Her rich, full eye, long lashes, and ripples of silky hair flushed under Haley’s bold admiration. Neatly dressed, finely moulded, a delicately formed hand and a trim foot and ankle were items of appearance that did not escape the quick eye of the trader, well used to run up at a glance the points of a fine female article. “Well, Eliza?” said Shelby. “I was looking for Harry, please, sir”—and she withdrew with the child on her arm. “By Jupiter,” said Haley, “there’s an article, now! You might make your fortune on that ar gal in Orleans.” “I don’t want to make my fortune on her,” Shelby said dryly, and turned to fresh wine.

Haley pressed: “Come, how will you trade about the gal?” “She is not to be sold,” said Shelby. “My wife would not part with her for her weight in gold.” “Women always say such things,” Haley laughed; yet he persisted: “You’ll let me have the boy, though?” He proposed to raise “handsome boys for the market—fancy articles entirely,” to stand at rich men’s doors and wait table. Shelby balked: “I hate to take the boy from his mother, sir.” Haley had a humane system: get the girl “off for a day,” do it quietly; buy earrings and a gown to make up. “These critters an’t like white folks, you know; they gets over things, only manage right.”

He enlarged, with becoming diffidence, on the “humanity” of his methods—how other traders pulled screaming infants from mothers’ arms, “very bad policy—damages the article.” He told of “a real handsome gal” in Orleans who went “ravin’ mad” and died in a week when her baby was torn away: “Clear waste, sir, of a thousand dollars, just for want of management; it’s always best to do the humane thing, sir; that’s been my experience.” And the trader leaned back in his chair, and folded his arms, with an air of virtuous decision, apparently considering himself a second Wilberforce. There was something so piquant and original in these elucidations of humanity, that Mr. Shelby could not help laughing in company. Perhaps you laugh too, dear reader; but you know humanity comes out in a variety of strange forms now-a-days, and there is no end to the odd things that humane people will say and do.

They spoke of Tom Loker—“a clever fellow, only the very devil with niggers—on principle”—and of Haley’s quieter, more “paying” system: avoid “onpleasant parts,” keep women “out of sight, out of mind.” He criticized Kentucky masters for “spilin’” their people with expectations: “’Tan’t no kindness to be givin’ on him notions.” Shelby, ill at ease, promised an answer by evening and cautioned secrecy. Alone, he thought: he would have “kicked the fellow down the steps,” but the man had him at advantage. “If anybody had ever said to me that I should sell Tom down south to one of those rascally traders, I should have said, ‘Is thy servant a dog, that he should do this thing?’ And now it must come.” Eliza’s child, too. “So much for being in debt—heigho!”

Perhaps the mildest form of slavery is in Kentucky: quiet agriculture; indulgent masters; affectionate slaves—a scene to tempt one to dream the patriarchal legend. “But over and above the scene there broods a portentous shadow—the shadow of law.” So long as the law counts “all these human beings, with beating hearts and living affections, only as so many things belonging to a master,” so long as misfortune can at any moment turn indulgence to “hopeless misery and toil,” nothing beautiful or desirable can be made in the best administration of slavery.

Mr. Shelby, a fair, kindly man, had speculated loosely; his notes had fallen into Haley’s hands—that was the key to all. Meanwhile, Eliza, passing the door, caught enough to know a trader was making offers—for somebody. She thought she heard an offer for her boy; her heart swelled; she strained him tight, so that he looked up in wonder. In the chamber, her tremor made her upset a pitcher and offer a nightgown for a silk dress. “Eliza, child, what ails you?” asked her mistress. Bursting into tears, Eliza stammered that a trader was with master. “Suppose there has,” said Mrs. Shelby. Would master “sell my Harry?” “Sell him! No, you foolish girl!” Mrs. Shelby assured her husband never dealt with southern traders, never meant to sell servants who behaved well. “Who do you think would want to buy your Harry? Do you think all the world are set on him as you are?” Reassured, Eliza laughed at her fears and braided her mistress’s hair.

Mrs. Shelby, a woman of high moral and religious sensibility, had been sincere in her incredulity. Her husband, who made no professions of religion, nevertheless reverenced the consistency of hers. In fact, if not exactly a believer in the doctrine of the efficiency of the extra good works of saints, he really seemed somehow or other to fancy that his wife had piety and benevolence enough for two—to indulge a shadowy expectation of getting into heaven through her superabundance of qualities to which he made no particular pretension. The heaviest load on him now was to break to her the contemplated arrangement. She, ignorant of his embarrassments, went out for an evening visit, and the matter passed from her mind.

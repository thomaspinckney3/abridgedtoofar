After Tom’s brutal whipping, Cassy secretly brings him water and dresses his wounds in the gin-house; hardened by years of sale and abuse and the loss of her children, she rages that God has forsaken them, but at Tom’s urging she reads of Christ’s forgiveness and, though despairing still, begins to be moved. She hints at a long, bitter history that has made her what she is.

The sitting-room of Legree’s establishment was a long, low, mouldering place with a wide hearth and a brazier of glowing charcoal. Once showy paper now hung in mildewed rags; damp and decay made a sickening odor; beer-stains and chalk scrawls scarred the walls. Saddles, bridles, coats, and whips lay in confused heaps, and the great dogs had made lairs among them. Though the night was not cold, the room was chill and vault-like; the red of the charcoal showed more squalor than comfort.

Legree, mixing punch from a cracked pitcher, grumbled to himself.

“Plague on that driver to kick up a row with the new hands! The fellow won’t be fit to work for a week,—right in the press of the season!”

“Yes, just like you,” said a voice behind him.

He started. It was Cassy, who had stolen upon his soliloquy.

“Hah! you she-devil! you’ve come back, have you?”

“Yes, I have,” she said, coolly; “come to have my own way, too!”

“You lie, you jade! I’ll be up to my word. Either behave yourself, or stay down to the quarters, and fare and work with the rest.”

“I’d rather, ten thousand times,” said the woman, “live in the dirtiest hole at the quarters, than be under your hoof!”

“But you are under my hoof, for all that,” said he, turning on her with a savage grin. “That’s one comfort. So, sit down here on my knee, my dear, and hear to reason,” he added, catching at her wrist.

“Simon Legree, take care!” she said, with a sharp flash of her eye,—so wild a light as to be almost appalling. “You’re afraid of me, Simon,” she added, low and deliberate; “and you’ve reason to be! But be careful, for I’ve got the devil in me!”

He pushed her off, and looked uncomfortable.

“After all, Cassy,” he muttered, “why can’t you be friends with me, as you used to?”

“Used to!” she said, bitterly, and stopped, choked by a surge of feeling.

Long had Cassy kept over him that strange control a strong, impassioned woman will hold even over a brutal man; but of late, galled past endurance, her temper would break into fits of raving that made her terrible to him; for, like most godless men, he was superstitious, and dreaded the insane. When he brought into the house Emmeline, the young girl he had lately bought, the smouldering embers of womanly feeling flashed up in Cassy’s worn heart; she took part with the girl; fierce quarrel followed; he swore she should go to the field; she, with proud scorn, declared she would, and worked there a day to show how little she feared his threats.

The outrage on poor Tom that afternoon had kindled her yet more. She had followed to upbraid him.

“I wish, Cassy,” said Legree, “you’d behave yourself decently.”

“You talk about behaving decently! And what have you been doing?—you, that haven’t even sense enough to keep from spoiling one of your best hands, right in the most pressing season, just for your devilish temper!”

“I was a fool, it’s a fact, to let any such brangle come up,” he said; “but, when the boy set up his will, he had to be broke in.”

“I reckon you won’t break him in!”

“Won’t I?” cried Legree, starting up. “I’d like to know if I won’t? He’ll be the first nigger that ever came it round me! I’ll break every bone in his body, but he shall give up!”

The door opened, and one of the drivers came forward, bowing, and held out a small paper.

“What’s that, you dog?” said Legree.

“It’s a witch thing, Mas’r! Something that niggers gets from witches. Keeps ’em from feelin’ when they’s flogged. He had it tied round his neck with a black string.”

Legree took the paper uneasily, and opened it.

Out dropped a silver dollar, and a long, shining curl of fair hair,—hair which, like a living thing, twined itself about his fingers.

“Damnation!” he screamed, stamping, tearing at the hair as if it burned him. “Where did this come from? Take it off!—burn it up!—burn it up!” He tore it from his hand and flung it into the charcoal. “What did you bring it to me for?”

The driver stared, aghast. Cassy, who had been turning to go, stopped and looked in wonder.

“Don’t you bring me any more of your devilish things!” he shouted, shaking his fist; and, picking up the silver dollar, he sent it through a window-pane into the dark.

The fellow slunk away. Legree sat down again, ashamed of his panic, and sullenly sipped his punch. Cassy slipped noiselessly out, to minister, as we have told, to poor Tom.

And what ailed Legree? What in a simple curl of fair hair could appall a man familiar with every cruelty? We must turn back. There had been a time when he had been rocked on a mother’s bosom,—cradled with prayers and hymns, his brow once wet in holy baptism. A fair-haired New England woman had led her only son, at Sabbath-bell, to worship; with long, unwearied love she had trained him. But, born of a hard-tempered sire, he followed the father’s steps—boisterous, tyrannical—despising counsel, breaking loose for the sea. He came home but once. Then the mother, with that yearning of a heart that must love and has nothing else to love, clung to him with passionate prayers to win him from sin to his soul’s good. That was his day of grace. He relented,—almost persuaded; but sin got the victory. He drank, swore, grew wilder; and, one night, when she knelt in the last agony of despair at his feet, he spurned her,—flung her senseless on the floor, and fled to his ship with curses. The next he heard was a letter thrust into his hand at a midnight revel; a long, curling lock fell from it, and twined about his fingers. The letter told him she was dead; dying, she blessed and forgave him. He burned the hair and burned the letter; and, as they hissed in the flame, he shuddered with thoughts of everlasting fires. He tried to drink and revel the memory away; but often, in the deep night, he saw that pale mother rise by his bed, and felt the soft twining of that hair till the cold sweat oozed and he sprang up in horror. Ye who wonder to hear, in the same evangel, that God is love and that God is a consuming fire—see ye not how, to the soul resolved in evil, perfect love is the most fearful torture?

“Blast it!” muttered Legree, tasting his glass. “Where did he get that? If it didn’t look just like—whoo! I thought I’d forgot that. Hang it! I’m lonesome! I’ll call Em. She hates me—the monkey! I don’t care,—I’ll make her come!”

He stepped into the great entry, where a once superb winding stair now rose bare and dirty among boxes and litter. Moonlight, through a broken fanlight, fell sickly; the air struck like a vault. He stopped, listening. A voice was singing—wild, pathetic—strangely ghostlike in that dreary old house.

“O there’ll be mourning, mourning, mourning,
O there’ll be mourning, at the judgment-seat of Christ!”

“Blast the girl!” he muttered. “I’ll choke her.—Em! Em!” he called, harshly. Only the walls mocked him. The sweet voice went on:

“Parents and children there shall part!
Parents and children there shall part!
    Shall part to meet no more!”

And clear and loud swelled through the empty halls the refrain,

“O there’ll be mourning, mourning, mourning,
O there’ll be mourning, at the judgment-seat of Christ!”

Legree stopped. Great drops stood on his forehead; his heart beat heavy. He thought he saw something white glimmer before him, and shuddered lest the form of his dead mother should rise.

“I know one thing,” he said, stumbling back into the sitting-room, and dropping into his chair; “I’ll let that fellow alone, after this! What did I want of his cussed paper? I b’lieve I’m bewitched! Where did he get that hair? It couldn’t have been that! I burnt that up,—I know I did! It would be a joke, if hair could rise from the dead!”

Ah, Legree! that golden tress was charmed; each hair held a spell of terror and remorse, binding thy cruel hands from uttermost evil on the helpless.

“I say!” he cried, stamping and whistling to the dogs, “wake up, some of you, and keep me company!” They only opened a sleepy eye and dozed again.

“I’ll have the drivers up here, to sing and dance one of their hell-dances, and keep off these notions,” he said; and, putting on his hat, he went out upon the verandah, and blew the horn with which he was wont to summon them.

He was often, in his gracious humors, used to warm these worthies with whiskey, and then amuse himself by setting them to sing, dance, or fight, as his mood took him.

It was between one and two o’clock when Cassy, returning from her tender offices to Tom, heard wild shrieking, whooping, and singing from the sitting-room, with the barking of dogs and a general uproar. She mounted the steps and looked in. Legree and the two black drivers, in furious intoxication, were staggering, whooping, upsetting chairs, making all manner of ludicrous and horrid grimaces.

She rested her small, slender hand on the blind, and looked fixedly on them; there was a world of anguish, scorn, and fierce bitterness in her dark eyes.

“Would it be a sin to rid the world of such a wretch?” she said to herself.

She turned hurriedly away, passed to a back door, glided up the stairs, and tapped at Emmeline’s door.

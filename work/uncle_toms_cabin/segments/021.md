Topsy

One morning, while Miss Ophelia was busy among her domestic cares, St. Clare’s voice was heard at the foot of the stairs.

“Come down here, Cousin; I’ve something to show you.”

“What is it?” said Miss Ophelia, coming down with her sewing in her hand.

“I’ve made a purchase for your department,—see here,” said St. Clare; and, with the word, he pulled along a little negro girl, about eight or nine years of age.

She was one of the blackest of her race; her round shining eyes, glittering as glass beads, moved with quick, restless glances over everything in the room. Her mouth, half open with astonishment at the wonders of the new Mas’r’s parlor, displayed a white, brilliant set of teeth. Her woolly hair was braided in sundry little tails, which stuck out in every direction. Her face wore an odd mixture of shrewdness and cunning, drawn over with a veil of doleful gravity and solemnity. She was dressed in a single filthy, ragged garment made of bagging; and stood with hands demurely folded. Altogether there was something goblin-like about her,—“so heathenish,” Miss Ophelia afterwards said,—as to inspire that good lady with utter dismay; and turning to St. Clare, she said,

“Augustine, what in the world have you brought that thing here for?”

“For you to educate, to be sure, and train in the way she should go. I thought she was a funny specimen in the Jim Crow line. Here, Topsy,” he added, giving a whistle, as a man would to call a dog, “give us a song, now, and show us some of your dancing.”

The black, glassy eyes glittered with wicked drollery; the thing struck up, in a clear shrill voice, an odd negro melody, keeping time with hands and feet,—spinning, clapping, knocking her knees together,—and producing in her throat those guttural sounds which distinguish the native music of her race; finally turning a summerset or two, and giving a prolonged closing note as odd and unearthly as a steam-whistle, she came down on the carpet, hands folded, with a sanctimonious meekness only broken by cunning glances shot from the corners of her eyes.

Miss Ophelia stood silent, paralyzed with amazement. St. Clare, like the mischievous fellow he was, enjoyed her astonishment; and, addressing the child, said,

“Topsy, this is your new mistress. I’m going to give you up to her; see now that you behave yourself.”

“Yes, Mas’r,” said Topsy, with grave sanctity, her wicked eyes twinkling.

“You’re going to be good, Topsy, you understand,” said St. Clare.

“O yes, Mas’r,” said Topsy, hands devoutly folded.

“Now, Augustine, what upon earth is this for?” said Miss Ophelia. “Your house is so full of these little plagues now that a body can’t set down their foot without treading on ’em. I get up, and find one asleep behind the door; see one black head poking from under the table; one lying on the door-mat; and they’re mopping and mowing and grinning between all the railings, tumbling over the kitchen floor! What on earth did you want with this one?”

“For you to educate—didn’t I tell you? You’re always preaching about educating. I thought I’d make you a present of a fresh-caught specimen, and let you try your hand, and bring her up in the way she should go.”

“I don’t want her, I’m sure;—I have more to do with them now than I want.”

“That’s you Christians, all over!—you’ll get up a society, and get some poor missionary to spend all his days among just such heathen. But let me see one of you that would take one into your house, and take the labor of their conversion on yourselves! No; when it comes to that, they are dirty and disagreeable, and it’s too much care, and so on.”

“Augustine, you know I didn’t think of it in that light,” said Miss Ophelia, softening. “Well, it might be a real missionary work,” she added, looking more favorably on the child.

St. Clare had touched the right string. “But,” she added, “I didn’t see the need of buying this one;—there are enough now, in your house, to take all my time and skill.”

“Well, then, Cousin,” said St. Clare, drawing her aside, “I ought to beg pardon for my good-for-nothing speeches. You are so good, after all, that there’s no sense in them. The fact is, this concern belonged to a couple of drunken creatures that keep a low restaurant I pass by every day, and I was tired of hearing her scream, and them beating and swearing at her. She looked bright and funny, too, as if something might be made of her;—so I bought her, and I’ll give her to you. Try, now, and give her a good orthodox New England bringing up, and see what it’ll make of her. I’ve no gift that way; but I’d like you to try.”

“Well, I’ll do what I can,” said Miss Ophelia; and she approached her new subject as one might approach a black spider, with benevolent designs.

“She’s dreadfully dirty, and half naked,” she said.

“Well, take her down stairs, and make some of them clean and clothe her up.”

Miss Ophelia carried her to the kitchen regions.

“Don’t see what Mas’r St. Clare wants of ’nother nigger!” said the cook, surveying the new arrival with no friendly air. “Won’t have her under my feet, I know!”

“Pah!” said two housemaids, with supreme disgust; “let her keep out of our way! What in the world Mas’r wanted another of these low niggers for, I can’t see!”

Miss Ophelia found nobody would undertake the cleansing and dressing; so she was forced to do it herself, with some ungracious, reluctant help. It is not for ears polite to hear the particulars of the first toilet of a neglected, abused child. When she saw on the back and shoulders great welts and calloused spots, ineffaceable marks of the system under which she had grown, her heart became pitiful within her.

The “young un” heard all remarks with her habitual doleful air, only scanning with keen, furtive glances the ornaments the maid wore in her ears. When at last arrayed in decent, whole clothing, hair cropped short, Miss Ophelia, with some satisfaction, said she looked more Christian-like, and in her mind began to mature plans for her instruction. Sitting down, she began to question her.

“How old are you, Topsy?”

“Dun no, Missis,” said the image, with a grin that showed all her teeth.

“Don’t know how old you are? Didn’t anybody ever tell you? Who was your mother?”

“Never had none!” said the child, with another grin.

“Never had any mother? What do you mean? Where were you born?”

“Never was born!” persisted Topsy, with another goblin grin.

“You mustn’t answer me so, child; I’m not playing with you. Tell me where you were born, and who your father and mother were.”

“Never was born,” reiterated she, more emphatically; “never had no father nor mother, nor nothin’. I was raised by a speculator, with lots of others. Old Aunt Sue took car on us.”

“How long have you lived with your master and mistress?”

“Dun no, Missis.”

“Is it a year, or more, or less?”

“Dun no, Missis.”

“Have you ever heard anything about God, Topsy?”

The child looked bewildered, and grinned.

“Do you know who made you?”

“Nobody, as I knows on,” said the child, with a short laugh. Her eyes twinkled. “I spect I grow’d. Don’t think nobody never made me.”

“Do you know how to sew?” said Miss Ophelia, turning to something tangible.

“No, Missis.”

“What can you do?—what did you do for your master and mistress?”

“Fetch water, wash dishes, rub knives, and wait on folks.”

“Were they good to you?”

“Spect they was,” said the child, scanning Miss Ophelia cunningly.

St. Clare, leaning over the back of the chair, said,

“You find virgin soil there, Cousin; put in your own ideas,—you won’t find many to pull up.”

Miss Ophelia’s ideas of education were very set and definite,—New England of a century ago: to teach them to mind; to teach the catechism, sewing, and reading; and to whip them if they told lies. Whatever floods of light now shine, grandmothers raised some tolerably fair men and women under this regime. At all events, Miss Ophelia knew of nothing else; and applied her mind to her heathen with the best diligence she could.

The child was announced as Miss Ophelia’s girl; and, looked on with no gracious eye in the kitchen, was chiefly confined to her mistress’s chamber. With a self-sacrifice some readers will appreciate, Miss Ophelia resolved, instead of comfortably making her own bed, sweeping and dusting her chamber, as she had hitherto done in scorn of help, to condemn herself to instructing Topsy in these operations,—woe the day!

She took Topsy into her chamber the first morning, and solemnly began a course in the art and mystery of bed-making.

“Now, Topsy, I’m going to show you just how my bed is to be made. I am very particular about my bed. You must learn exactly how to do it.”

“Yes, ma’am,” says Topsy, with a deep sigh and a face of woful earnestness.

“This is the hem of the sheet,—this is the right side, and this the wrong;—will you remember?”

“Yes, ma’am,” with another sigh.

“The under sheet you must bring over the bolster,—so,—and tuck it clear down under the mattress, nice and smooth,—so. Do you see?”

“Yes, ma’am,” with profound attention.

“But the upper sheet must be brought down thus, and tucked firm and smooth at the foot,—the narrow hem at the foot.”

“Yes, ma’am,” said Topsy;—and what Miss Ophelia did not see was, that during the zeal of her manipulations, the young disciple had snatched a pair of gloves and a ribbon, slipped them into her sleeves, and stood with hands dutifully folded.

“Now, Topsy, let’s see you do this,” said Miss Ophelia, pulling off the clothes, and seating herself.

Topsy, with gravity and adroitness, went through the exercise to Miss Ophelia’s satisfaction. But an unlucky flutter of ribbon hung out of her sleeve, and caught Miss Ophelia’s eye. Instantly she pounced upon it.

“What’s this? You naughty, wicked child,—you’ve been stealing this!”

The ribbon was pulled out of Topsy’s sleeve; yet she was not the least disconcerted; she only looked at it with surprised innocence.

“Laws! why, that ar’s Miss Feely’s ribbon, an’t it? How could it a got caught in my sleeve?”

“Topsy, you naughty girl, don’t you tell me a lie,—you stole that ribbon!”

“Missis, I declar for ’t, I didn’t;—never seed it till dis yer blessed minnit.”

“Topsy,” said Miss Ophelia, “don’t you know it’s wicked to tell lies?”

“I never tell no lies, Miss Feely,” said Topsy, with virtuous gravity; “it’s jist the truth I’ve been a tellin now, and an’t nothin else.”

“Topsy, I shall have to whip you, if you tell lies so.”

“Laws, Missis, if you’s to whip all day, couldn’t say no other way,” said Topsy, beginning to blubber. “I never seed dat ar;—it must a got caught. Miss Feeley must have left it on de bed, and it got caught in de clothes, and so got in my sleeve.”

Miss Ophelia, indignant at the barefaced lie, caught and shook the child.

“Don’t you tell me that again!”

The shake brought a glove to the floor from the other sleeve.

“There, you!” said Miss Ophelia; “will you tell me now you didn’t steal the ribbon?”

Topsy confessed to the gloves, but persisted about the ribbon.

“Now, Topsy,” said Miss Ophelia, “if you’ll confess all about it, I won’t whip you this time.”

Thus adjured, Topsy confessed to ribbon and gloves, with woful protestations; and, being pressed further, added,

“Laws, Missis! I took Miss Eva’s red thing she wars on her neck.”

“You did, you naughty child!—What else?”

“I took dem yer-rings,—dem red ones,—out o’ de housemaid’s ears.”

“Go bring them to me this minute, both of ’em.”

“Laws, Missis! I can’t,—they ’s burnt up!”

“Burnt up!—what a story! Go get ’em, or I’ll whip you.”

Topsy, with loud protestations and tears, declared she could not. “They ’s burnt up,—they was.”

“What did you burn ’em for?”

“Cause I ’s wicked,—I is. I ’s mighty wicked, any how. I can’t help it.”

Just then Eva came innocently into the room, with the identical coral necklace on her neck.

“Why, Eva, where did you get your necklace?” said Miss Ophelia.

“Get it? Why, I’ve had it on all day,” said Eva. “Had it on yesterday, too; and, what is funny, Aunty, I had it on all night. I forgot to take it off.”

Miss Ophelia looked perfectly bewildered; the more so, as a housemaid came in that instant, with newly-ironed linen poised on her head, and the coral ear-drops shaking in her ears!

“I’m sure I can’t tell what to do with such a child!” said Miss Ophelia, in despair. “What in the world did you tell me you took those things for, Topsy?”

“Why, Missis said I must ’fess; and I couldn’t think o’ nothin’ else to ’fess,” said Topsy, rubbing her eyes.

“But I didn’t want you to confess things you didn’t do,” said Miss Ophelia; “that’s telling a lie, as much as the other.”

“Laws, now, is it?” said Topsy, with innocent wonder.

“There an’t any such thing as truth in that limb,” said the housemaid, indignantly. “If I was Mas’r St. Clare, I’d whip her till the blood run. I would,—I’d let her catch it!”

“No, no,” said Eva, with an air of command she could at times assume; “you mustn’t talk so. I can’t bear to hear it.”

“Miss Eva, you ’s so good, you don’t know nothin’ how to get along with niggers. There’s no way but to cut ’em well up,” muttered the girl.

“Don’t you say another word of that sort!” and the child’s eye flashed, her cheek deepened its color. The maid was cowed.

Eva stood looking at Topsy. There stood the two children, representatives of the two extremes of society: the fair, high-bred child, with golden head, deep eyes, spiritual brow, and prince-like movements; and her black, keen, subtle, cringing, yet acute neighbor. The Saxon, born of ages of cultivation; the Afric, born of ages of oppression.

Something of such thoughts struggled in Eva’s mind,—dim, instinctive. When Miss Ophelia expatiated on Topsy’s wickedness, the child looked perplexed and sorrowful, and said, sweetly,

“Poor Topsy, why need you steal? You’re going to be taken good care of now. I’m sure I’d rather give you anything of mine, than have you steal it.”

It was the first word of kindness the child had ever heard; the tone struck strangely on that wild heart, and a sparkle like a tear shone in the keen round eye; but it was followed by the short laugh and habitual grin. The ear that has never heard anything but abuse is incredulous of so heavenly a thing as kindness.

But what was to be done with Topsy? Miss Ophelia found the case a puzzler; her rules didn’t seem to apply. She resolved to take time to think of it; and, by way of gaining time, and in hopes of indefinite moral virtues supposed to be inherent in dark closets, she shut Topsy up in one, till she had arranged her ideas further.

“I don’t see,” said Miss Ophelia to St. Clare, “how I’m to manage that child, without whipping her.”

“Well, whip her, then, to your heart’s content; I’ll give you full power to do what you like.”

“Children always have to be whipped,” said Miss Ophelia; “I never heard of bringing them up without.”

“O, well, certainly,” said St. Clare; “do as you think best. Only I’ll make one suggestion: I’ve seen this child whipped with a poker, knocked down with shovel or tongs, whichever came handiest, &c.; and, seeing she is used to that style, I think your whippings will have to be pretty energetic, to make much impression.”

“What is to be done with her, then?” said Miss Ophelia.

“You have started a serious question,” said St. Clare. “What is to be done with a human being that can be governed only by the lash,—that fails? It’s a common state of things down here!”

“I’m sure I don’t know; I never saw such a child as this.”

“Such children are very common among us, and such men and women, too. How are they to be governed?”

“I can’t say,” said Miss Ophelia.

“Nor I,” said St. Clare. “The horrid cruelties and outrages that get into the papers,—such cases as Prue’s,—what do they come from? In many cases, a gradual hardening on both sides,—the owner growing more cruel, the servant more callous. Whipping and abuse are like laudanum; you have to double the dose as the sensibilities decline. I saw this very early when I became an owner; and I resolved never to begin, because I did not know when I should stop,—and I resolved, at least, to protect my own moral nature. The consequence is, my servants act like spoiled children; but I think that better than for us both to be brutalized together. You have talked much of responsibilities in educating, Cousin. I wanted you to try with one child, a specimen of thousands among us.”

“It is your system makes such children,” said Miss Ophelia.

“I know it; but they are made,—they exist,—and what is to be done with them?”

“Well, as it appears a duty, I shall persevere, and do the best I can,” said Miss Ophelia.

She instituted regular hours and employments, and undertook to teach her to read and sew.

In the former, the child was quick. She learned her letters as if by magic, and was soon able to read plain reading; but sewing was a more difficult matter. The creature was as lithe as a cat and as active as a monkey; the confinement of sewing was her abomination; so she broke her needles, threw them slyly out of the window or down in chinks; tangled, broke, and dirtied her thread; or, with a sly movement, threw a spool away altogether. Her motions were as quick as a practised conjurer’s, her command of face quite as great; and though Miss Ophelia could not help feeling so many accidents could not happen in succession, yet she could not detect her without a watchfulness which would leave her no time for anything else.

Topsy soon became noted. Her talent for every species of drollery, grimace, and mimicry,—for dancing, tumbling, climbing, singing, whistling, imitating every sound that hit her fancy,—seemed inexhaustible. In play-hours she invariably had every child in the house at her heels,—not excepting Miss Eva, who appeared fascinated by her wild diablerie, as a dove is sometimes charmed by a glittering serpent. Miss Ophelia was uneasy that Eva should fancy Topsy’s society, and implored St. Clare to forbid it.

“Poh! let the child alone,” said St. Clare. “Topsy will do her good.”

“But so depraved a child,—are you not afraid she will teach her mischief?”

“She can’t teach her mischief; she might teach it to some children, but evil rolls off Eva’s mind like dew off a cabbage-leaf,—not a drop sinks in.”

“Don’t be too sure,” said Miss Ophelia. “I’d never let a child of mine play with Topsy.”

“Well, your children needn’t; but mine may. If Eva could have been spoiled, it would have been done years ago.”

The upper servants at first despised Topsy; they soon altered their opinion. It was discovered that whoever cast an indignity on Topsy was sure to meet some inconvenient accident shortly after: a pair of ear-rings or some cherished trinket missing; an article of dress suddenly ruined; a stumble into a pail of hot water; a libation of slop deluging them from above in full gala dress. When investigation was made, there was nobody found to stand sponsor. Topsy was cited time and again; but always sustained her examinations with edifying innocence and gravity. Nobody doubted who did the things; but no scrap of direct evidence could be found, and Miss Ophelia was too just to proceed without it. The mischiefs were always so nicely timed, too, as further to shelter the aggressor,—vengeances chosen when the victims were in disgrace with their mistress, and any complaint would meet no sympathy. In short, Topsy made the household understand the propriety of letting her alone; and she was let alone.

She was smart and energetic in manual operations, learning everything taught with surprising quickness. With a few lessons, she did the proprieties of Miss Ophelia’s chamber in a way with which even that particular lady could find no fault. Mortal hands could not lay spread smoother, adjust pillows more accurately, sweep and dust and arrange more perfectly, than Topsy, when she chose,—but she didn’t very often choose. If, after three or four days of careful supervision, Miss Ophelia supposed Topsy had fallen into her way, could do without over-looking, and so went off, Topsy held a carnival of confusion for some hours. Instead of making the bed, she amused herself pulling off pillow-cases, butting her woolly head among the pillows, grotesquely ornamented with feathers; climbed the posts, hung head downward; flourished sheets and spreads all over; dressed the bolster in Miss Ophelia’s night-clothes, and enacted performances therewith,—singing, whistling, and making grimaces at herself in the looking-glass; in short, as Miss Ophelia phrased it, “raising Cain” generally.

On one occasion Miss Ophelia found Topsy with her very best scarlet India Canton crape shawl wound round her head for a turban, going on with rehearsals before the glass in great style,—Miss Ophelia having, with unheard-of carelessness, left the key for once in her drawer.

“Topsy!” she would say, at the end of patience, “what does make you act so?”

“Dunno, Missis,—I spects cause I ’s so wicked!”

“I don’t know what I shall do with you, Topsy.”

“Law, Missis, you must whip me; my old Missis allers whipped me. I an’t used to workin’ unless I gets whipped.”

“Why, Topsy, I don’t want to whip you. You can do well, if you’ve a mind to; what is the reason you won’t?”

“Laws, Missis, I ’s used to whippin’; I spects it’s good for me.”

Miss Ophelia tried the recipe, and Topsy made a terrible commotion, screaming, groaning and imploring; though, half an hour afterwards, roosted on some balcony projection, surrounded by admiring “young uns,” she expressed the utmost contempt of the affair.

“Law, Miss Feely whip!—wouldn’t kill a skeeter, her whippins. Oughter see how old Mas’r made de flesh fly; old Mas’r know’d how!”

Topsy made great capital of her own sins and enormities, considering them as something peculiarly distinguishing.

“Law, you niggers,” she would say, “does you know you ’s all sinners? Well, you is—everybody is. White folks is sinners, too,—Miss Feely says so; but I spects niggers is de biggest ones; but lor! ye an’t any on ye up to me. I ’s so awful wicked, there can’t nobody do nothin’ with me. I used to keep old Missis a swarin’ at me half de time. I spects I ’s de wickedest critter in de world;” and Topsy would cut a summerset, and come up brisk and shining on a higher perch, evidently pluming herself on the distinction.

Miss Ophelia busied herself on Sundays, teaching Topsy the catechism. Topsy had uncommon verbal memory, and committed with a fluency that encouraged her instructress.

“What good do you expect it is going to do her?” said St. Clare.

“Why, it always has done children good. It’s what children have to learn,” said Miss Ophelia.

“Understand it or not,” said St. Clare.

“O, children never understand it at the time; but, after they are grown up, it’ll come to them.”

“Mine hasn’t come to me yet,” said St. Clare, “though I’ll testify you put it into me pretty thoroughly when I was a boy.”

“Ah, you were always good at learning, Augustine. I used to have great hopes of you,” said Miss Ophelia.

“Well, haven’t you now?”

“I wish you were as good as you were when you were a boy, Augustine.”

“So do I, that’s a fact, Cousin. Well, go ahead and catechize Topsy; may be you’ll make out something yet.”

Topsy, who had stood like a black statue, hands decently folded, now, at a signal, went on:

“Our first parents, being left to the freedom of their own will, fell from the state wherein they were created.”

Topsy’s eyes twinkled; she looked inquiringly.

“What is it, Topsy?” said Miss Ophelia.

“Please, Missis, was dat ar state Kintuck?”

“What state, Topsy?”

“Dat state dey fell out of. I used to hear Mas’r tell how we came down from Kintuck.”

St. Clare laughed.

“You’ll have to give her a meaning, or she’ll make one,” said he. “There seems a theory of emigration suggested there.”

“O! Augustine, be still,” said Miss Ophelia; “how can I do anything if you will be laughing?”

“Well, I won’t disturb the exercises again, on my honor;” and St. Clare took his paper into the parlor, and sat down, till Topsy finished. They were all very well, only now and then she would transpose important words, and persist in the mistake; and St. Clare, after all his promises, took a wicked pleasure in these, calling Topsy to him to repeat the offending passages, in spite of Miss Ophelia’s remonstrances.

“How do you think I can do anything with the child, if you go on so, Augustine?” she would say.

“Well, it is too bad,—I won’t again; but I do like to hear the droll little image stumble over those big words!”

“But you confirm her in the wrong way.”

“What’s the odds? One word is as good as another to her.”

“You wanted me to bring her up right; and you ought to remember she is a reasonable creature, and be careful of your influence.”

“O, dismal! so I ought; but, as Topsy herself says, ‘I ’s so wicked!’”

In very much this way Topsy’s training proceeded, for a year or two,—Miss Ophelia worrying herself from day to day with her, as with a chronic plague, to whose inflictions one becomes accustomed, as to neuralgia.

St. Clare took the same kind of amusement in the child that a man might in the tricks of a parrot or pointer. Whenever her sins brought her into disgrace elsewhere, she took refuge behind his chair; and St. Clare, one way or other, would make peace. From him she got many a stray picayune, which she laid out in nuts and candies, and distributed with careless generosity to all the children; for Topsy, to do her justice, was good-natured and liberal, and only spiteful in self-defence. She is fairly introduced into our corps de ballet, and will figure, from time to time, in her turn, with other performers.

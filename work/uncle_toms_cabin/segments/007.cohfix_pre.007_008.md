Mr. and Mrs. Shelby, after their protracted discussion of the night before, did not readily sink to repose, and, in consequence, slept somewhat later than usual the ensuing morning.

“I wonder what keeps Eliza,” said Mrs. Shelby, after giving her bell repeated pulls, to no purpose.

Mr. Shelby was before his dressing-glass, razor in hand, when the door opened and a colored boy entered with shaving-water.

“Andy,” said his mistress, “step to Eliza’s door, and tell her I have rung for her three times. Poor thing!” she added, with a sigh.

Andy soon returned, eyes wide.

“Lor, Missis! Lizy’s drawers is all open, and her things lyin’ every which way; and I b’lieve she’s just done clared out!”

The truth flashed on both at once. Mr. Shelby exclaimed,

“Then she suspected it, and she’s off!”

“The Lord be thanked!” said Mrs. Shelby. “I trust she is.”

“Wife, you talk like a fool! It will be something pretty awkward for me, if she is. Haley saw I hesitated about selling this child; he’ll think I connived at it. It touches my honor!” And he left the room hastily.

There was running and ejaculating, doors opened and shut, faces of all shades peered everywhere. One person only, who might have shed light, was silent,—the head cook, Aunt Chloe. With a heavy cloud on her face, she went on making breakfast biscuits, as if she saw and heard nothing.

Soon a flock of little roosters were roosting on the verandah rails, every one bent on being first to apprise the strange Mas’r of his ill luck. There was whispering, winking, and proclamations that “he’ll be rael mad, I’ll be bound!” mingled with recollections of his swearing yesterday.

When, at last, Haley appeared, booted and spurred, he was saluted with the tidings on every hand; and the imps were not disappointed of their hope to hear him “swar,” which he did with a fluency and fervency that delighted them, while they dodged his riding-whip and tumbled in a heap under the verandah, kicking up their heels in immeasurable giggle.

“If I had the little devils!” muttered Haley, between his teeth.

“But you ha’nt got ’em, though!” said Andy, with a flourish and a string of indescribable mouths at the trader’s back, once he was beyond hearing.

“I say now, Shelby, this yer’s a most extro’rnary business!” said Haley, abruptly entering the parlor. “It seems that gal’s off, with her young un.”

“Mr. Haley, Mrs. Shelby is present,” said Mr. Shelby.

“I beg pardon, ma’am,” said Haley, bowing with a lowering brow; “but I say this yer’s a sing’lar report. Is it true, sir?”

“Sir,” said Mr. Shelby, “if you wish to communicate with me, you must observe something of the decorum of a gentleman. Andy, take Mr. Haley’s hat and riding-whip. Take a seat, sir. Yes, sir; I regret to say, the young woman, excited by overhearing, or having reported to her, something of this business, has taken her child in the night and made off.”

“I did expect fair dealing in this matter,” said Haley.

“Well, sir,” said Mr. Shelby, turning sharply, “what am I to understand by that remark? If any man calls my honor in question, I have but one answer for him.”

The trader cowered a little, and said, lower, “It’s plaguy hard on a fellow, that had made a fair bargain, to be gulled that way.”

“Mr. Haley,” said Mr. Shelby, “if I did not think you had some cause for disappointment, I should not have borne your rude and unceremonious entrance. I allow of no insinuations upon me, as if I were partner to any unfairness. Moreover, I shall give you every assistance, in the use of horses, servants, &c., for the recovery of your property. So, in short, Haley,”—and he dropped to his ordinary easy frankness,—“the best way is to keep good-natured and eat some breakfast, and then we will see what is to be done.”

Mrs. Shelby rose, said engagements would prevent her being at table, and, deputing a very respectable mulatto woman to attend the gentlemen’s coffee at the sideboard, left the room.

“Old lady don’t like your humble servant, over and above,” said Haley, with an uneasy attempt at familiarity.

“I am not accustomed to hear my wife spoken of with such freedom,” said Mr. Shelby, dryly.

“Beg pardon; of course—only a joke, you know,” stammered Haley.

“Some jokes are less agreeable than others,” rejoined Shelby.

The rumor of Tom’s fate and Eliza’s flight went round the place like fire in stubble. It was the topic everywhere; nothing was done but to discuss what would come of it. Black Sam—so called for being some shades blacker than any other son of ebony there—revolved the matter with a comprehensiveness of vision and a strict lookout to his own well-being, that would have done credit to any white patriot in Washington.

“It’s an ill wind dat blow nowhar,—dat ar a fact,” said Sam, sententiously, giving a hoist to his pantaloons, and adroitly substituting a long nail for a missing suspender-button.

“Yes, it’s an ill wind blows nowhar,” he repeated. “Now, dar, Tom’s down—wal, course der’s room for some nigger to be up—and why not dis nigger?—dat’s de idee. Tom, a ridin’ round—boots blacked—pass in his pocket—all grand as Cuffee—but who he? Now, why shouldn’t Sam?—dat’s what I want to know.”

“Halloo, Sam—O Sam! Mas’r wants you to cotch Bill and Jerry,” cried Andy.

“High! what’s afoot now, young un?”

“Why, you don’t know, I s’pose, that Lizy’s cut stick, and clared out, with her young un?”

“You teach your granny!” said Sam, with infinite contempt. “Knows it a heap sight sooner than you did; dis nigger an’t so green, now!”

“Well, anyhow, Mas’r wants Bill and Jerry geared; and you and I ’s to go with Mas’r Haley, to look arter her.”

“Good, now! dat’s de time o’ day!” said Sam. “It’s Sam dat’s called for in dese yer times. He’s de nigger. See if I don’t cotch her, now; Mas’r’ll see what Sam can do!”

“Ah! but, Sam,” said Andy, “you’d better think twice; for Missis don’t want her cotched, and she’ll be in yer wool.”

“High!” said Sam, opening his eyes. “How you know dat?”

“Heard her say so, my own self, dis blessed mornin’, when I bring in Mas’r’s shaving-water. She sent me to see why Lizy didn’t come; and when I telled her she was off, she jest riz up, and ses she, ‘The Lord be praised;’ and Mas’r, he seemed rael mad, and ses he, ‘Wife, you talk like a fool.’ But Lor! she’ll bring him to! It’s allers best to stand Missis’ side o’ de fence.”

Sam scratched his woolly pate,—which, if it did not contain deep wisdom, held much of that species called “knowing which side the bread is buttered.” After a hitch to his pantaloons—his method for assisting perplexities—he delivered himself gravely:

“Der an’t no sayin’—never—’bout no kind o’ thing in dis yer world,” he said at last, emphasizing this like a philosopher, as if he had tried other worlds.

“Now, sartin I’d a said Missis would a scoured de varsal world after Lizy,” added Sam, thoughtfully.

“So she would,” said Andy; “but can’t ye see through a ladder, ye black nigger? Missis don’t want dis yer Mas’r Haley to get Lizy’s boy; dat’s de go!”

“High!” said Sam, with that indescribable intonation born of his race.

“And ye’d best be makin’ tracks for dem hosses,—mighty sudden,—for I hearn Missis ’quirin’ arter yer,” added Andy.

Sam bestirred himself; and soon came bearing down gloriously with Bill and Jerry in a full canter; adroitly throwing himself off, he brought them up at the horse-post like a tornado. Haley’s horse, a skittish young colt, winced and pulled hard.

“Ho, ho! skeery, ar ye?” said Sam, a mischievous gleam lighting his black visage. “I’ll fix ye now!”

Under the beech that overshadowed the place, the small sharp triangular beech-nuts lay thick. With one between finger and thumb, Sam approached the colt, stroking and soothing; and, on pretence of adjusting the saddle, slipped under it the little torment, so that the least weight would fret the animal’s nerves without leaving mark.

“Dar!” he said, rolling his eyes with approving grin; “me fix ’em!”

Mrs. Shelby appeared on the balcony, beckoning. Sam approached with as good a determination to pay court as ever suitor at St. James’ or Washington.

“Why have you been loitering so, Sam? I sent Andy to hurry you.”

“Lord bless you, Missis! horses won’t be cotched all in a minnit; they’d done clared out to de south pasture, and the Lord knows whar!”

“Sam, how often must I tell you not to say ‘Lord bless you,’ and ‘the Lord knows,’ and such things? It’s wicked.”

“O, Lord bless my soul! I done forgot, Missis! I won’t say nothing of de sort no more.”

“Why, Sam, you just have said it again.”

“Did I? O, Lord! I mean—I didn’t go fur to say it.”

“You must be careful, Sam.”

“Just let me get my breath, Missis, and I’ll start fair. I’ll be bery careful.”

“Well, Sam, you are to go with Mr. Haley, to show him the road, and help him. Be careful of the horses; you know Jerry was a little lame last week; don’t ride them too fast.”

Mrs. Shelby spoke the last words low, with strong emphasis.

“Let dis child alone for dat!” said Sam, rolling up his eyes with a volume of meaning. “Lord knows! High!—didn’t say dat!”—and he caught his breath with such a ludicrous flourish that his mistress laughed, spite of herself. “Yes, Missis, I’ll look out for de hosses!”

“Now, Andy,” said Sam, under the beech, “I wouldn’t be ’t all surprised if dat gen’lman’s crittur should gib a fling, by and by. You know, Andy, critturs will do such things;”—and Sam poked him, suggestive.

“High!” said Andy, with instant appreciation.

“Yes; Missis wants to make time,—dat ar’s clar. I jis make a little for her. Now, you see, get all dese yer hosses loose, caperin’ permiscus, and I spec Mas’r won’t be off in a hurry.”

Andy grinned.

“Yer see,” said Sam, “if Mas’r Haley’s horse should begin to act contrary, and cut up, you and I jist lets go of our’n to help him, and we’ll help him—oh yes!” They threw back their heads and broke into a low, immoderate laugh, snapping fingers and flourishing heels with exquisite delight.

Haley appeared on the verandah, somewhat mollified by cups of very good coffee; he came out smiling and talking. Sam and Andy, clawing for fragmentary palm-leaves they were pleased to call hats, flew to the horse-posts.

“Well, boys,” said Haley, “look alive now; we must lose no time.”

“Not a bit of him, Mas’r!” said Sam, putting the rein in his hand and holding his stirrup, while Andy untied Bill and Jerry.

The instant Haley touched the saddle, the mettlesome creature bounded with a spring that threw his master sprawling on the soft turf. Sam, with frantic ejaculations, made a dive at the reins, but only succeeded in brushing his blazing palm-leaf into the horse’s eyes, which did not allay his confusion. Over went Sam; the white beast, with snorts and whisking heels, pranced away toward the lower lawn, followed by Bill and Jerry, whom Andy had not failed to let loose, speeding them off with direful cries.

Now ensued a miscellaneous scene of confusion. Sam and Andy ran and shouted,—dogs barked here and there,—and all the smaller specimens on the place, male and female, raced, clapped hands, whooped, and shouted with outrageous officiousness. The white horse, fleet and spirited, entered into the sport with gusto; having a lawn of near half a mile, sloping into indefinite woodland, he took delight in letting pursuers come near, then whisking off with a snort, and careered far down some alley of the wood-lot.

Nothing was further from Sam’s mind than to have any one of the troop taken until the season should seem to him most befitting; and the exertions that he made were certainly most heroic. Like the sword of Coeur de Lion, always blazing in the thickest of the battle, Sam’s palm-leaf was to be seen everywhere when there was the least danger that a horse could be caught; there he would bear down, shouting, “Now for it! cotch him! cotch him!” in a way to set everything to rout.

Haley ran up and down, cursed, swore, and stamped miscellaneously. Mr. Shelby in vain shouted directions from the balcony; and Mrs. Shelby from her chamber window alternately laughed and wondered,—not without some inkling of what lay at the bottom.

At last, about twelve o’clock, Sam appeared triumphant, mounted on Jerry, with Haley’s horse by his side, reeking with sweat, but with flashing eyes and dilated nostrils, as if the spirit of freedom had not yet quite subsided.

“He’s cotched!” he exclaimed, triumphantly. “If ’t hadn’t been for me, they might a bust themselves, all on ’em; but I cotched him!”

“You!” growled Haley, in no amiable mood. “If it hadn’t been for you, this never would have happened.”

“Lord bless us, Mas’r,” said Sam, in the deepest concern, “and me that has been racin’ and chasin’ till the sweat pours!”

“Well, well!” said Haley, “you’ve lost me near three hours, with your cursed nonsense. Now let’s be off, and have no more fooling.”

“Why, Mas’r,” said Sam, deprecating, “I b’lieve you mean to kill us all clar, horses and all. Here we is ready to drop, and de critters in a reek. Mas’r won’t think o’ startin’ now till arter dinner. Mas’r’s hoss wants rubben down; see how he splashed hisself; and Jerry limps too; don’t think Missis would be willin’ we should start dis yer way, no how. Lord bless you, Mas’r, we can ketch up, if we do stop. Lizy never was no great walker.”

Mrs. Shelby, who, greatly to her amusement, had overheard, now resolved to do her part. She came forward, courteously expressed concern for Haley’s accident, and pressed him to stay to dinner, saying the cook should bring it on immediately.

All things considered, Haley, with rather an equivocal grace, proceeded to the parlor; while Sam, rolling his eyes after him with unutterable meaning, led the horses to the stable-yard.

“Did yer see him, Andy? did yer see him?” said Sam, once beyond the barn, fastening the horse. “O, Lor, if it warn’t as good as a meetin’, to see him a dancin’ and kickin’ and swarin’ at us. ‘Swar away, ole fellow,’ says I to myself; ‘will yer have yer hoss now, or wait till you cotch him?’ Lor, Andy, I think I can see him now.”

“Yer oughter seen how mad he looked, when you brought de hoss up. Lord, he’d a killed you, if he durs’ to; and there you was a standin’ as innercent and humble,” said Andy.

“Rather specks I am an old hoss,” said Sam. “Did yer see Missis up stars at de winder? I seed her laughin’.”

“I was racin’ so, I didn’t see nothing,” said Andy.

“Well, yer see,” said Sam, proceeding gravely to wash down Haley’s pony, “I ’se ’quired what yer may call a habit o’ bobservation, Andy. It’s a very ’portant habit; and I ’commend yer to be cultivatin’ it, now yer young. Hist up dat hind foot. Yer see, Andy, it’s bobservation makes all de difference in niggers. Didn’t I see which way de wind blew dis yer mornin’? Didn’t I see what Missis wanted, though she never let on? Dat ar’s bobservation. I ’spects it’s what you may call a faculty. Faculties is different in different peoples, but cultivation goes a great way.”

“I guess if I hadn’t helped your bobservation dis mornin’, yer wouldn’t have seen your way so smart,” said Andy.

“Andy,” said Sam, “you’s a promisin’ child, der an’t no manner o’ doubt. I thinks lots o’ yer; and I don’t feel no ways ashamed to take idees from you. We oughtenter overlook nobody, ’cause de smartest on us gets tripped up sometimes. And so, Andy, let’s go up to de house now. I’ll be boun’ Missis’ll give us an uncommon good bite, dis yer time.”

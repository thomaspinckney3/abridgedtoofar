Eliza made her desperate retreat across the river just at dusk. The gray mist rose from the Ohio, and she vanished up the farther bank while the swollen current and floundering ice made a hopeless barrier between her and the man who hunted her. Haley, the trader, turned back, sour and baffled, to the little tavern.

A woman showed him into a parlor with a rag carpet, a black shining oil-cloth on the table, lank high-backed chairs, plaster saints on the mantel above a dimly-smoking grate, and a long settle by the chimney. Here Haley sat down to meditate on the instability of human hopes, and to curse himself in a style too select for rehearsal.

He started at the harsh voice of a man dismounting at the door, and hurried to the window.

“By the land! if this yer an’t the nearest to Providence,” said he. “I do b’lieve that ar’s Tom Loker.”

He hastened out. At the bar stood a brawny, bull-dog sort of fellow in a buffalo-skin coat, hair outward,—Tom Loker by name,—every feature speaking brutal, unhesitating violence. With him came a small, lithe companion, sleek, black-haired, sharp-nosed, with keen, mousing eyes and a dry, cautious air. The great man tossed off raw spirits; the little man ordered a julep, smelt and tasted it with exquisite circumspection, and sipped it in well-advised drops.

“Wal, now, who’d a thought this luck ’ad come to me? Why, Loker, how are ye?” cried Haley, advancing with his hand.

“The devil!” was the civil reply. “What brought you here, Haley?”

The small man ceased sipping, and poked his head forward to survey the newcomer, like a cat watching a fluttering leaf.

“I say, Tom, this yer’s the luckiest thing in the world. I’m in a hobble, and you must help me out.”

“Ugh? aw! like enough!” grunted Loker. “A body may be pretty sure o’ that, when you’re glad to see ’em; something to be made off of ’em. What’s the blow now?”

“You’ve got a friend here?” said Haley, glancing at the small man; “partner, perhaps?”

“Yes, I have. Here’s that ar feller I was in with in Natchez.”

“Shall be pleased with his acquaintance,” said the little one, thrusting out a hand like a raven’s claw. “Mr. Haley, I believe?”

“The same, sir. And now, gentlemen, seein’ as we’ve met so happy, I’ll stand treat in this yer parlor. Hot water, sugar, cigars, and plenty o’ the real stuff!” he called to the landlord. “We’ll have a blow-out.”

Candles were lighted, the grate urged to a glow, and the three worthies sat with their bottles and tumblers. Haley began a pathetic recital of his wrongs; Loker listened, mouth shut, with surly attention; the small man, while compounding his punch with anxious ceremony, from time to time thrust nose and chin almost into Haley’s face, to lose not a word. The end of the tale threw him into a silent shaking laugh; he perked up his thin lips, shoulders heaving with inward enjoyment.

“So, then, ye’r fairly sewed up, an’t ye?” he said. “He! he! It’s neatly done.”

“This young-un business makes lots of trouble in the trade,” groaned Haley.

“If we could raise a breed o’ gals that didn’t care for their young uns,” said the little man; “’t would be ’bout the greatest mod’rn improvement I knows on.” He patronized the joke with a dry sniggle.

“Jes so,” said Haley. “The more trouble a young un is, and the more good for nothin’, as a gen’l thing, the tighter they sticks.”

“Wal, jest so with me,” said the small man. “Once I bought a gal,—tight, likely wench, and considerable smart,—and she had a young un, mis’able sickly, crooked-backed, or somethin’. I gin it away to a man that thought he’d chance raisin’ on ’t, bein’ it didn’t cost nothin’. I never thought o’ the gal’s takin’ on about it,—but, Lord! yer oughter seen her. She cried and lopped round as if she’d lost every friend she had. It re’lly was droll to think on ’t; there ain’t no end to women’s notions.”

“Wal, down on Red River,” put in Haley, “I got a gal traded off on me with a likely lookin’ child. Eyes bright as yourn; but, come to look, stone blind,—fact. So I passes him along, not sayin’ nothin’, and got him swapped for a keg o’ whiskey; but, come to get him away, the gal was jest like a tiger. ’T was afore we started, and I hadn’t my gang chained; so what should she do but ups on a cotton bale, like a cat, ketches a knife, and made all fly for a minnit; and, when she seed ’t warn’t no use, she pitches head first, young un and all, into the river,—went down plump, and never ris.”

“Bah!” growled Loker, who had listened with ill-suppressed disgust; “shif’less, both on ye! My gals don’t cut up no such shines.”

“Indeed! how do you help it?” said the small man, briskly.

“Help it? why, I buys a gal,—if she’s got a young un to be sold, I jest walks up, puts my fist to her face, an’ says, ‘Look here, now, if you give me one word out o’ your head, I’ll smash yer face in. I won’t hear one word—not the beginnin’ of a word. This young un’s mine, and not yourn; I’m goin’ to sell it first chance; mind ye don’t cut up none o’ yer shines, or I’ll make ye wish ye’d never been born.’ They sees ’t an’t no play when I gets hold. I makes ’em as whist as fishes.” He brought down his fist with a thump that explained his meaning.

“That ar’s emphasis,” said the little man, with another giggle. “An’t Tom peculiar? If you an’t the devil, Tom, you ’s his twin brother, I’ll say that for ye!”

Tom received the compliment with a rough complacency.

Haley, warmed by much drink, began to elevate and enlarge his moral faculties.

“Wal, now, Tom,” said he, gesturing from his chair, “ye re’lly is too bad, as I’ve al’ays told ye. Ye know, down in Natchez we used to talk, and I proved to ye we made full as much, and was as well off for this world, by treatin’ on ’em well; besides, keepin’ a better chance for comin’ in the kingdom at last, when wust comes to wust. I b’lieve in religion; and one o’ these days, when I’ve got matters tight and snug, I calculates to tend to my soul; and so what’s the use o’ doin’ any more wickedness than ’s re’lly necessary?—’t an’t prudent.”

“Tend to yer soul!” returned Tom, with contempt. “Take a bright lookout to find a soul in you. If the devil sifts you through a hair sieve, he won’t find one.”

“Why, Tom, you’re cross,” said Haley. “Why can’t ye take it pleasant, when a feller’s talkin’ for yer good?”

“Stop that ar pious jaw,” said Tom, gruffly. “After all, what’s the odds ’twixt me and you? ’Tan’t that you care more,—it’s clean, dog meanness,—wantin’ to cheat the devil and save your own skin. Your gettin’ religion arter all is too p’isin mean; run up a bill with the devil all your life, and then sneak out when pay time comes!”

“Come, come, gentlemen,” interposed the small man; “this isn’t business. There’s different ways o’ lookin’. Mr. Haley’s a nice man, and has his conscience; and, Tom, you have your ways; but quarrellin’ won’t answer. Let’s go to business. Now, Mr. Haley, you want us to undertake to catch this yer gal?”

“The gal’s no matter o’ mine,—she’s Shelby’s. It’s only the boy. I was a fool for buyin’ the monkey.”

“You’re gen’rally a fool,” growled Tom.

“Come, Loker, none o’ your huffs,” purred the small man. “Mr. Haley’s puttin’ us in the way of a job. This yer gal—how is she? what is she?”

“Wal! white and handsome,—well brought up. I’d a gin Shelby eight hundred or a thousand, and made well on her.”

“White and handsome—well brought up!” The small man’s sharp features all started with enterprise. “Look here, now, Loker,—beautiful openin’. We’ll do a business here on our own account; we does the catchin’; the boy, o’ course, goes to Mr. Haley; we takes the gal to Orleans to speculate on. An’t it beautiful?”

Loker’s heavy mouth, that had stood ajar, snapped to like a dog’s on a bone. He brought his fist down till all rang. “It’ll do!”

“Lord bless ye, Tom, save your fist for time o’ need,” said the other. “Now, ye see,” he continued to Haley, stirring his punch, “we has justices convenient at all p’ints, that does up little jobs in our line reasonable. Tom does the knockin’ down; I comes in all dressed—shinin’ boots—first chop—when the swearin’s to be done. One day, I’m Mr. Twickem, from New Orleans; ’nother, a planter from Pearl River, workin’ seven hundred niggers; then a distant relation of Henry Clay. Talents is different. Tom’s a roarer for thumpin’; but at lyin’ he an’t good,—don’t come natural. But as for swearin’ to anything and puttin’ in the circumstances with a long face, I’d like to see the feller beat me. Sometimes I rather wish justices was more particular; ’t would be more relishin’, more fun, yer know.”

“But, gentlemen, an’t I to come in for a share?” said Haley.

“An’t it enough we catch the boy for ye?” said Loker. “What more d’ye want?”

“Wal, if I gives you the job, say ten per cent. on the profits, expenses paid,” ventured Haley.

“Don’t I know you, Dan Haley?” thundered Tom, with an oath, and another blow on the table. “Don’t think to come it over me! S’pose we’ve took up the catchin’ trade jest to ’commodate gentlemen like you, and git nothin’ for ourselves? Not by a long chalk! We’ll have the gal out and out; and you keep quiet, or we’ll have both. What’s to hinder? Han’t you showed us the game? It’s as free to us as you.”

“O, wal, let it go at that,” said Haley, scared. “You catch the boy; you allers did trade far with me, Tom, and was up to yer word.”

“Ye know that,” said Tom. “I won’t lie in my ’counts with the devil himself. What I ses I’ll do, I will do—you know that, Dan Haley.”

“Jes so. Promise to have the boy for me in a week, at any p’int you’ll name,—that’s all I want.”

“It an’t all I want, by a long jump,” said Tom. “I’ve learned to hold an eel, when I catch him. You’ve got to fork over fifty dollars, flat down, or this child don’t start a peg.”

“Why, when the job may bring you a clean profit of a thousand or sixteen hundred, you’re onreasonable,” said Haley.

“Yes—and hasn’t we business booked five weeks ahead? S’pose we leaves all and goes bush-whackin’ arter yer young uns, and don’t catch the gal,—and gals allers is the devil to catch,—what then? Would you pay us a cent? No, no; flap down your fifty. If we get the job, and it pays, I’ll hand it back; if we don’t, it’s for our trouble. That’s far,—an’t it?” said the small man, soothingly. “It’s only a retainin’ fee,—we lawyers, yer know. Keep easy. Tom’ll have the boy for ye anywhere ye’ll name.”

“If I find the young un,” said Loker, “I’ll bring him on to Cincinnati, and leave him where you can call.”

“Now, to the particulars,” resumed the small man, pulling from a greasy pocket-book a long paper, and mumbling over sundry cases of “man and wife—six hundred;” “wench Polly and two children—six hundred for her or her head;” “boy Jim—three hundred, dead or alive.” “Them easy cases can wait. Now, Mr. Haley, you saw the gal land?”

“To be sure,—as plain as I see you.”

“And a man helpin’ her up the bank?” said Tom.

“To be sure, I did.”

“Most likely she’s took in somewheres,” mused the small man; “but where’s the question. Tom?”

“We must cross the river to-night—no mistake,” said Tom.

“But there’s no boat about,” objected the small one. “The ice is runnin’ awfully; an’t it dangerous?”

“Don’ know nothin’ ’bout that—only it’s got to be done,” said Tom.

“Dear me—it’s—” he said, going to the window; “it’s dark as a wolf’s mouth, and, Tom—”

“The long and short is, you’re scared,” said Tom. “But you’ve got to go. Lie by a day, and the gal’ll be on the underground line and up to Sandusky ’fore you start.”

“O, no; I an’t a grain afraid—only—”

“Only what?”

“Well, about the boat. There an’t any.”

“I heard the woman say there was one comin’ along this evenin’, and a man goin’ to cross in it. Neck or nothin’, we must go with him,” said Tom.

“I s’pose you’ve got good dogs,” said Haley.

“First rate,” said the small man; “but what’s the use? you han’t got nothin’ o’ hers to smell on.”

“Yes, I have,” said Haley, triumphantly. “Here’s her shawl; she left it on the bed in her hurry. Her bonnet, too.”

“That ar’s lucky,” said Loker. “Fork over.”

“Though the dogs might damage the gal, if they come on her unawares,” muttered Haley.

“That ar’s a consideration,” said the other. “Our dogs tore a feller half to pieces once, down in Mobile, ’fore we could get ’em off.”

“For this sort that’s to be sold for their looks, that ar won’t answer,” said Haley.

“I do see,” returned the small man. “Besides, if she’s took in, ’tan’t no go neither. Dogs is no ’count up in these yer States; they only does in plantations, where niggers does their own runnin’.”

Loker had stepped to the bar to inquire. “They say the man’s come with the boat,” he reported. The small man cast a rueful look at the warm room, but rose. After a word or two more, Haley, with visible reluctance, counted out the fifty dollars into Tom’s hard palm; and the trio separated for the night.

If any refined and Christian reader object to the company here shown, let him conquer his squeamishness betimes. The catching business, we remind him, rises, under certain constitutions, to the dignity of a lawful and patriotic profession; and if human property persists in its locomotive tendencies, the trader and the catcher may yet sit among our aristocracy.

While this went on at the tavern, Sam and Andy—Shelby’s men—rode home in high felicitation. Sam was in feathers, venting his exultation in whoops, somersets on the saddle, and solemn lectures to Andy for laughing. He kept the horses to their best pace till, between ten and eleven, their heels clattered under the balcony. Mrs. Shelby, the mistress, flew to the railing; Mr. Shelby, her husband, followed.

“Is that you, Sam? Where are they?” she cried.

“Mas’r Haley ’s a-restin’ at the tavern; he’s drefful fatigued, Missis.”

“And Eliza, Sam?”

“Wal, she’s clar ’cross Jordan,—as a body may say,—in the land o’ Canaan.”

“Why, Sam, what do you mean?” gasped Mrs. Shelby, the possible sense of those words blanching her.

“Wal, Missis, de Lord he persarves his own. Lizy’s done gone over de river into ’Hio, as ’markably as ef de Lord took her over in a charrit o’ fire and two hosses.”

“Come up here, Sam,” said Mr. Shelby, passing his arm about his wife, who stood shivering. “You allow yourself to feel too much, Emily.”

“Feel too much! Am not I a woman,—a mother? Are we not both responsible to God for this poor girl? My God! lay not this sin to our charge.”

“What sin, Emily? You see we have only done what we were obliged to.”

“There’s an awful feeling of guilt about it, though,” she said.

“Now, Sam,” said Mr. Shelby, as Sam appeared at the parlor-door, palm-leaf in hand, “tell us distinctly. Where is Eliza?”

“Wal, Mas’r, I saw her, with my own eyes, a crossin’ on de floatin’ ice. She crossed most ’markably; it wasn’t no less nor a miracle; and I saw a man help her up de ’Hio side; den she was lost in de dusk.”

“Sam, I think this rather apocryphal,—this miracle. Crossing on floating ice isn’t so easily done,” said Mr. Shelby.

“Easy! couldn’t nobody a done it without de Lord,” said Sam. “’T was jis dis yer way. Mas’r Haley, and me, and Andy, we comes up to de little tavern by de river. I rides a leetle ahead,—(I’s so zealous to be a cotchin’ Lizy, I couldn’t hold in, no way),—and, when I comes by de winder, sure enough dere she was, right in plain sight, and dey diggin’ on behind. Wal, I loses off my hat, and sings out nuff to raise de dead. Course Lizy she hars, and she dodges back, when Mas’r Haley goes past; and den she clars out de side door, down to de river. Dere was de current runnin’ ten feet wide by de shore, and over t’other side ice a sawin’ and a jiggling up and down, kinder like a great island. We come right behind her; and I thought my soul he’d got her sure,—when she gin sich a screech as I never hearn, and dere she was, clar over t’other side o’ de current, on de ice! and den on she went, a screechin’ and a jumpin’;—de ice went crack! c’wallop! crackin’! chunk!—and she a boundin’ like a buck! Lord, de spring dat ar gal’s got in her an’t common, I’m o’ ’pinion.”

Mrs. Shelby sat white and still, hands clasped. “God be praised, she isn’t dead! But where is the poor child now?”

“De Lord will pervide,” said Sam, rolling up his eyes. “As I’s been a sayin’, dis yer’s a providence. Thar’s allers instruments ris up to do de Lord’s will. Now, if ’t hadn’t been for me to-day, she’d a been took a dozen times. Warn’t it I started off de hosses dis mornin’, and kep ’em chasin’ till nigh dinner? And didn’t I car Mas’r Haley nigh five miles out o’ de road dis evenin’, or else he’d a come up wid Lizy as easy as a dog arter a coon? Dese yer’s all providences.”

“They are a kind of providences you’ll have to be sparing of, Master Sam. I allow no such practices with gentlemen on my place,” said Mr. Shelby, with such sternness as he could command.

There is little use in making believe be angry with a negro or a child; both instinctively see through the veil. Sam, not disheartened, drew down the corners of his mouth in penitential gravity.

“Mas’r quite right,—quite; ’t was ugly on me; and course Mas’r and Missis wouldn’t ’courage no such works. But a poor nigger’s ’mazin’ tempted to act ugly when fellers cuts up sich shines as dat ar Mas’r Haley; he an’t no gen’l’man, no way; anybody’s been raised as I’s been can’t help seein’ dat ar.”

“Well, Sam,” said Mrs. Shelby, “as you appear to have a proper sense of your errors, go now and tell Aunt Chloe she may get you some of that cold ham left from dinner. You and Andy must be hungry.”

“Missis is a heap too good for us,” said Sam, bowing, and departed.

Sam had a native talent for making capital of everything that turned up for his own especial glory. Having done up his piety and humility to the parlor, he cocked his palm-leaf with a rakish air, and proceeded to Aunt Chloe’s dominions, to flourish in the kitchen. There existed between him and Aunt Chloe, the cook, a decided coolness; but, meditating designs on the provision department, Sam came eminently conciliatory,—dwelt on the hardship he had suffered in behalf of a persecuted fellow-creature, and how Missis herself had ordered him to Aunt Chloe for supplies,—thus unequivocally acknowledging her sovereignty in culinary matters.

It took accordingly. No simple, virtuous body was ever more easily cajoled by an electioneering politician than Aunt Chloe was won by Sam’s suavities. In a trice he sat happy and glorious over a large tin pan containing a sort of olla podrida of all that had appeared for two or three days,—savory ham, golden corn-cake, fragments of pie, chicken wings, gizzards, drumsticks,—Sam, monarch of all he surveyed, palm-leaf rakishly askew, patronizing Andy at his right.

The kitchen filled. From cabins round about flocked compeers to hear the day’s exploits. Now was Sam’s hour of glory. The story was rehearsed with all ornament and varnish fit to heighten effect, for Sam never let a tale lose gilding in his hands. Roars of laughter attended; the smaller fry sprawled on floor and perched in corners, squealing; Sam, in the midst, kept an immovable gravity, rolling up his eyes and darting droll glances, without dropping the sententious elevation of his oratory.

“Yer see, fellow-countrymen,” said Sam, elevating a turkey’s leg, “yer see what dis yer chile’s up ter, for fendin’ yer all,—yes, all on yer. For him as tries to git one o’ our people is as good as tryin’ to git all; de principle’s de same,—dat ar’s clar. And any o’ dese yer drivers comes smellin’ arter any our people, why, he’s got me in his way; I’m de feller he’s got to set in wid;—I’ll stand up for yer rights; I’ll fend ’em to de last breath!”

“Why, but, Sam, yer telled me jis dis mornin’ you’d help Mas’r to cotch Lizy; seems to me yer talk don’t hang together,” said Andy.

“I tell you now, Andy,” said Sam, with awful superiority, “don’t yer be a talkin’ ’bout what yer don’t know nothin’ on. Boys like you means well, but dey can’t be spected to collusitate de great principles o’ action.”

Andy looked rebuked, particularly by “collusitate,” which the younger listeners took as a settler, while Sam went on.

“Dat ar was conscience, Andy. When I fust thought o’ gwine arter Lizy, I spected Mas’r was sot dat way. When I found Missis was sot contrar, dat ar was conscience more yet,—’cause fellers gits more by stickin’ to Missis’ side. So yer see I’s persistent either way,—sticks up to conscience, and holds on to principles. Yes, principles,” cried Sam, tossing a chicken’s neck. “What’s principles good for, if we isn’t persistent? Thar, Andy, you may have dat ar bone; ’tan’t picked quite clean.”

“Dis yer matter ’bout persistence,” continued Sam, with the air of entering an abstruse theme, “dis yer ’sistency an’t seed inter very clar by mos’ anybody. Folks ses,—and nat’rally dey ses,—when a feller stan’s up for a thing one day and de contrar de next, why he an’t persistent. Hand me dat bit o’ corn-cake, Andy. But let’s look inter it. S’pose I’s tryin’ to git top o’ de hay. I puts up my larder dis side; ’tan’t no go. Den, ’cause I don’t try dere no more, but puts my larder right de contrar side,—an’t I persistent? I’s persistent in wantin’ to git up, which ary side my larder is. Don’t ye see, all on yer?”

“It’s the only thing ye ever was persistent in, Lord knows!” muttered Aunt Chloe, whom so much merriment affected like vinegar upon nitre.

“Yes, indeed!” said Sam, rising, full of supper and glory, for a closing effort. “Yes, my feller-citizens and ladies o’ de other sex, I has principles,—I’s proud to ’oon ’em; dey’s perquisite to dese yer times, and to all times. I has principles, and I sticks to ’em like forty; jest anythin’ I thinks is principle, I goes in to ’t;—I wouldn’t mind if dey burnt me ’live; I’d walk right up to de stake, and say, ‘Here I comes to shed my last blood fur my principles, fur my country, fur de gen’l interests o’ society.’”

“Well,” said Aunt Chloe, “one o’ yer principles’ll have to be to git to bed some time to-night, and not keep everybody up till mornin’. Now, every one o’ you young uns that don’t want to be cracked had better be scase, mighty sudden.”

“Niggers! all on yer,” said Sam, waving his palm-leaf with benignity, “I give yer my blessin’. Go to bed now, and be good boys.”

With this pathetic benediction, the assembly broke up.

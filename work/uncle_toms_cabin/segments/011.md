The February morning looked gray and drizzling into Uncle Tom’s cabin. The little table stood before the fire under an ironing-cloth; a coarse but clean shirt or two hung on a chair-back; and Aunt Chloe, wiping tears with the back of her hand, rubbed and ironed every fold with scrupulous exactness.

Tom sat by with his Testament open on his knee, head leaning on his hand. It was early; the children still slept in their rude trundle-bed. He rose softly to look at them.

“It’s the last time,” he said.

Aunt Chloe did not answer; she ironed the same seam over and over, set the iron down with a plunge, then sat and lifted up her voice and wept.

“S’pose we must be resigned; but, oh Lord! how ken I? If I know’d anything whar you’s goin’, or how they’d sarve you! Missis says she’ll try and ’deem ye in a year or two; but Lor! nobody never comes up that goes down thar! They kills ’em! I’ve hearn ’em tell how dey works ’em up on dem ar plantations.”

“There’ll be the same God there, Chloe, that there is here.”

“Well,” said Aunt Chloe, “s’pose dere will; but de Lord lets drefful things happen, sometimes. I don’t seem to get no comfort dat way.”

“I’m in the Lord’s hands,” said Tom; “nothin’ can go no furder than he lets it;—and thar’s one thing I can thank him for. It’s me that’s sold and going down, and not you nur the chil’en. Here you’re safe;—what comes will come only on me; and the Lord, he’ll help me,—I know he will.”

“Marcies!” said Aunt Chloe, when he tried to count them; “don’t see no marcy in’t! ’tan’t right! Mas’r never ought ter left it so ye could be took for his debts. Ye’ve arnt him all he gets for ye, twice over. He owed ye yer freedom. Sich a faithful crittur as ye’ve been,—and allers sot his business ’fore yer own! Them as sells heart’s love and heart’s blood, to get out thar scrapes, de Lord’ll be up to ’em!”

“Chloe! if ye love me, ye won’t talk so, when perhaps jest the last time we’ll ever have together! And it goes agin me to hear one word agin Mas’r. Wan’t he put in my arms a baby?—it’s natur I should think a heap of him. Mas’rs is used to havin’ all these yer things done for ’em; nat’lly they don’t think so much on ’t. Set him ’longside other Mas’rs—who’s had the treatment I’ve had? And he never would a let this come on me, if he could a seed it aforehand. I know he wouldn’t.”

“Wal, any way, thar’s wrong about it somewhar,” said Aunt Chloe, sturdy in justice. “I can’t make out whar ’t is, but thar’s wrong somewhar, I’m clar o’ that.”

“Yer ought ter look up to the Lord above—he’s above all—thar don’t a sparrow fall without him.”

“It don’t seem to comfort me, but I spect it orter,” she said; then, forcing herself to bustle, “I’ll jes wet up de corn-cake, and get ye one good breakfast, ’cause nobody knows when you’ll get another.”

To those sold south, every home-love was a chain of pain. “Down river” was told from childhood as the last severity; ignorance peopled it with horrors; and men and women, patient and timid by nature, were nerved to all perils rather than go.

The simple morning meal smoked on the table; Mrs. Shelby had excused Chloe from the great house. She had killed her choicest chicken, baked corn-cake to Tom’s very taste, and brought out long-saved preserves.

“Lor, Pete,” cried Mose, “han’t we got a buster of a breakfast!” and caught at a bit of chicken.

Aunt Chloe boxed his ear. “Thar now! crowin’ over de last breakfast yer poor daddy’s gwine to have to home!”

“O, Chloe!” said Tom, gently.

“Wal, I can’t help it,” she sobbed, hiding her face. “I’s so tossed about, it makes me act ugly.”

The baby clamored to be taken; she wiped her eyes, caught the child up.

“Now I’s done, I hope,—now do eat somethin’. This yer’s my nicest chicken. Thar, boys, ye shall have some, poor critturs! Yer mammy’s been cross to yer.”

After breakfast she put up his clothes.

“Jest like as not, he’ll take ’em all away. Yer flannels for rhumatis is in this corner; be careful, ’cause there won’t nobody make ye no more. Here’s yer old shirts; these yer is new ones. I toed off these yer stockings last night, and put de ball in ’em to mend with. But Lor! who’ll ever mend for ye?” She laid her head on the box-side and sobbed. “To think on ’t! no crittur to do for ye, sick or well! I don’t railly think I ought ter be good now!”

The boys, having eaten all there was, began to whimper; Tom set the baby to crowing and scratching his face, taking what sport he might of her little hands.

“Ay, crow away, poor crittur!” said Chloe. “Ye’ll have to come to it, too! ye’ll live to see yer husband sold, or mebbe be sold yerself; and these yer boys, they’s to be sold, I s’pose, too, when dey gets good for somethin’!”

“Thar’s Missis a-comin’ in!” cried one.

Mrs. Shelby entered; Chloe set a chair for her, gruff and crusty. The lady, pale and anxious, looked on the silent group, sat, and, covering her face, sobbed.

“Lor, now, Missis, don’t—don’t!” burst out Chloe; and for a few moments they all wept together; and in those tears the high and the lowly melted all bitterness.

“My good fellow,” said Mrs. Shelby, “I can’t give you anything to do you any good. If I give you money, it will only be taken from you. But I tell you solemnly, before God, I will keep trace of you, and bring you back as soon as I can command the money;—and, till then, trust in God!”

The boys cried out that Mas’r Haley was coming. An unceremonious kick pushed open the door. Haley stood there, ill-humored from a hard ride and last night’s ill success.

“Come,” said he, “ye nigger, ye’r ready? Servant, ma’am!” He lifted his hat to the lady.

Chloe shut and corded the box, then stood looking at the trader, her tears turned to sparks. Tom rose meekly, shouldered the box. His wife took the baby to go with him to the wagon; the children trailed behind.

Mrs. Shelby detained the trader on the verandah a moment, speaking earnestly; meanwhile all hands from the place gathered about the wagon to bid farewell to their old associate. Tom had been head servant and Christian teacher; honest grief showed most among the women.

“Why, Chloe, you bar it better ’n we do!” said one, who had wept freely, seeing Chloe’s gloomy calm.

“I’s done my tears!” she said, looking grimly at the trader. “I does not feel to cry ’fore dat ar old limb, no how!”

“Get in!” said Haley, striding through the scowling ring. He drew a heavy pair of shackles from under the seat and made them fast around each ankle.

A smothered groan ran through the circle.

“Mr. Haley,” called Mrs. Shelby from the verandah, “that precaution is entirely unnecessary.”

“Don’ know, ma’am; I’ve lost one five hundred dollars from this yer place, and I can’t afford to run no more risks.”

“What else could she spect on him?” muttered Chloe, while the boys clung to her gown, sobbing.

“I’m sorry,” said Tom, “that Mas’r George happened to be away. Give my love to Mas’r George.”

Haley whipped up; Tom’s steady, mournful look clung to the old place till it was lost.

Mr. Shelby was not at home. He had sold Tom under pressing necessity, and, at first, felt relief; then his wife’s expostulations and Tom’s manly disinterestedness made his feelings sore. He told himself he had a right—that everybody did it; yet he could not satisfy his heart, and he had gone up the country on business to be away till all was over.

They rattled along the open pike; after a mile Haley drew up at a blacksmith’s shop, and, taking in a pair of handcuffs, asked a little alteration.

“These yer’s a little too small for his build,” said Haley.

“Lor! now, if thar an’t Shelby’s Tom. He han’t sold him, now?” said the smith.

“Yes, he has,” said Haley.

“Now, ye don’t! Reely—who’d a thought it! Why, ye needn’t go to fetterin’ him this yer way. He’s the faithfullest, best crittur—”

“Yes, yes; but your good fellers is the very critturs to want ter run off. Them stupid ones as doesn’t care whar they go, and shifless, drunken ones, they’ll stick by; but these yer prime fellers, they hates it like sin. No way but to fetter ’em; got legs,—they’ll use ’em.”

“Well,” said the smith, hunting his tools, “them plantations down thar an’t jest the place a Kentuck nigger wants to go. They dies thar tol’able fast, don’t they?”

“Wal, yes, tol’able fast, ther dying is; what with the ’climating and one thing and another, they dies so as to keep the market up brisk,” said Haley.

“Wal, now, a feller can’t help thinkin’ it’s a mighty pity to have a likely, quiet feller, as good un as Tom, go down to be fairly ground up on one o’ them sugar plantations.”

“Wal, he’s got a fa’r chance. I promised to do well by him. I’ll get him in house-servant in some good old family; and if he stands the fever and ’climating, he’ll have a berth good as any nigger ought ter ask for.”

“He leaves his wife and chil’en up here, s’pose?”

“Yes; but he’ll get another thar. Lord, thar’s women enough everywhar,” said Haley.

Tom sat mournfully outside. Suddenly he heard the click of hoofs; young Master George sprang into the wagon, threw his arms round his neck, sobbing and scolding.

“I declare, it’s real mean! I don’t care what they say! It’s a nasty, mean shame! If I was a man, they shouldn’t do it,—they should not, so!” cried George.

“O! Mas’r George! this does me good!” said Tom. “I couldn’t bar to go off without seein’ ye!” He moved his fettered feet; George saw.

“What a shame!” he cried. “I’ll knock that old fellow down—I will!”

“No you won’t, Mas’r George; and you mustn’t talk so loud. It won’t help me any, to anger him.”

“Well, I won’t, for your sake; but only to think of it—isn’t it a shame? They never sent for me; and, if it hadn’t been for Tom Lincon, I shouldn’t have heard it. I tell you, I blew ’em up well, all of ’em, at home!”

“That ar wasn’t right, I’m ’feard, Mas’r George.”

“Can’t help it! I say it’s a shame! Look here, Uncle Tom,” he added low, turning his back to the shop, “I’ve brought you my dollar!”

“O! I couldn’t think o’ takin’ on ’t, Mas’r George, no ways in the world!” said Tom, moved.

“But you shall take it! I told Aunt Chloe I’d do it; and she advised me to make a hole and put a string through, so you could hang it round your neck, and keep it out of sight; else this mean scamp would take it away. I tell ye, Tom, I want to blow him up! it would do me good!”

“No, don’t, Mas’r George, for it won’t do me any good.”

“Well, I won’t, for your sake,” said George, tying the dollar and buttoning Tom’s coat over it. “Keep it; and remember, every time you see it, that I’ll come down after you, and bring you back. Aunt Chloe and I have been talking about it. I’ll tease father’s life out, if he don’t do it.”

“O! Mas’r George, ye mustn’t talk so ’bout yer father!”

“Lor, Uncle Tom, I don’t mean anything bad.”

“And now, Mas’r George,” said Tom, “ye must be a good boy; ’member how many hearts is sot on ye. Al’ays keep close to yer mother. Don’t be gettin’ into them foolish ways boys has o’ gettin’ too big to mind their mothers. The Lord gives good many things twice over; but he don’t give ye a mother but once. Ye’ll never see sich another woman, Mas’r George, if ye live to be a hundred years old. So, hold on to her, and grow up, and be a comfort to her,—thar’s my own good boy,—you will now, won’t ye?”

“Yes, I will, Uncle Tom,” said George, seriously.

“And be careful of yer speaking, Mas’r George. Young boys, when they comes to your age, is wilful, sometimes; but real gentlemen never lets fall words that isn’t ’spectful to thar parents. Ye an’t ’fended, Mas’r George?”

“No, indeed; you always did give me good advice.”

“I’s older,” said Tom, stroking the curly head with a hand as gentle as a woman’s, “and I sees all that’s bound up in you. O, Mas’r George, you has everything,—l’arnin’, privileges, readin’, writin’,—and you’ll grow up a great, learned, good man; and all the people on the place, and your mother and father, ’ll be so proud on ye! Be a good Mas’r, like yer father; and be a Christian, like yer mother. ’Member yer Creator in the days o’ yer youth, Mas’r George.”

“I’ll be real good, Uncle Tom,” said George. “I’m going to be a first-rater; and don’t you be discouraged. I’ll have you back yet. I’ll build our house all over, and you shall have a room for a parlor with a carpet on it, when I’m a man. O, you’ll have good times yet!”

Haley came to the door with the handcuffs.

“Look here, now, Mister,” said George, drawing himself up as he got out, “I shall let father and mother know how you treat Uncle Tom!”

“You’re welcome,” said the trader.

“I should think you’d be ashamed to spend all your life buying men and women, and chaining them, like cattle! I should think you’d feel mean!”

“So long as your grand folks wants to buy men and women, I’m as good as they is,” said Haley; “‘tan’t any meaner sellin’ on ’em than ’t is buyin’!”

“I’ll never do either, when I’m a man,” said George. “I’m ashamed, this day, that I’m a Kentuckian. I always was proud of it before.”

“Well, good-by, Uncle Tom; keep a stiff upper lip.”

“Good-by, Mas’r George,” said Tom, looking fondly at him. “God Almighty bless you! Ah! Kentucky han’t got many like you!”

Away went the boy; Tom looked till the clatter of hoofs died—the last sound or sight of home. Over his heart there seemed a warm spot where the young hands had hung the precious dollar. He put his hand and held it close.

“Now, I tell ye what, Tom,” said Haley, throwing in the handcuffs, “I mean to start fa’r with ye, as I gen’ally do with my niggers. You treat me fa’r, and I’ll treat you fa’r; I an’t never hard on my niggers. If niggers is quiet, and don’t try to get off, they has good times with me; and if they don’t, why, it’s thar fault, and not mine.”

Tom assured him he had no thought of running; the assurance sat superfluous on a man with iron on his feet. But Haley, from habit, commenced his relations with such exhortations, to inspire cheerfulness, as he deemed, and prevent unpleasant scenes.

And here, for the present, we take our leave of Tom, to pursue the fortunes of other characters in our story.

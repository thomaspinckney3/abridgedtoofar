An Evening in Uncle Tom’s Cabin

Uncle Tom’s cabin nestled close by “the house,” the master’s dwelling. A neat garden-patch sprang strawberries, raspberries, vegetables; a scarlet bignonia and multiflora rose curtained the rough logs; Aunt Chloe’s annuals—marigolds, petunias, four-o’clocks—unfolded their splendors to her pride. Enter: the house-supper over, Aunt Chloe, head cook, had come to “get her ole man’s supper.” A round, black, shining face beneath a checked turban; a soul that was cook to the very bone. Barnyard fowl grew grave at her approach, reflecting on their latter end. Corn-cake in all its kinds—hoe-cake, dodgers, muffins—was her sublime mystery.

Within, one corner held a snowy bed with a piece of carpet—Aunt Chloe’s upper walks—the sacred drawing-room; the other corner, a humbler bed for use. Over the fireplace glittered scriptural prints and a portrait of General Washington that would have much astonished that hero. Two woolly-headed boys guided the baby’s first tottering failures, cheering each tumble as cleverness; the table, somewhat rheumatic in its limbs, bore cups and saucers of brilliant pattern, with symptoms of a meal.

At the table sat Uncle Tom, Mr. Shelby’s best hand—large, broad-chested, glossy black; African features softened by steady good sense and benevolence; self-respecting dignity united with confiding simplicity. He patiently copied letters, big fingers moving slowly over a slate, overseen by young Mas’r George, bright boy of thirteen. “Not that way, Uncle Tom,—that makes a q, you see.” “La sakes, now, does it?” Tom admired the flourishing row of g’s and q’s, and recommenced.

“How easy white folks al’us does things!” said Aunt Chloe, greasing a griddle with a scrap of bacon, eyeing George with pride. “The way he can write, now! and read, too! and come out here evenings and read his lessons to us,—mighty interestin’!” “Aunt Chloe, I’m getting mighty hungry,” said George. “Mose done, Mas’r George,” she peeped—“browning beautiful—a real lovely brown.” She scorned Sally’s green hand at cake-making—“cake ris all to one side—no shape at all”—and whisked from the bake-kettle a pound-cake no city confectioner need be ashamed of.

They heaped sausages, batter-cakes. “They wanted me to come to supper in the house,” said George, “but I knew what was what.” “So you did, honey,” Aunt Chloe nudged, bustling. He reached for the cake with a great knife; “La bless you! you wouldn’t be for cuttin’ it wid dat ar great heavy knife! Smash all down—spile all de pretty rise.” Producing a thin old knife “I keeps sharp a purpose,” she feathered the slices. “Tom Lincon says their Jinny is a better cook than you,” George teased. Aunt Chloe tossed her head: “Dem Lincons an’t much count, no way! As to gettin’ up anything in style, they don’t begin. Good Lor! can Missis Lincon sweep into a room like my missis?” Still, in the “good, plain, common cookin’,” Jinny would “make a good pone o’ bread,” “bile her taters far,” but “come to de higher branches”—the flecky paste “as melts in your mouth”—“she can’t.” Aunt Chloe recalled General Knox praising her chicken pie—how she and Missis had “come pretty near quarrelling about dat ar crust,” till she’d said, with saucy reverence, that the Lord “must have meant me to make de pie-crust, and you to stay in de parlor.” Missis had “larfed in her eyes” and let the cook cook.

When even a boy can eat no more, George noticed the pile of glistening eyes from the corner and tossed them bits. Aunt Chloe baked a goodly pile for the children, seated Tom and George by the chimney, settled the baby on Tom’s big shoulder while Mose and Pete roared like bears and Aunt Chloe declared they “fairly took her head off.” She pulled out a trundle-bed—“we’s goin’ to have the meetin’”—then, at George’s word, shoved it under and let the boys sit up.

Chairs were lacking. “Old Uncle Peter sung both de legs out of dat oldest cheer, last week,” Mose said. Barrels were rolled in, wedged with stones, spanned with boards; tubs and pails were turned; the rickety chairs drawn to the wall. “Mas’r George is such a beautiful reader, now, I know he’ll stay to read for us,” Aunt Chloe said. The room filled—from gray patriarchs to lads and girls. There was harmless gossip—Aunt Sally’s new red headkerchief; how Missis would give Lizzy the spotted muslin when the new barège was made; a new sorrel colt Mr. Shelby thought of buying. Then singing commenced—fine, wild voices, even through nasal intonations. They sang:

“Die on the field of battle,
Die on the field of battle,
    Glory in my soul.”

And oft repeated:

“O, I’m going to glory,—won’t you come along with me?
Don’t you see the angels beck’ning, and a calling me away?
Don’t you see the golden city and the everlasting day?”

They sang of Jordan’s banks, Canaan’s fields, the New Jerusalem; some laughed, some cried, some clapped hands, as if they had crossed the river. An old gray-headed woman, leaning on her staff, testified: “Well, chil’en! I’m mighty glad to hear ye and see ye once more, ’cause I don’t know when I’ll be gone to glory; but I’ve done got ready—my little bundle all tied up, and my bonnet on, jest a waitin’ for the stage to come along and take me home. Sometimes, in the night, I think I hear the wheels a rattlin’. Now, you jest be ready too; for dat ar glory is a mighty thing!” The circle struck up: “O Canaan, bright Canaan, I’m bound for the land of Canaan.”

Mas’r George read the last chapters of Revelation, interrupted by “The sakes now!” “Only hear that!” “Jest think on ’t!” Well trained by his mother, he ventured sober expositions; “a minister couldn’t lay it off better,” they said. Uncle Tom, patriarch in religious matters, exhorted with simple, hearty sincerity; but it was in prayer that he excelled—touching simplicity, childlike earnestness—Scripture “dropping from his lips unconsciously”; he “prayed right up,” so that responses threatened to drown him.

While devotion breathed in the cabin of the man, another scene passed in the master’s hall. The trader and Mr. Shelby sat at a table spread with papers and pens. Mr. Shelby counted bundles of bills, pushing them across; Haley counted likewise. “All fair,” said the trader. “And now for signing these yer.” Shelby hastily signed the bills of sale “like a man that hurries over some disagreeable business,” and pushed them over with the money. Haley drew a parchment from his well-worn valise, glanced, handed it to Shelby, who took it with suppressed eagerness. “Wal, now, the thing’s done!” said the trader, rising. “It’s done!” repeated Mr. Shelby, musingly; fetching a long breath, “It’s done!”

“Yer don’t seem to feel much pleased with it,” Haley observed. “Haley,” said Shelby, “I hope you’ll remember that you promised, on your honor, you wouldn’t sell Tom, without knowing what sort of hands he’s going into.” “Why, you’ve just done it, sir,” said the trader. “Circumstances, you well know, obliged me,” Shelby returned, haughtily. “Wal, you know, they may ’blige me, too,” said Haley. “Howsomever, I’ll do the very best I can in gettin’ Tom a good berth; as to my treatin’ on him bad, you needn’t be a grain afeard. If there’s anything that I thank the Lord for, it is that I’m never noways cruel.” After such expositions of “humanity,” Mr. Shelby was not reassured; but, as it was the best comfort the case admitted, he let the trader go and betook himself to a solitary cigar.

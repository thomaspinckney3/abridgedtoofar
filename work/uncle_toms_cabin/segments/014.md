A quiet scene now rises before us. A large, neatly-painted kitchen, the yellow floor glossy and smooth, not a particle of dust; a well-blacked cooking-stove; rows of shining tin, suggestive of unmentionable good things; old, glossy green chairs; a small flag-bottomed rocker with a patch-work cushion, and a larger one, motherly and wide, whose hospitable arms seconded the solicitation of its feather cushions,—a real comfortable, persuasive old chair, worth, in the way of honest, homely enjoyment, a dozen of your plush drawing-room gentry. In it, gently swaying, her eyes bent on fine sewing, sat our friend Eliza—paler and thinner than in her Kentucky home, with a world of quiet sorrow under the shadow of her long eyelashes, the girlish heart grown firm beneath heavy discipline; and when, anon, her large dark eye rose to follow the gambols of little Harry, sporting, like some tropical butterfly, hither and thither over the floor, there was in it a depth of steady resolve unknown to her earlier, happier days.

By her sat a woman with a bright tin pan in her lap, sorting dried peaches. Fifty-five or sixty, perhaps; but hers was one of those faces that time touches only to brighten. The snowy lisse crape cap of the strait Quaker pattern, the plain white muslin handkerchief lying in placid folds, the drab shawl and dress, told their community. Round and rosy, with a soft, healthful bloom; smooth-parted hair, partially silvered, over a high, placid brow where time had written only peace on earth, good will to men; beneath, a large pair of clear, honest, loving brown eyes, into which to look was to see to the bottom of a true heart. So much is said and sung of beautiful young girls; why not of beautiful old women? We refer the doubter to our good friend Rachel Halliday, as she sits in her little rocking-chair. It had a turn for quacking and squeaking,—a subdued “creechy crawchy,”—but old Simeon declared it was as good as any music to him, and the children avowed they would not miss mother’s chair for anything. For why? For twenty years and more, nothing but loving words, gentle moralities, motherly kindness had come from it; head-aches and heart-aches cured there; difficulties, spiritual and temporal, solved there,—all by one good, loving woman,—God bless her!

“And so thee still thinks of going to Canada, Eliza?” said Rachel, quietly turning her peaches.

“Yes, ma’am,” said Eliza, firmly. “I must go onward. I dare not stop.”

“And what’ll thee do when thee gets there? Thee must think about that, my daughter.”

“My daughter” came naturally from Rachel’s lips; hers was the face and form that made “mother” the most natural word in the world. Eliza’s hands trembled; tears fell on her work; but she answered, firm still,—

“I shall do—anything I can find. I hope I can find something.”

“Thee knows thee can stay here, as long as thee pleases,” said Rachel.

“O, thank you,” said Eliza, “but—” she pointed to Harry—“I can’t sleep nights; I can’t rest. Last night I dreamed I saw that man coming into the yard,” she shuddered.

“Poor child!” said Rachel, wiping her eyes. “But thee mustn’t feel so. The Lord hath ordered it so that never hath a fugitive been stolen from our village. I trust thine will not be the first.”

The door opened, and a little short, round, pin-cushiony woman came in, with a cheery, blooming face, like a ripe apple,—dressed, like Rachel, in sober gray. This was Ruth Stedman. She dusted her little drab bonnet, coaxed a wayward curl beneath the Quaker cap, and turned, well pleased,—as most might be who looked at her; for she was a wholesome, whole-hearted, chirruping little woman.

“Ruth, this friend is Eliza Harris; and this is the little boy I told thee of.”

“I am glad to see thee, Eliza,—very,” said Ruth, shaking hands as if with an old acquaintance. “And this is thy dear boy,—I brought a cake for him.”

“Where’s thy baby, Ruth?” said Rachel.

“O, he’s coming; but thy Mary caught him as I came in, and ran off with him to the barn, to show him to the children.”

Mary, an honest, rosy girl, with large brown eyes like her mother’s, soon appeared with a great, white, fat fellow in her arms.

“Ah! ha!” said Rachel, taking him. “How good he looks, and how he does grow!”

Ruth untied hood and wrappers with brisk fingers, kissed him, set him on the floor to collect his thoughts; the little thumb went into the little mouth, as if it were quite a thing of course; and the mother, seating herself, drew out a long stocking of mixed blue and white yarn and fell to knitting.

“Mary, thee’d better fill the kettle, hadn’t thee?” suggested Rachel.

Mary filled it; soon it purred and steamed, a censer of hospitality; the peaches, by gentle hint, were set to stew; Rachel took down a snowy moulding-board, tied on an apron, and fell quietly to making biscuits. “Mary, hadn’t thee better tell John to get a chicken ready?” and Mary disappeared accordingly. Compassion and neighborly care ran, like a spring, through their speech: who had sat up with the sick, who would bake and mend on the morrow; and, “John can come in here to his meals, if thee needs to stay all day,” said Rachel.

Simeon Halliday, tall, straight, muscular, in drab coat and broad-brimmed hat, now entered.

“How is thee, Ruth?” he said, warmly, offering his broad hand. “And how is John?”

“Any news, father?” said Rachel, putting biscuits into the oven.

“Peter Stebbins told me they should be along tonight with friends,” said Simeon, significantly, washing at the neat back-porch sink.

“Indeed!” said Rachel, thoughtfully, glancing at Eliza.

“Did thee say thy name was Harris?” said Simeon to Eliza, reentering.

Eliza tremulously answered, “Yes.”

“Mother!” said Simeon, calling Rachel into the porch.

“What does thee want, father?” said Rachel, rubbing floury hands.

“This child’s husband is in the settlement, and will be here tonight.”

“Now, thee doesn’t say that, father?” and all Rachel’s face grew radiant.

“It’s really true. Peter was down yesterday to the other stand, and found an old woman and two men; one said his name was George Harris; and, from what he told of his history, I am certain. He is a bright, likely fellow, too.”

“Shall we tell her now?” said Simeon.

“Let’s tell Ruth,” said Rachel. “Here, Ruth,—come here.”

“Ruth, what does thee think? Father says Eliza’s husband is in the last company, and will be here tonight.”

At that, the little Quakeress gave such a bound, clapping her hands, that two stray curls fell on her white neckerchief.

“Hush thee, dear!” said Rachel. “Hush, Ruth! Tell us, shall we tell her now?”

“Now! to be sure,—this very minute. Why, now, suppose ’t was my John, how should I feel? Do tell her, right off.”

“Thee uses thyself only to learn how to love thy neighbor, Ruth,” said Simeon, beaming.

“To be sure. Isn’t it what we are made for? If I didn’t love John and the baby, I should not know how to feel for her. Come, now do tell her,—do! Take her into thy bed-room, there, and let me fry the chicken while thee does it.”

Rachel went in to Eliza, and, opening the door of a small bed-room, said, gently, “Come in here with me, my daughter; I have news to tell thee.”

The blood flushed in Eliza’s pale face; she rose, trembling; looked toward her boy.

“No, no,” cried little Ruth, darting up, seizing her hands. “Never thee fear; it’s good news, Eliza,—go in, go in!” And, turning, she caught Harry and began kissing him. “Thee’ll see thy father, little one. Does thee know it? Thy father is coming.”

Within, Rachel drew Eliza toward her, and said, “The Lord hath had mercy on thee, daughter; thy husband hath escaped from the house of bondage.”

The blood flushed to Eliza’s cheek, and ebbed to her heart. She sat down, pale and faint.

“Have courage, child,” said Rachel, laying a hand on her head. “He is among friends, who will bring him here tonight.”

“Tonight!” Eliza repeated. “Tonight!” The word lost meaning; all went misty.

When she awoke, she found herself snugly tucked up, a blanket over her, little Ruth rubbing her hands with camphor. She lay in a dreamy, delicious languor, such as one who has long borne a load and now feels it gone, and would rest. The tension, never ceased since the first hour of her flight, gave way; a strange feeling of security and rest came over her; and as she lay, eyes open, she followed, as in a quiet dream, the motions about her: the door open into the other room; the supper-table, snowy cloth; the dreamy murmur of the singing tea-kettle; Ruth tripping with plates of cake and saucers of preserves, stopping to put a cake into Harry’s hand, to pat his head, to twine a curl round her snowy fingers; the ample, motherly form of Rachel, who ever and anon came to smooth something, to give a tuck here and there, expressing good-will; the sunshine of her clear brown eyes. Ruth’s husband came in; she flew to whisper him, pointing toward the room; they all sat at tea; little Harry in a high chair under Rachel’s ample wing; low murmurs of talk; gentle tinkling of tea-spoons; musical clatter of cups,—and Eliza slept, as she had not slept since that fearful midnight when she fled through the frosty starlight.

She dreamed of a beautiful country,—a land of rest,—green shores, pleasant islands, glittering water; and there, in a house kind voices told her was a home, she saw her boy free and happy. She heard her husband’s footsteps; felt him coming nearer; his arms were round her, his tears falling on her face,—and she awoke! It was no dream. The daylight had long faded; her child lay calmly sleeping; a candle burned dimly on the stand; and her husband was sobbing by her pillow.

The next morning was a cheerful one at the Quaker house. “Mother” was up betimes, surrounded by busy girls and boys, obedient to Rachel’s gentle “Thee had better,” or more gentle “Hadn’t thee better?” in the getting of breakfast; for a breakfast in the luxurious valleys of Indiana is a thing complicated and multiform. While John ran to the spring for water, Simeon the second sifted meal for corn-cakes, and Mary ground coffee, Rachel moved quietly,—making biscuits, cutting up chicken,—diffusing a sort of sunny radiance. If zeal jostled zeal, her “Come! come!” or “I wouldn’t, now,” allayed it. We have read of the cestus of Venus that turned heads; we had rather have the cestus of Rachel Halliday, that kept heads from being turned, and made everything go on harmoniously.

While all went sociably and well, Simeon the elder stood in his shirt-sleeves before a little glass, engaged in the anti-patriarchal operation of shaving. Even the knives and forks had a social clatter; the chicken and ham a cheerful fizzle in the pan, as if they rather enjoyed being cooked than otherwise;—and when George and Eliza and little Harry came out, such a hearty, rejoicing welcome met them, no wonder it seemed like a dream.

At last, they were all seated, while Mary stood at the stove, baking griddle-cakes, which, as they gained the exact golden-brown tint, were transferred to the table.

Rachel never looked so benignly happy as at the head of her table. There was so much motherliness even in the way she passed a plate or poured a cup, it seemed to put a spirit into the food and drink.

It was the first time George had sat down on equal terms at any white man’s table; he sat at first with some constraint, which melted off, like fog, in the genial rays of this simple kindness. This, indeed, was a home,—home,—a word George had never yet known a meaning for; and a belief in God, and trust in his providence, began to encircle his heart, as, with a golden cloud of protection and confidence, dark, misanthropic, pining doubts and fierce despair melted away before the light of a living Gospel, breathed in living faces, preached by a thousand unconscious acts of love and good will.

“Father, what if thee should get found out again?” said Simeon second, buttering his cake.

“I should pay my fine,” said Simeon, quietly.

“But what if they put thee in prison?”

“Couldn’t thee and mother manage the farm?” said Simeon, smiling.

“Mother can do almost everything,” said the boy. “But isn’t it a shame to make such laws?”

“Thee mustn’t speak evil of thy rulers, Simeon,” said his father, gravely. “The Lord only gives us our worldly goods that we may do justice and mercy; if our rulers require a price of us for it, we must deliver it up.”

“Well, I hate those old slaveholders!” broke out the boy.

“I am surprised at thee, son,” said Simeon. “Thy mother never taught thee so. I would do even the same for the slaveholder as for the slave, if the Lord brought him to my door in affliction.”

Simeon second blushed scarlet; his mother only smiled. “Simeon is my good boy; he will grow older, by and by, and then he will be like his father.”

“I hope, my good sir, that you are not exposed to any difficulty on our account,” said George, anxiously.

“Fear nothing, George, for therefore are we sent into the world. If we would not meet trouble for a good cause, we were not worthy of our name.”

“But, for me,” said George, “I could not bear it.”

“Fear not, then, friend George; it is not for thee, but for God and man, we do it,” said Simeon. “And now thou must lie by quietly this day, and tonight, at ten o’clock, Phineas Fletcher will carry thee onward to the next stand,—thee and the rest of thy company. The pursuers are hard after thee; we must not delay.”

“If that is the case, why wait till evening?” said George.

“Thou art safe here by daylight,” said Simeon; “for every one in the settlement is a Friend, and all are watching. It has been found safer to travel by night.”

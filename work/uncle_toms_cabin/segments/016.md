Of Tom’s New Master, and Various Other Matters

Since the thread of our humble hero’s life has now become interwoven with that of higher ones, it is necessary to give some brief introduction to them.

Augustine St. Clare was the son of a wealthy planter of Louisiana. The family had its origin in Canada. Of two brothers, very similar in temperament and character, one had settled on a flourishing farm in Vermont, and the other became an opulent planter in Louisiana. The mother of Augustine was a Huguenot French lady, whose family had emigrated to Louisiana during the days of its early settlement. Augustine and another brother were the only children of their parents. Having inherited from his mother an exceeding delicacy of constitution, he was, at the instance of physicians, during many years of his boyhood, sent to the care of his uncle in Vermont, to brace his health in a severer climate.

In childhood, he was remarkable for an extreme and marked sensitiveness of character, more akin to the softness of woman than the ordinary hardness of his own sex. Time overgrew this with the rough bark of manhood, yet few knew how living it lay at the core. His talents were of the first order, though his mind always preferred the ideal and the æsthetic, with a repugnance to the actual business of life which often attends that balance of faculties. Soon after college, his whole nature was kindled into one intense effervescence of romantic passion. His hour came,—the hour that comes only once; his star rose,—that star that rises so often in vain, to be remembered only as a thing of dreams; and it rose for him in vain. To drop the figure,—he saw and won the love of a high-minded and beautiful woman, in one of the northern states, and they were affianced. He returned south to make arrangements for their marriage, when, most unexpectedly, his letters were returned to him, with a short note from her guardian, stating that ere it reached him the lady would be the wife of another.

Stung to madness, he vainly hoped, as many another has done, to fling the whole thing from his heart by one desperate effort. Too proud to supplicate or seek explanation, he threw himself at once into a whirl of fashionable society, and in a fortnight from the time of the fatal letter was the accepted lover of the reigning belle of the season; and, as soon as arrangements could be made, he became the husband of a fine figure, a pair of bright dark eyes, and a hundred thousand dollars; and, of course, everybody thought him a happy fellow.

In the full tide of gay company, a letter in that well-remembered hand was brought to him. He changed color, preserved his composure, finished his playful badinage, and then was missed. Alone, he read the letter, now worse than idle and useless to be read. It told how fraud had stood between them,—letters intercepted, importunities urged, health failing, hope dying,—till the truth, at last, was discovered. It ended with hope, thankfulness, undying affection,—more bitter than death to him. He wrote:

“I have received yours,—but too late. I believed all I heard. I was desperate. _I am married_, and all is over. Only forget,—it is all that remains for either of us.”

And thus ended the romance and ideal of life for Augustine St. Clare. But the _real_ remained,—the _real_, like the flat, bare, oozy tide-mud, when the blue sparkling wave, with all its company of gliding boats and white-winged ships, its music of oars and chiming waters, has gone down, and there it lies, flat, slimy, bare,—exceedingly real.

In novels, people’s hearts break, and they die, and that is the end; and in a story this is very convenient. But in life we do not die when all that makes life bright dies to us. The busy round of eating, drinking, dressing, walking, visiting, buying, selling, talking, reading, must be gone through; and this remained to Augustine.

Had his wife been a whole woman, she might, as woman can, have done something to mend the broken threads. But Marie St. Clare could not even see that they had been broken. As before stated, she consisted of a fine figure, a pair of splendid eyes, and a hundred thousand dollars; and none of these ministered to a mind diseased. When Augustine, pale as death, pleaded sick-headache, she recommended hartshorn; when the paleness and headaches recurred, she said it was unfortunate for her, because he didn’t like going into company with her, and it seemed odd to go so much alone, when they were just married.

As the honeymoon gloss wore away, he discovered that a beautiful young woman, brought up to be caressed and waited on, might prove quite a hard mistress at home. Marie never had much capability of affection or sensibility, and the little she had was merged in a most intense unconscious selfishness,—the more hopeless from its quiet obtuseness. From infancy, servants had studied her caprices; the idea that they had feelings or rights had never dawned upon her. Her father, whose only child she was, had never denied her anything; so, entering life, beautiful, accomplished, and an heiress, she saw all the eligibles and non-eligibles sigh at her feet, and had no doubt Augustine was most fortunate in having obtained her. It is a mistake to suppose that a woman with no heart will be an easy creditor. There is not on earth a more merciless exactor of love than a thoroughly selfish woman; and the more unlovely she grows, the more jealously she exacts it. When St. Clare dropped off the gallantries which had flowed from the habitude of courtship, he found his sultana nothing ready to resign her slave. There were tears, poutings, small tempests, discontents, upbraidings. He, good-natured and self-indulgent, sought to buy off with presents and flatteries; and when Marie became mother to a beautiful daughter, he felt, for a time, something like tenderness awakened.

St. Clare’s mother had been a woman of uncommon elevation and purity; he gave his child her name, fondly fancying a reproduction of her image. This had been remarked with petulant jealousy by Marie, who regarded his devotion to the child with suspicion; all that was given to her seemed so much taken from herself. From the birth of the child, her health gradually sunk. A life of bodily and mental inaction,—the friction of ceaseless ennui and discontent,—with ordinary weakness of maternity,—in a few years changed the blooming belle into a faded, sickly woman, whose time was divided among a variety of fanciful diseases, and who considered herself, in every sense, the most ill-used person in existence.

Her complaints were endless; but her forte lay in sick-headache, confining her to her room perhaps three days in six. As, of course, all family arrangements fell to servants, St. Clare found his menage anything but comfortable. His only daughter was exceedingly delicate, and he feared that, with no one to look after her, her health might fall a sacrifice to her mother’s inefficiency. He had taken her to Vermont, and persuaded his cousin, Miss Ophelia St. Clare, to return with him to his southern residence; and they are now returning on this boat, where we introduce them.

And now, while the distant domes and spires of New Orleans rise to our view, there is yet time for an introduction to Miss Ophelia.

Whoever has travelled in New England will remember, in some cool village, the large farmhouse, with its clean-swept grassy yard, shaded by the massive sugar-maple; the air of order and stillness, of perpetuity, that seemed to breathe over the whole place. Nothing lost, or out of order; not a picket loose in the fence, not a particle of litter in the yard, with its clumps of lilac under the windows. Within, wide, clean rooms, where nothing ever seems to be doing or going to be done; everything forever rigidly in place; all household arrangements moving with the punctual exactness of the old clock in the corner. In the family keeping-room, the staid, respectable old book-case, with glass doors, where Rollin, Milton, Bunyan, and Scott’s Family Bible stand side by side in decorous order, with multitudes of other books, solemn and respectable. There are no servants; the lady in the snowy cap, with spectacles, sits sewing every afternoon among her daughters, as if nothing ever had been done, or were to be done,—she and her girls, in some long-forgotten fore part of the day, “did up the work,” and, for the rest of the time, probably, at all hours when you would see them, it is “done up.” The old kitchen floor never seems stained; tables, chairs, various utensils, never disordered; though meals are got there, washing and ironing there performed, and though pounds of butter and cheese somehow there come into existence.

On such a farm, in such a house and family, Miss Ophelia had spent a quiet existence of some forty-five years, when her cousin invited her to his southern mansion. The eldest of a large family, she was still considered, by father and mother, as one of “the children,” and the proposal that she should go to Orleans was momentous to the family circle. The old father took down the atlas and looked out the latitude and longitude, and read travels to make up his mind as to the country. The good mother inquired anxiously “if Orleans wasn’t an awful wicked place,” seeming to think it most equal to going among heathen.

It was known at the minister’s, the doctor’s, and at Miss Peabody’s milliner shop, that Ophelia St. Clare was “talking about” going away down to Orleans; and of course the whole village could do no less than help this important process. The minister, inclining to abolitionist views, doubted whether such a step might not encourage southerners; while the doctor, a stanch colonizationist, held that Miss Ophelia ought to go, to show the Orleans people that we don’t think hardly of them, after all; southern people needed encouraging. When it was fully known she had resolved to go, she was solemnly invited to tea by all friends and neighbors for a fortnight, and her plans duly canvassed. The dress-maker, who came in to help, acquired daily accessions of importance from the developments with regard to Miss Ophelia’s wardrobe. It was credibly ascertained that Squire Sinclare, as his name was commonly contracted, had counted out fifty dollars, and told her to buy any clothes she thought best; that two silk dresses and a bonnet had been sent for from Boston; and, what divided opinion as to propriety, there had been no such parasol seen in those parts; there were rumors of a hemstitched pocket-handkerchief,—even that one had lace all around; but this last point remains unsettled to this day.

Miss Ophelia, as you now behold her, stands in a shining brown linen travelling-dress, tall, square-formed, angular. Her face thin and rather sharp; the lips compressed, as if she made up her mind definitely on all subjects; the keen, dark eyes with a peculiarly searching, advised movement, travelling over everything, as if looking for something to take care of. All her movements were sharp, decided, energetic; and, though never much of a talker, her words were remarkably direct, and to the purpose, when she spoke. In habits, she was a living impersonation of order, method, and exactness. In punctuality, inevitable as a clock, inexorable as a railroad engine; holding in decided contempt anything of a contrary character. The great sin of sins, in her eyes,—the sum of all evils,—was expressed by one common word in her vocabulary—“shiftlessness.” Her finale and ultimatum of contempt consisted in a very emphatic pronunciation of the word “shiftless;” and by this she characterized all modes of procedure not directly and inevitably related to some purpose then in mind.

As to mental cultivation,—she had a clear, strong, active mind, well and thoroughly read in history and the older English classics, and thought with great strength within certain narrow limits. Her theological tenets were made up, labelled, and put by, like the bundles in her patch trunk; just so many of them, and never to be any more. So, also, her ideas on most matters of practical life,—housekeeping, and the various political relations of her native village. And, underlying all, deeper than anything else, higher and broader, lay the strongest principle of her being—conscientiousness. Miss Ophelia was the absolute bond-slave of the “_ought_.” Once make her certain that the “path of duty,” as she phrased it, lay in any direction, and fire and water could not keep her from it.

But how can Miss Ophelia get along with Augustine St. Clare,—gay, easy, unpunctual, unpractical, sceptical,—walking with nonchalant freedom over every one of her most cherished habits and opinions? To tell the truth, Miss Ophelia loved him. When a boy, it had been hers to teach him his catechism, mend his clothes, comb his hair, and bring him up generally in the way he should go; and her heart, having a warm side, Augustine had, as he usually did with most people, monopolized a large share of it. He easily persuaded her that the “path of duty” lay in the direction of New Orleans,—she must go to take care of Eva, and keep everything from wreck during his wife’s frequent illnesses. The idea of a house without anybody to take care of it went to her heart; she loved the lovely little girl, and though she regarded Augustine as very much of a heathen, yet she loved him, laughed at his jokes, and forbore his failings more than those who knew him thought credible.

There she is, now, in her state-room, surrounded by carpet-bags, boxes, baskets, each with some separate responsibility, tying, binding up, packing, fastening, with a face of great earnestness.

“Now, Eva, have you kept count of your things? Of course you haven’t,—children never do: there’s the spotted carpet-bag and the little blue band-box with your best bonnet,—that’s two; then the India rubber satchel is three; and my tape and needle box is four; and my band-box, five; and my collar-box; and that little hair trunk, seven. What have you done with your sunshade? Give it to me, and let me put a paper round it, and tie it to my umbrella with my shade;—there, now.”

“Why, aunty, we are only going up home;—what is the use?”

“To keep it nice, child; people must take care of their things, if they ever mean to have anything; and now, Eva, is your thimble put up?”

“Really, aunty, I don’t know.”

“Well, never mind; I’ll look your box over,—thimble, wax, two spools, scissors, knife, tape-needle; all right,—put it in here. What did you ever do, child, when you were coming on with only your papa. I should have thought you’d a lost everything you had.”

“Well, aunty, I did lose a great many; and then, when we stopped anywhere, papa would buy some more of whatever it was.”

“Mercy on us, child,—what a way!”

“It was a very easy way, aunty,” said Eva.

“It’s a dreadful shiftless one,” said aunty.

“Why, aunty, what’ll you do now?” said Eva; “that trunk is too full to be shut down.”

“It _must_ shut down,” said aunty, with the air of a general, as she squeezed the things in, and sprung upon the lid;—still a little gap remained.

“Get up here, Eva!” said Miss Ophelia, courageously; “what has been done can be done again. This trunk has _got to be_ shut and locked—there are no two ways about it.”

The hasp snapped; she turned the key and pocketed it in triumph.

“Now we’re ready. Where’s your papa? I think it time this baggage was set out. Do look out, Eva, and see if you see your papa.”

“O, yes, he’s down the other end of the gentlemen’s cabin, eating an orange.”

“He can’t know how near we are coming,” said aunty; “hadn’t you better run and speak to him?”

“Papa never is in a hurry about anything,” said Eva. “Do step on the guards, aunty. Look! there’s our house, up that street!”

The boat began, with heavy groans, to push up among the steamers at the levee. Eva joyously pointed out spires and domes. Miss Ophelia had an eye on the baggage.

The usual turmoil of landing ensued—waiters running, men tugging trunks, women calling, everybody crowding to the plank. Miss Ophelia seated herself resolutely on the lately vanquished trunk, marshalling her goods in military order, resolved to defend them to the last.

“Shall I take your trunk, ma’am?” “Shall I take your baggage?” showered upon her unheeded. She sat upright as a darning-needle stuck in a board, holding her bundle of umbrella and parasols, and wondering “what upon earth her papa could be thinking of; he couldn’t have fallen over, now,” till St. Clare came up, with his usual careless motion, and giving Eva a quarter of the orange, said,

“Well, Cousin Vermont, I suppose you are all ready.”

“I’ve been ready, waiting, nearly an hour,” said Miss Ophelia. “I began to be really concerned about you.”

“That’s a clever fellow, now,” said he. “Well, the carriage is waiting, and the crowd are now off, so one can walk out in a decent and Christian manner, and not be pushed and shoved. Here,” he added to a driver, “take these things.”

“I’ll go and see to his putting them in,” said Miss Ophelia.

“O, pshaw, cousin, what’s the use?” said St. Clare.

“Well, at any rate, I’ll carry this, and this, and this,” said Miss Ophelia, pouncing on three boxes and a small carpet-bag.

“My dear Miss Vermont, positively you mustn’t come the Green Mountains over us that way. You must adopt at least a piece of a southern principle, and not walk out under all that load. They’ll take you for a waiting-maid; give them to this fellow; he’ll put them down as if they were eggs, now.”

She looked despairingly as her treasures were taken; but rejoiced to find herself once more in the carriage with them, in a state of preservation.

“Where’s Tom?” said Eva.

“O, he’s on the outside, Pussy. I’m going to take Tom up to mother for a peace-offering, to make up for that drunken fellow that upset the carriage.”

“O, Tom will make a splendid driver, I know,” said Eva; “he’ll never get drunk.”

The carriage stopped before an ancient mansion, built in the odd mixture of Spanish and French style seen in some parts of New Orleans. In Moorish fashion, a square building enclosed a court-yard, into which the carriage drove through an arched gateway. The inside court had been arranged to gratify a picturesque and voluptuous ideality. Wide galleries ran around the four sides, with Moorish arches, slender pillars, and arabesque ornaments. In the center a fountain threw high its silvery water into a marble basin fringed with violets; the pellucid water was alive with myriads of gold and silver fishes. A walk of pebbled mosaic encircled it; turf, smooth as green velvet, lay beyond; orange-trees, fragrant with blossoms, threw shade; marble vases of arabesque sculpture held the choicest tropical flowers; pomegranates with flame-colored blooms, Arabian jessamines with silvery stars, luxuriant roses bending under their abundance, golden jessamines, lemon-scented verbenum; and here and there a mystic aloe sat like some old enchanter among the perishable bloom.

Curtains of Moorish stuff festooned the galleries, to be drawn at pleasure against the sun. The whole had a luxurious and romantic air.

As the carriage drove in, Eva seemed like a bird ready to burst from a cage, with the wild eagerness of delight.

“O, isn’t it beautiful, lovely! my own dear, darling home!” she said to Miss Ophelia. “Isn’t it beautiful?”

“’T is a pretty place,” said Miss Ophelia, as she alighted; “though it looks rather old and heathenish to me.”

Tom got down, and looked about with calm, still enjoyment. The negro is an exotic of the most gorgeous countries, with, deep in his heart, a passion for all that is splendid, rich, fanciful; a passion which, rudely indulged by an untrained taste, draws on them the ridicule of the colder white race.

St. Clare, in heart a poetical voluptuary, smiled at Miss Ophelia’s remark, and, turning to Tom, whose beaming face was radiant with admiration, said,

“Tom, my boy, this seems to suit you.”

“Yes, Mas’r, it looks about the right thing,” said Tom.

Trunks were hustled off, the hackman paid, and a crowd, of all ages and sizes, came running through the galleries, above and below, to see Mas’r come in. Foremost was a highly-dressed young mulatto valet, in the ultra extreme of the mode, gracefully waving a scented handkerchief, exerting himself with great alacrity to drive the flock of domestics to the other end of the verandah.

“Back! all of you. I am ashamed of you,” he said, with an elegant air. “Would you intrude on Master’s domestic relations, in the first hour of his return?”

The people fell back, huddled at a respectful distance, except two stout porters, who conveyed away the baggage. Owing to the valet’s systematic arrangements, when St. Clare turned from paying the hackman, there was nobody in view but the valet himself, conspicuous in satin vest and white pants, bowing with inexpressible grace.

“Well, well,” said St. Clare, with negligent drollery, “that’s very well got up. See that the baggage is well bestowed. I’ll come to the people in a minute;” and, so saying, he led Miss Ophelia to a large parlor that opened on the verandah.

Eva had flown like a bird to a little boudoir opening likewise on the verandah.

“Mamma!” cried she, in rapture, throwing herself on a tall, dark-eyed, sallow woman, half risen from a couch, and embracing her over and over.

“That’ll do,—take care, child,—don’t, you make my head ache,” said the mother, languidly kissing her.

St. Clare came in, embraced his wife in orthodox husbandly fashion, and then presented his cousin. Marie lifted her large eyes with some curiosity, and received her with languid politeness. A crowd of servants pressed to the entry door; among them a middle-aged mulatto woman, of very respectable appearance, stood foremost, trembling with expectation and joy.

“O, there’s my mammy!” said Eva, flying across the room; and, throwing herself into her arms, she kissed her repeatedly.

This woman did not tell her that she made her head ache, but, on the contrary, hugged her and laughed and cried, till her sanity was to be doubted; and when released, Eva flew from one to another, shaking hands and kissing, in a way that Miss Ophelia afterwards declared fairly turned her stomach.

“Well!” said Miss Ophelia, “you southern children can do something that I couldn’t.”

“What, now, pray?” said St. Clare.

“Well, I want to be kind to everybody, and I wouldn’t have anything hurt; but as to kissing—”

“Niggers,” said St. Clare, “that you’re not up to,—hey?”

“Yes, that’s it. How can she?”

St. Clare laughed, as he went into the passage.

“Halloa, here, what’s to pay out here? Here, you all—Mammy, Jimmy, Polly, Sukey—glad to see Mas’r?” he said, shaking hands from one to another. “Look out for the babies!” he added, as he stumbled over a sooty little urchin crawling on all fours. “If I step upon anybody, let ’em mention it.”

There was laughing, blessing Mas’r, as he distributed small pieces of change.

“Come, now, take yourselves off, like good boys and girls,” he said; and the whole assemblage disappeared into the large verandah, followed by Eva, who carried a large satchel, filled during her homeward journey with apples, nuts, candy, ribbons, laces, toys of every description.

As St. Clare turned back his eye fell upon Tom, standing uneasily, shifting from foot to foot, while the valet leaned negligently against the banisters, examining Tom through an opera-glass, with an air any dandy might envy.

“Puh! you puppy,” said his master, striking down the glass; “is that the way you treat your company? Seems to me, sir,” he added, laying his finger on the elegant figured satin vest the fellow was sporting, “seems to me that’s _my_ vest.”

“O! Master, this vest all stained with wine; of course, a gentleman in Master’s standing never wears a vest like this. It does for a poor nigger-fellow, like me.”

“So, that’s it, is it?” said St. Clare, carelessly. “Well, here, I’m going to show this Tom to his mistress, and then you take him to the kitchen; and mind you don’t put on any of your airs to him. He’s worth two such puppies as you.”

“Here, Tom,” said St. Clare, beckoning.

Tom entered. He looked wistfully on the velvet carpets, and the before unimagined splendors of mirrors, pictures, statues, curtains, and, like the Queen of Sheba before Solomon, there was no more spirit in him. He looked afraid even to set his feet down.

“See here, Marie,” said St. Clare to his wife, “I’ve bought you a coachman, at last, to order. I tell you, he’s a regular hearse for blackness and sobriety, and will drive you like a funeral, if you want. Open your eyes, now, and look at him. Now, don’t say I never think about you when I’m gone.”

Marie opened her eyes, and fixed them on Tom, without rising.

“I know he’ll get drunk,” she said.

“No, he’s warranted a pious and sober article.”

“Well, I hope he may turn out well,” said the lady; “it’s more than I expect, though.”

“Show Tom down stairs,” said St. Clare to the valet; “and, mind yourself; remember what I told you.”

The fellow tripped forward, and Tom, with lumbering tread, went after.

“He’s a perfect behemoth!” said Marie.

“Come, now, Marie,” said St. Clare, seating himself on a stool beside her sofa, “be gracious, and say something pretty to a fellow.”

“You’ve been gone a fortnight beyond the time,” said the lady, pouting.

“Well, you know I wrote you the reason.”

“Such a short, cold letter!” said she.

“Dear me! the mail was just going, and it had to be that or nothing.”

“That’s just the way, always,” said Marie; “always something to make your journeys long, and letters short.”

“See here, now,” he added, drawing an elegant velvet case from his pocket, and opening it, “here’s a present I got for you in New York.”

It was a daguerreotype, clear and soft as an engraving, representing Eva and her father sitting hand in hand.

Marie looked at it with a dissatisfied air.

“What made you sit in such an awkward position?” she said.

“Well, the position may be a matter of opinion; but what do you think of the likeness?”

“If you don’t think anything of my opinion in one case, I suppose you wouldn’t in another,” said the lady, shutting the case.

“Hang the woman!” said St. Clare, mentally; aloud he added, “Come, now, Marie, what do you think of the likeness? Don’t be nonsensical, now.”

“It’s very inconsiderate of you, St. Clare,” said the lady, “to insist on my talking and looking at things. You know I’ve been lying all day with the sick-headache; and there’s been such a tumult made ever since you came, I’m half dead.”

“You’re subject to the sick-headache, ma’am!” said Miss Ophelia, suddenly rising from the deep arm-chair, where she had sat quietly, taking an inventory of the furniture, and calculating its expense.

“Yes, I’m a perfect martyr to it,” said the lady.

“Juniper-berry tea is good for sick-headache,” said Miss Ophelia; “at least, Auguste, Deacon Abraham Perry’s wife, used to say so; and she was a great nurse.”

“I’ll have the first juniper-berries that get ripe in our garden by the lake brought in for that special purpose,” said St. Clare, gravely pulling the bell; “meanwhile, cousin, you must be wanting to retire to your apartment, and refresh yourself a little, after your journey. Tell the nurse to come here.”

The decent mulatto woman whom Eva had caressed so rapturously soon entered; she was neatly dressed, with a high red and yellow turban on her head, a recent gift of Eva.

“I put this lady under your care,” said St. Clare; “she is tired, and wants rest; take her to her chamber, and be sure she is made comfortable.”

And Miss Ophelia disappeared in the rear of the nurse.

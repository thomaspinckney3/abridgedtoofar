The Young Master

Two days after, a young man drove a light wagon up the avenue of China trees, flung the reins on the horse’s neck, sprang out, and inquired for the owner.

It was George Shelby; and how he came there must be briefly told.

Miss Ophelia’s letter to Mrs. Shelby, by some unlucky detention at a remote post-office, lay for months before it reached its mark; and, ere it was received, Tom had vanished into the distant swamps of Red River. Mrs. Shelby read with sore anxiety; but immediate action was impossible. Her husband lay delirious in the crisis of a fever; and Master George, grown from boy to tall youth, was her sole reliance in ordering his father’s affairs. Guided by the name of the St. Clare lawyer, which Miss Ophelia had thoughtfully sent, they wrote; but the answer came—he knew nothing. Tom had been sold at public auction; the money was paid; there the knowledge ended. Then Mr. Shelby died; and the absorbing pressure of settling large and tangled estates fell, by his appointment, on his wife as sole executrix. With characteristic energy, she and George set themselves to reduce all to order; yet neither could rest easy. Six months later, business taking him down the river, George resolved to go to New Orleans, push inquiry in person, and, if possible, restore their old friend.

After weary search, by the merest chance, he met a man who knew; and, money in his pocket, he took steamboat for Red River, resolved to find and re-purchase Tom.

He was soon introduced into the house, where he found Legree in the sitting-room. Legree received him with a kind of surly hospitality.

“I understand,” said the young man, “that you bought, in New Orleans, a boy, named Tom. He used to be on my father’s place, and I came to see if I couldn’t buy him back.”

Legree’s brow darkened, and he broke out, passionately: “Yes, I did buy such a fellow,—and a h—l of a bargain I had of it, too! The most rebellious, saucy, impudent dog! Set up my niggers to run away; got off two gals, worth eight hundred or a thousand apiece. He owned to that; and, when I bid him tell me where they was, he up and said he knew, but he wouldn’t tell; and stood to it, though I gave him the cussedest flogging I ever gave nigger yet. I b’lieve he’s trying to die; but I don’t know as he’ll make it out.”

“Where is he?” said George, impetuously. “Let me see him.” His cheek crimsoned, and his eye flashed; but he prudently held in his words.

“He’s in dat ar shed,” said a little fellow, who stood holding George’s horse.

Legree kicked the boy, and swore at him; but George, without reply, strode to the spot.

Tom had lain two days since the fatal night,—not suffering; every nerve of pain was blunted and destroyed. For the most part, he was in a quiet stupor; for the strong, well-knit frame did not at once release the imprisoned spirit. By stealth, in the darkness, poor desolated creatures had come, filching from their scanty rest, to repay, with the cup of cold water given with full hearts, some of those ministrations of love he had so abundantly bestowed. Tears had fallen on the honest, insensible face,—tears of late repentance in poor, ignorant heathen, whom his dying patience had awakened,—and bitter prayers to a late-found Saviour, whom they scarce knew more than by the name, yet whom the yearning, ignorant heart never implores in vain. Cassy, gliding from her concealment, and, by overhearing, learning the sacrifice made for her and Emmeline, had dared detection to come; and, moved by the last few words his affectionate soul had strength to breathe, the long winter of despair—the ice of years—broke; and the dark, despairing woman wept and prayed.

When George entered, his head reeled, and his heart sickened.

“Is it possible,—is it possible?” said he, kneeling. “Uncle Tom, my poor, poor old friend!”

Something in the voice pierced the dying ear. He moved his head gently, smiled, and said,

“Jesus can make a dying-bed
Feel soft as down pillows are.”

Tears, that did honor to his manly heart, fell from the young man’s eyes, as he bent over him.

“O, dear Uncle Tom! do wake,—do speak once more! Look up! Here’s Mas’r George,—your own little Mas’r George. Don’t you know me?”

“Mas’r George!” said Tom, opening his eyes, feebly. “Mas’r George!” He looked bewildered. Slowly the idea filled his soul; the vacant eye fixed and brightened; the whole face lighted; the hard hands clasped; tears ran down.

“Bless the Lord! it is,—it is,—it’s all I wanted! They haven’t forgot me. It warms my soul; it does my heart good! Now I shall die content! Bless the Lord, on my soul!”

“You shan’t die! you mustn’t die, nor think of it! I’ve come to buy you, and take you home,” cried George, with impetuous vehemence.

“O, Mas’r George, ye’re too late. The Lord’s bought me, and is going to take me home,—and I long to go. Heaven is better than Kintuck.”

“O, don’t die! It’ll kill me!—it’ll break my heart to think what you’ve suffered,—and lying in this old shed, here! Poor, poor fellow!”

“Don’t call me poor fellow!” said Tom, solemnly. “I have been poor fellow; but that’s all past and gone, now. I’m right in the door, going into glory! O, Mas’r George! Heaven has come! I’ve got the victory!—the Lord Jesus has given it to me! Glory be to His name!”

George sat, awe-struck, gazing in silence, at the force with which these broken sentences were uttered.

Tom grasped his hand, and went on: “Ye mustn’t, now, tell Chloe, poor soul! how ye found me;—’t would be so drefful to her. Only tell her ye found me going into glory; and that I couldn’t stay for no one. And tell her the Lord’s stood by me everywhere and al’ays, and made everything light and easy. And oh, the poor chil’en, and the baby;—my old heart’s been most broke for ’em, time and agin! Tell ’em all to follow me—follow me! Give my love to Mas’r, and dear good Missis, and everybody in the place! Ye don’t know! ’Pears like I loves ’em all! I loves every creature everywhar!—it’s nothing but love! O, Mas’r George! what a thing ’t is to be a Christian!”

At this moment, Legree sauntered to the door, looked in with a dogged, affected carelessness, and turned away.

“The old Satan!” said George, in his indignation. “It’s a comfort to think the devil will pay him for this, some of these days!”

“O, don’t!—oh, ye mustn’t!” said Tom, grasping his hand; “he’s a poor mis’able critter! it’s awful to think on ’t! Oh, if he only could repent, the Lord would forgive him now; but I’m ’feared he never will!”

“I hope he won’t!” said George; “I never want to see him in heaven!”

“Hush, Mas’r George!—it worries me! Don’t feel so! He an’t done me no real harm,—only opened the gate of the kingdom for me; that’s all!”

The sudden flush of strength, lent by joy at meeting his young master, ebbed. A sinking fell on him; he closed his eyes; and that mysterious, sublime change passed over his face that told of other worlds. He drew long, deep breaths; the broad chest rose and fell heavily; the expression was that of a conqueror.

“Who,—who,—who shall separate us from the love of Christ?” he said, in a voice that contended with mortal weakness; and, with a smile, he fell asleep.

George sat fixed in solemn awe. The place seemed holy; and, as he closed the lifeless eyes, and rose up from the dead, one thought possessed him,—that expressed by his simple old friend,—“What a thing it is to be a Christian!”

He turned: Legree stood, sullenly, behind him. The presence was simply loathsome; the fierceness of youthful passion was checked; he only longed to be rid of him with fewest words. Fixing his keen dark eyes on Legree, he said, pointing to the dead, “You have got all you ever can of him. What shall I pay you for the body? I will take it away, and bury it decently.”

“I don’t sell dead niggers,” said Legree, doggedly. “You are welcome to bury him where and when you like.”

“Boys,” said George, authoritatively, to two or three negroes who were looking on, “help me lift him up, and carry him to my wagon; and get me a spade.”

One ran for a spade; the others assisted George to bear the body. He spread his cloak in the wagon, disposed the body carefully in it, moved the seat to give room; then, turning, with forced composure, fixed his eyes on Legree.

“I have not, as yet, said to you what I think of this most atrocious affair;—this is not the time and place. But, sir, this innocent blood shall have justice. I will proclaim this murder. I will go to the very first magistrate, and expose you.”

“Do!” said Legree, snapping his fingers, scornfully. “I’d like to see you doing it. Where you going to get witnesses?—how you going to prove it?—Come, now!”

George saw, at once, the force of the defiance. There was not a white person on the place; and, in southern courts, colored testimony is nothing. He felt as if he could have rent the heavens with his heart’s indignant cry for justice; but in vain.

“After all, what a fuss, for a dead nigger!” said Legree.

The word was as fire to powder. Prudence was never a cardinal virtue of the Kentucky boy. George turned, and, with one indignant blow, knocked Legree flat upon his face; and, standing over him, blazing with wrath and defiance, he might have personified his great namesake triumphing over the dragon. Some men are bettered by being knocked down; they conceive a respect for the hand that lays them fairly in the dust. Legree was of this sort. Rising, brushing his clothes, he eyed the slowly-retreating wagon with consideration, and held his peace till it was out of sight.

Beyond the plantation bounds, George had marked a dry, sandy knoll, shaded by a few trees; there they made the grave.

“Shall we take off the cloak, Mas’r?” said the negroes, when the place was ready.

“No, no,—bury it with him! It’s all I can give you, now, poor Tom, and you shall have it.”

They laid him in; the men shovelled away, silently; they banked it, and spread green turf.

“You may go, boys,” said George, slipping a quarter into each hand. They lingered.

“If young Mas’r would please buy us—” said one.

“We’d serve him so faithful!” said the other.

“Hard times here, Mas’r!” said the first. “Do, Mas’r, buy us, please!”

“I can’t!—I can’t!” said George, with difficulty, motioning them off; “it’s impossible!”

They looked dejected, and walked away in silence.

“Witness, eternal God!” said George, kneeling on the grave of his poor friend; “oh, witness, that, from this hour, I will do what one man can to drive out this curse of slavery from my land!”

There is no monument to mark his rest. He needs none. His Lord knows where he lies, and will raise him up, immortal, to appear with Him in His glory. Pity him not! Such a life and death are not for pity. Not in the riches of omnipotence is the chief glory of God, but in self-denying, suffering love; and blessed are they whom He calls into fellowship with Him, bearing their cross after Him with patience. “Blessed are they that mourn, for they shall be comforted.”

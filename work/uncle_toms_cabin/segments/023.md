“The Grass Withereth—the Flower Fadeth”

Life passes, with us all, a day at a time; so it passed with our friend Tom, till two years were gone. Though parted from all his soul held dear, he was never wholly miserable; for, so well is the harp of human feeling strung, that nothing but a crash that breaks every string can wholly mar its harmony. The hours, as they glided, brought their alleviations; and Tom, reading in his one book of one who had “learned in whatsoever state he was, therewith to be content,” found that good and reasonable doctrine quietly shaping his habit of mind.

A letter, in due time, came from young Master George, in a brave, round hand that, Tom said, “might be read most acrost the room.” It brought home tidings,—that his wife, Aunt Chloe, had been hired to a confectioner in Louisville, and was laying by all her gains for his redemption; that all at the house were well; that his cabin stood shut, but was to be set forth with new ornaments when he came back. Tom thought it the most wonderful composition of modern times, carried it in his breast, and even took counsel with Eva whether it might not be framed; only, how to show both sides at once?

Meantime, the friendship between Tom and little Eva had grown with the child’s growth. He loved her as something frail and earthly, yet almost worshipped her as something heavenly and divine. He gazed on her as an Italian sailor on the image of the child Jesus,—with reverence and tenderness; and to humor her graceful fancies, to meet those thousand simple wants that invest childhood like a many-colored rainbow, was his chief delight. In the morning market his eyes were for flowers; the choicest peach or orange slipped into his pocket for her; and the sight that pleased him most was her sunny head at the gate, and her childish cry,—“Well, Uncle Tom, what have you got for me today?”

Nor was Eva less zealous in kind offices. Though a child, she was a beautiful reader. A fine musical ear, a quick poetic fancy, and an instinctive sympathy with what’s grand and noble, made her such a reader of the Bible as Tom had never before heard. She began to read for him; soon her own earnest nature wound itself about the majestic book, and it became dear to her for the strange yearnings it awoke. Most of all she loved the Revelations and the Prophecies,—their dim, wondrous imagery and fervent language, whose meaning she and her simple friend questioned vainly, yet felt alike. They spoke of a glory to be revealed,—a wondrous something yet to come,—and though not understood, they were not profitless; for the soul, a trembling stranger between two dim eternities, must yearn toward the unknown, and the cloudy pillar of inspiration has voices that answer to her expecting nature.

At this time, the whole St. Clare establishment was removed to their villa on Lake Pontchartrain. The heats had driven all who could to the shore and its cool sea-breezes. St. Clare’s villa, an East Indian cottage with light bamboo verandahs, opened into gardens where tropical bloom ran down by winding paths to the lake,—its silvery water rising and falling in sunbeams, never for an hour the same, yet every hour more beautiful.

It was one of those intensely golden sunsets that turns the whole horizon to a blaze, and makes the water another sky. White-winged vessels glided like spirits; little golden stars trembled in the glow. Tom and Eva sat in a mossy arbor at the foot of the garden. It was Sunday evening; Eva’s Bible lay open on her knee. She read,—“And I saw a sea of glass, mingled with fire.”

“Tom,” said Eva, suddenly stopping, and pointing to the lake, “there ’t is.”

“What, Miss Eva?”

“Don’t you see,—there?” She pointed to the glassy water, that, as it rose and fell, reflected the golden sky. “There’s a ‘sea of glass, mingled with fire.’”

“True enough, Miss Eva,” said Tom; and he sang, softly,

“O, had I the wings of the morning,
    I’d fly away to Canaan’s shore;
Bright angels should convey me home,
    To the new Jerusalem.”

“Where do you suppose new Jerusalem is, Uncle Tom?” said Eva.

“O, up in the clouds, Miss Eva.”

“Then I think I see it,” said Eva. “Look in those clouds!—they look like great gates of pearl; and you can see beyond them—far, far off—it’s all gold. Tom, sing about ‘spirits bright.’”

Tom sang the words of the hymn,

“I see a band of spirits bright,
    That taste the glories there;
They all are robed in spotless white,
    And conquering palms they bear.”

“Uncle Tom, I’ve seen them,” said Eva.

If she had told him she had been to heaven, he would not have been surprised.

“They come to me sometimes in my sleep, those spirits,” she murmured; and her eyes grew dreamy, and she hummed, low,

“They are all robed in spotless white,
    And conquering palms they bear.”

“Uncle Tom,” said Eva, “I’m going there.”

“Where, Miss Eva?”

She rose, and pointed her little hand to the sky. The glow of evening lit her golden hair and flushed cheek with an unearthly radiance, and her eyes were bent earnestly upward.

“I’m going there,” she said, “to the spirits bright, Tom; I’m going, before long.”

The faithful old heart felt a sudden thrust. How often, within six months, had he noticed those little hands grow thinner, the skin more transparent, the breath shorter; how soon she flagged now at play. He had heard Miss Ophelia speak often of a cough all her medicaments could not cure; and even now the fever-rose burned in cheek and hand. Yet never till now had the thought come home.

Has there ever been a child like Eva? Yes,—but their names are on grave-stones, and their smiles, their heavenly eyes, their singular words and ways, are among the buried treasures of yearning hearts. When you see that deep spiritual light in the eye,—when the little soul speaks with a wisdom not of childhood,—hope not to keep that child; for the seal of heaven is on it, and the light of immortality looks out.

The colloquy was broken by a hasty call from Miss Ophelia, St. Clare’s northern cousin.

“Eva—Eva!—why, child, the dew is falling; you mustn’t be out there!”

They hastened in.

Miss Ophelia was old and skilled in nursing. She knew well the first guileful footsteps of that soft, insidious disease which, while no fibre seems broken, seals the fairest irrevocably for death. She noted the slight, dry cough, the daily brightening cheek; nor could fever’s lustre deceive her.

She tried to communicate her fears to St. Clare, Eva’s father; but he threw them back with a petulance unlike his careless good-humor.

“Don’t be croaking, Cousin,—I hate it!” he said. “Don’t you see the child is only growing? Children always lose strength when they grow fast.”

“But she has that cough!”

“O! nonsense of that cough!—it is not anything. She has taken a little cold, perhaps.”

“Well, it was just so—” she began.

“O! stop these hobgoblin’ nurse legends. You old hands get so wise a child cannot cough or sneeze but you see ruin at hand. Take care of her; keep her from the night air; don’t let her play too hard; she’ll do well enough.”

So he said; but he grew nervous and restless. He watched Eva feverishly, repeating, with a painful frequency, that “the child was quite well,” that there wasn’t anything in that cough; it was only some little stomach affection such as children often had. He kept by her more than before, took her often to ride, brought home receipts and strengthening mixtures,—“not,” he said, “that the child needed it; but then it would not do her any harm.”

If truth must be told, what struck the deepest pang was the daily maturing of the child’s mind and feelings. Still a child in fanciful grace, she often dropped words of such reach of thought and strange, unworldly wisdom that they seemed inspiration. At such times St. Clare would feel a sudden thrill, clasp her in his arms, as if that fond hold could save her, and his heart rise wild with the determination to keep her, never to let her go.

Her whole soul seemed absorbed in works of love and kindness. She had always been impulsively generous; now there was a touching, womanly thoughtfulness that all observed. She still loved to be with Topsy—Miss Ophelia’s little pupil—and the various colored children; but she was rather a spectator than an actor in their plays. She would sit half an hour laughing at Topsy’s odd tricks; then a shadow would pass across her face; her eyes grew misty, and her thoughts were afar.

“Mamma,” she said, suddenly, to Marie, her mother, one day, “why don’t we teach our servants to read?”

“What a question, child! People never do.”

“Why don’t they?” said Eva.

“Because it is no use for them to read. It don’t help them to work any better, and they are not made for anything else.”

“But they ought to read the Bible, mamma, to learn God’s will.”

“O! they can get that read to them all they need.”

“It seems to me, mamma, the Bible is for every one to read themselves. They need it many times when nobody is by to read.”

“Eva, you are an odd child,” said her mother.

“Miss Ophelia has taught Topsy to read,” continued Eva.

“Yes; and you see how much good it does. Topsy is the worst creature I ever saw!”

“Here’s poor Mammy,” said Eva,—her nurse, who loved the book so and wished she could read. “What will she do when I can’t read to her?”

Marie, busy turning over a drawer, answered, “Of course, by and by, Eva, you will have other things to think of besides reading the Bible round to servants. Not but what it’s proper; I’ve done it myself, when I had health. But when you come to be dressing, and going into company, you won’t have time. See here!” She lifted a casket. “These jewels I’m going to give you when you come out. I wore them to my first ball. I can tell you, Eva, I made a sensation.”

Eva took the case and lifted a diamond necklace. Her large, thoughtful eyes rested on it; plainly her thoughts were elsewhere.

“How sober you look, child!” said Marie.

“Are these worth a great deal of money, mamma?”

“To be sure. Your father sent to France for them. They are worth a small fortune.”

“I wish I had them,” said Eva, “to do what I pleased with!”

“What would you do with them?”

“I’d sell them, and buy a place in the free states, and take all our people there, and hire teachers, to teach them to read and write.”

Marie laughed.

“Set up a boarding-school! Wouldn’t you teach them to play on the piano, and paint on velvet?”

“I’d teach them to read their own Bible, and write their own letters, and read letters that are written to them,” said Eva, steadily. “I know, mamma, it comes very hard on them that they can’t do these things. Tom feels it—Mammy does—a great many do. I think it’s wrong.”

“Come, come, Eva; you are only a child! You don’t know anything about these things,” said Marie. “Besides, your talking makes my head ache.”

Eva stole away; and after that, she assiduously gave her nurse reading lessons.

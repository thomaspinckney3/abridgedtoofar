Death

Weep not for those whom the veil of the tomb,
In life’s early morning, hath hid from our eyes.

Eva’s bed-room was a spacious apartment, opening, like all the rooms, upon the broad verandah. One door communicated with her father and mother’s chamber; the other, with Miss Ophelia’s. St. Clare had gratified his own eye and taste in furnishing it for her—rose-colored and white muslin draperies; a Paris matting, bordered with rose-buds; light bamboo bedstead, chairs, and lounges, in graceful patterns; above the bed an alabaster bracket with an angel, wings drooped, holding forth a crown of myrtle; over all, gauzy rose-striped curtains, to bar the mosquitos. A little bamboo table, with a Parian lily-vase always filled; an alabaster writing-stand for her childish efforts; on the marble mantle a fair statuette of Jesus receiving little children, with marble vases on either hand, for which it was Tom’s pride to bring morning bouquets. Paintings of children smiled from the walls. Nowhere could the eye turn without images of childhood, of beauty, and of peace; and her little eyes, when they opened in the morning light, always fell upon something that turned the heart to soothing and beautiful thoughts.

The deceitful strength which had borne her awhile was passing. More seldom was her light step heard in the verandah, and oftener she was found reclined upon a little lounge by the open window, her large, deep eyes fixed on the rising and falling of the lake.

One afternoon, thus reclining, her Bible half open, her transparent fingers lying listlessly between the leaves, she heard, in the verandah, her mother’s voice in sharp tones.

“What now, you baggage!—what new piece of mischief? You’ve been picking the flowers, hey?” A smart slap followed.

“Law, Missis! they’s for Miss Eva,” came the answer, in a voice she knew—Topsy’s.

“Miss Eva! A pretty excuse!—you suppose she wants your flowers, you good-for-nothing nigger! Get along with you!”

Eva started, and, in a moment, was in the verandah.

“O, don’t, mother! I should like the flowers; do give them to me; I want them!”

“Why, Eva, your room is full now.”

“I can’t have too many,” said Eva. “Topsy, bring them here.”

Topsy came up with unwonted hesitation and bashfulness, unlike her eldritch boldness. It was a singular bouquet—a scarlet geranium and a single white japonica, with glossy leaves; tied with a studied eye to contrast.

“It’s a beautiful bouquet!” said Eva. “Topsy, you arrange flowers very prettily. Here is a vase I haven’t any flowers for. I wish you’d arrange something every day for it.”

“Well, that’s odd!” said Marie. “What in the world do you want that for?”

“Never mind, mamma; you’d as lief as not Topsy should do it,—had you not?”

“Of course, anything you please, dear! Topsy, you hear your young mistress;—see that you mind.”

Topsy courtesied shortly, and turned away; and Eva saw a tear roll down the dark cheek.

“You see, mamma, I knew poor Topsy wanted to do something for me,” said Eva.

“O, nonsense! it’s only because she likes mischief. She knows she mustn’t pick flowers; so she does it. But, if you fancy to have her pluck them, so be it.”

“Mamma, I think Topsy is different. She’s trying to be a good girl.”

“She’ll have to try a good while before she gets to be good,” said Marie, carelessly.

“Well, you know, mamma, poor Topsy! everything has always been against her.”

“Not since she’s been here, I’m sure. If she hasn’t been talked to, and preached to, and everything done!—and she’s just so ugly, and always will be; you can’t make anything of the creature!”

“But, mamma, it’s so different, to be brought up as I’ve been—with so many friends, so many things to make me good and happy—than to be brought up as she has been, all the time, till she came here!”

“Most likely,” said Marie, yawning. “Dear me, how hot it is!”

“Mamma, you believe, don’t you, that Topsy could become an angel, as well as any of us, if she were a Christian?”

“Topsy! what a ridiculous idea! Nobody but you would think of it. I suppose she could, though.”

“But, mamma, isn’t God her Father as much as ours? Isn’t Jesus her Saviour?”

“Well, that may be. I suppose God made everybody. Where is my smelling-bottle?”

“It’s such a pity—oh! such a pity!” said Eva, looking out on the lake, half to herself.

“What’s a pity?” said Marie.

“That any one who could be a bright angel, and live with angels, should go all down, down, down, and nobody help them!—oh dear!”

“Well, we can’t help it; it’s no use worrying, Eva. We ought to be thankful for our own advantages.”

“I hardly can be,” said Eva; “I’m so sorry for poor folks that haven’t any.”

“That’s odd enough,” said Marie. “I’m sure my religion makes me thankful for my advantages.”

“Mamma,” said Eva, “I want to have some of my hair cut off,—a good deal of it.”

“What for?”

“I want to give some away to my friends, while I am able to give it to them myself. Won’t you ask aunty to come and cut it for me?”

Miss Ophelia came with her scissors; and St. Clare entered with fruit.

“Papa, I just want aunty to cut off some of my hair;—there’s too much, and it makes my head hot. Besides, I want to give some of it away.”

“Take care,—don’t spoil the looks of it!” said he. “Cut underneath; Eva’s curls are my pride.”

“O, papa!” said Eva, sadly.

“Yes; and I want them kept handsome against the time I take you up to your uncle’s, to see Cousin Henrique,” said St. Clare, gayly.

“I shall never go there, papa;—I am going to a better country. O, do believe me! Don’t you see, papa, I get weaker every day?”

“Why do you insist that I shall believe such a cruel thing, Eva?” said her father.

“Only because it is true, papa; and, if you will believe it now, perhaps you will feel about it as I do.”

He stood gloomily eying the long, beautiful curls, as, one by one, they were laid in her lap. She raised them, twined them round her thin fingers, and looked, from time to time, anxiously at him.

“It’s just what I’ve been foreboding!” said Marie. “It has preyed upon my health, bringing me downward to the grave, though nobody regards it. I have seen this, long. St. Clare, you will see, after a while, that I was right.”

“Which will afford you great consolation, no doubt!” said St. Clare, with a dry bitterness.

Eva’s clear, comprehending gaze went from one to the other, as of a soul half loosed from earth. She beckoned her father; he sat down by her.

“Papa, my strength fades away every day, and I know I must go. There are things I must say and do; and you are so unwilling I should speak. But it must come; there’s no putting it off. Do be willing I should speak now!”

“My child, I am willing!” said St. Clare, covering his eyes with one hand, and taking hers with the other.

“Then, I want to see all our people together. I have some things I must say to them.”

Miss Ophelia sent; soon they were all convened. Eva lay back upon her pillows; her hair hung loosely; the crimson of her cheek contrasted painfully with the intense whiteness of face and slender hands; her great, soul-like eyes fixed earnestly upon every one. The spiritual face, the severed locks lying by her, the father’s averted head, Marie’s sobs—these smote at once upon the feelings of a susceptible race. They sighed, shook their heads; there was a deep silence like that of a funeral.

“I sent for you all, my dear friends,” said Eva, “because I love you. I love you all; and I have something to say to you which I want you always to remember. I am going to leave you. In a few more weeks you will see me no more—”

A burst of groans and sobs broke from all; her slender voice was lost. She waited, and then, with a tone that checked them,—

“If you love me, you must not interrupt me so. Listen. I want to speak to you about your souls. Many of you, I am afraid, are very careless. You are thinking only of this world. I want you to remember there is a beautiful world where Jesus is. I am going there; and you can go there. It is for you as much as for me. But, if you want to go there, you must not live idle, careless, thoughtless lives. You must be Christians; you must remember that each one of you can become angels, and be angels forever. If you want to be Christians, Jesus will help you. You must pray to him; you must read—”

She checked herself; a piteous look came into her face.

“O dear! you can’t read—poor souls!” She hid her face and sobbed; their smothered sobs answered from the floor where many knelt.

“Never mind,” she said, raising her face, smiling through tears. “I have prayed for you; and I know Jesus will help you, even if you can’t read. Try to do the best you can; pray every day; ask Him to help you; and get the Bible read to you whenever you can; and I think I shall see you all in heaven.”

“Amen,” murmured Tom and some of the elder ones; the younger, wholly overcome, sobbed with bowed heads.

“I know you all love me.”

“Yes; oh, yes! indeed we do! Lord bless her!” broke involuntarily from many lips.

“Yes, I know it! There isn’t one of you that hasn’t always been very kind to me; and I want to give you something, that, when you look at it, you shall always remember me. I’m going to give all of you a curl of my hair; and, when you look at it, think that I loved you, and am gone to heaven; and that I want to see you all there.”

They gathered about her, with tears and sobs, and took from her hands what seemed a last pledge of love. They fell on their knees, kissed the hem of her garment; the elder poured forth endearments, mingled with prayers and blessings. Miss Ophelia, fearing for her little patient, signed to each to pass out.

At last all were gone but Tom. She held forth a shining tress.

“Here, Uncle Tom; a beautiful one for you. O, I am so happy, Uncle Tom, to think I shall see you in heaven,—for I’m sure I shall.”

Tom took it with tears streaming down his dark cheeks; he could only look up where he had always looked for help.

Miss Ophelia thought all gone; but, turning, found Topsy lingering, wiping her eyes.

“Where did you start up from?” said Miss Ophelia.

“I was here,” said Topsy. “O, Miss Eva, I’ve been a bad girl; but won’t you give me one, too?”

“Yes, poor Topsy! to be sure I will. There—every time you look at that, think that I love you, and wanted you to be a good girl.”

“O, Miss Eva, I is tryin’!” said Topsy, earnestly; “but, Lor, it’s so hard to be good! ’Pears like I an’t used to it, no ways!”

“Jesus knows it, Topsy; he is sorry for you; he will help you.”

Topsy, with her eyes hid in her apron, was passed from the room; as she went, she hid the precious curl in her bosom.

All being gone, Miss Ophelia shut the door. That worthy lady had wiped away many tears of her own; but concern for the consequence of so much excitement was uppermost.

St. Clare had sat all the while in one attitude, his hand shading his eyes.

“Papa!” said Eva, gently, laying her hand on his.

He started and shivered, but made no answer.

“Dear papa!”

“I cannot,” said St. Clare, rising,—“I cannot have it so! The Almighty hath dealt very bitterly with me!”

“Augustine! has not God a right to do what he will with his own?” said Miss Ophelia.

“Perhaps so; but that doesn’t make it easier to bear,” said he, with a dry, hard, tearless manner, turning away.

“Papa, you break my heart!” said Eva, throwing herself into his arms. “You must not feel so!”

Her violent sobbing alarmed them; his thought turned at once.

“There, Eva,—there, dearest! Hush! hush! I was wrong; I was wicked. I will feel any way, do any way,—only don’t distress yourself. I will be resigned; I was wicked to speak as I did.”

She lay like a wearied dove in his arms; he bent over her, soothing her by every tender word he could think of. Marie went into her room and fell into hysterics.

“You didn’t give me a curl, Eva,” said her father, with a sad smile.

“They are all yours, papa,” said she, smiling; “yours and mamma’s; and you must give dear aunty as many as she wants. I only gave them to our poor people myself, because, you know, papa, they might be forgotten when I am gone; and I hoped it might help them remember. You are a Christian, are you not, papa?” she added, doubtfully.

“Why do you ask me?”

“I don’t know. You are so good, I don’t see how you can help it.”

“What is being a Christian, Eva?”

“Loving Christ most of all,” said Eva.

“Do you, Eva?”

“Certainly I do.”

“You never saw him,” said St. Clare.

“That makes no difference,” said Eva. “I believe him, and in a few days I shall see him;” and the young face grew fervent, radiant with joy.

St. Clare said no more. He had seen such a feeling in his mother; but no chord within him vibrated to it.

After this she declined rapidly. Her beautiful room became avowedly a sick room. Miss Ophelia, day and night, performed the nurse’s duty with that adroitness and exactness which all had smiled at in common days, but now blessed. With clear head, punctual remembrance of every prescription, and tranquil hand, she kept every disagreeable incident from sight. Those who had shrugged at her “setnesses” owned her the very person needed.

Uncle Tom was much in Eva’s room. The child suffered from nervous restlessness, and it relieved her to be carried; and Tom’s greatest delight was to bear her, resting on a pillow, up and down the room, or out upon the verandah; and, when fresh lake-breezes blew in the morning, he would walk with her under the orange-trees, or, sitting in their old seats, sing their favorite hymns. Her father often did the same; but when he was weary, Eva would say,

“O, papa, let Tom take me. Poor fellow! it pleases him; and you know it’s all he can do now, and he wants to do something!”

“So do I, Eva!” said her father.

“Well, papa, you can do everything, and are everything to me. You read to me—you sit up nights—and Tom has only this one thing, and his singing; and I know, too, he does it easier than you. He carries me so strong!”

The desire to do something pervaded all the servants; each, in their way, did what they could. The farewell voyage of the little spirit was so bright and placid—so softly wafted toward heavenly shores—that it was hard to realize death approached. She felt no pain, only tranquil weakness, daily and almost insensibly increasing; and she was so beautiful, so loving, so trustful, so happy, that one yielded to the soothing air of innocence and peace that breathed around her. A strange calm fell on St. Clare. It was not hope; that was impossible. It was not resignation; it was only a resting in the beautiful present, wishing to think of no future—like that hush we feel in the bright mild woods of autumn, when the hectic flush is on the trees, and the last flowers linger by the brook, and we joy the more for knowing it will soon pass away.

To Tom she said what she would not burden her father with—those mysterious intimations the soul feels as the cords begin to unbind. At last he would not sleep in his room, but lay all night in the outer verandah, ready at every call.

“Uncle Tom, what alive have you taken to sleeping anywhere and everywhere, like a dog, for?” said Miss Ophelia, finding him stretched by her door near eleven o’clock, as she went to bolt it. “I thought you was one of the orderly sort.”

“I do, Miss Feely,” said Tom, mysteriously. “I do, but now—”

“Well, what now?”

“We mustn’t speak loud; Mas’r St. Clare won’t hear on’t; but, Miss Feely, you know there must be somebody watchin’ for the bridegroom.”

“What do you mean, Tom?”

“You know it says in Scripture, ‘At midnight there was a great cry made. Behold, the bridegroom cometh.’ That’s what I’m spectin’ now, every night, Miss Feely,—and I couldn’t sleep out o’ hearin’, no ways.”

“Why, Uncle Tom, what makes you think so?”

“Miss Eva, she talks to me. The Lord, he sends his messenger in the soul. I must be thar; for when that ar blessed child goes into the kingdom, they’ll open the door so wide, we’ll all get a look in at the glory, Miss Feely.”

“Did Miss Eva say she felt more unwell than usual to-night?”

“No; but she telled me, this mornin’, she was comin’ nearer; thar’s them that tells it to the child. It’s the angels,—‘it’s the trumpet sound afore the break o’ day,’” said Tom, quoting a hymn.

Eva had been unusually bright that afternoon; she sat raised in bed, looked over her trinkets, and named the friends for each; her manner more animated, her voice more natural, than for weeks. St. Clare, kissing her for the night, had said to Miss Ophelia, with a lighter heart than for many days, “Cousin, we may keep her with us, after all; she is certainly better.”

But at midnight—strange, mystic hour, when the veil between the frail present and the eternal future grows thin—came the messenger.

A quick step—Miss Ophelia’s; she had resolved to sit up; and, at the turn of the night, had discerned what experienced nurses call “a change.” The outer door was opened quickly; Tom, watching, was on the alert.

“Go for the doctor, Tom! lose not a moment,” said she; and, stepping across, she rapped at St. Clare’s door.

“Cousin,” she said, “I wish you would come.”

Those words fell on his heart like clods upon a coffin. Why did they? He was in the room in an instant, bending over Eva, who still slept.

What was it he saw that made his heart stand still? Thou knowest, reader, who hast seen that same expression on the face dearest to thee—that look indescribable, hopeless, unmistakable, which says thy beloved is no longer thine. Yet, on the child’s face, no ghastly imprint; rather a high and almost sublime expression—the overshadowing presence of spiritual natures, the dawning of immortal life in that childish soul.

They stood so still that even the watch seemed too loud. In a few moments, Tom returned with the doctor. He entered, gave one look, and stood silent as the rest.

“When did this change take place?” he whispered to Miss Ophelia.

“About the turn of the night.”

Marie, roused by the doctor’s entrance, hurried in.

“Augustine! Cousin!—O!—what—”

“Hush!” said St. Clare, hoarsely; “she is dying!”

Lights; footsteps; anxious faces thronging the verandah, looking tearfully through the glass; but St. Clare saw only that look on the little sleeper’s face.

“O, if she would only wake, and speak once more!” He stooped: “Eva, darling!”

The large blue eyes unclosed; a smile passed; she tried to raise her head and speak.

“Do you know me, Eva?”

“Dear papa,” said the child, with a last effort, throwing her arms about his neck; in a moment they dropped again. A spasm of mortal agony crossed the face; she struggled for breath, and threw up her little hands.

“O, God, this is dreadful!” said St. Clare, turning away in agony, wringing Tom’s hand unconsciously. “O, Tom, my boy, it is killing me!”

Tom held his master’s hands between his own; with tears streaming down his cheeks, he looked up for help where he had always been used to look.

“Pray that this may be cut short!” said St. Clare. “This wrings my heart.”

“O, bless the Lord! it’s over,—it’s over, dear Mas’r!” said Tom. “Look at her.”

She lay panting, as one exhausted; the large, clear eyes rolled up and fixed. Ah, what said those eyes, that spoke so much of heaven! Earth was past—and earthly pain. So solemn, so mysterious, was the triumphant brightness of that face that even sorrow’s sobs were checked. They pressed around her, breathless.

“Eva,” said St. Clare, gently.

She did not hear.

“O, Eva, tell us what you see! What is it?” said her father.

A bright, glorious smile passed over her face, and she said, brokenly, “O! love,—joy,—peace!” gave one sigh, and passed from death unto life.

Farewell, beloved child! the bright, eternal doors have closed after thee; those who watched thy entrance shall wake to the cold gray sky of daily life, and thou—gone forever. But for thee, little one, all tears are wiped away.

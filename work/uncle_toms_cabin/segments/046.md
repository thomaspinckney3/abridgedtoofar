Concluding Remarks

The writer has been often asked whether this narrative be true; she gives one general answer. The separate incidents are, to a very great extent, authentic, occurring either under her own observation, or that of personal friends; counterparts of almost all the characters have been seen; many sayings are word for word as heard or reported. The personal appearance and character of Eliza were drawn from life; the incorruptible fidelity, piety, and honesty of Uncle Tom had more than one development to her knowledge. The mother's crossing the Ohio on the ice is a well-known fact. The story of "old Prue" fell under the observation of her brother, then a collecting-clerk in New Orleans. From the same source came the planter Legree: her brother wrote, after visiting his plantation, that the man made him feel of his fist, "calloused with knocking down niggers," and that he drew a long breath, as if escaped from an ogre's den.

That Tom's tragical fate has had its parallels, there are living witnesses all over our land to testify. Let it be remembered that, in all southern states, no person of colored lineage can testify in a suit against a white; it is easy to see how a case may occur wherever a man's passions outweigh his interests, and a slave has principle enough to resist his will. There is, actually, nothing to protect the slave's life, but the character of the master. To say such cases are rare is to forget that, were New England laws so framed that a master could now and then torture an apprentice to death, we should not be satisfied to be told they are "no sample of general practice." This injustice is an inherent one in the slave system,—it cannot exist without it.

The public and shameless sale of beautiful mulatto and quadroon girls has been notorious since the capture of the schooner Pearl. Hon. Horace Mann, counsel for the defendants, related that among the seventy-six who attempted escape in 1848 was a young girl doomed for the New Orleans market; friends offered a great ransom, but the trader was inexorable; God smote her with death on the passage. Two sisters, when pleaded for, were bantered with promises of fine dresses and furniture; "Yes," said the pleader, "that may do very well in this life, but what will become of them in the next?" They were sent to New Orleans, and afterwards redeemed at enormous cost. Is it not plain that the histories of Emmeline and Cassy may have many counterparts?

Justice, too, obliges the author to say that the fairness and generosity ascribed to St. Clare are not without a parallel. A few years since, a young southern gentleman, in Cincinnati with a favorite servant named Nathan, who had waited on him from a boy, lost the man to a Quaker's protection. In high anger he visited the Quaker; but, being candid, was quieted by arguments he had never heard. He said, "If, to my face, he says it is his desire to be free, I will liberate him." An interview was procured, and Nathan was asked whether he had ever had cause to complain. "No, Mas'r; you've always been good to me." "Why then leave me?" "Mas'r may die, and then who get me?—I'd rather be a free man." "Nathan, in your place, I think I should feel very much so, myself. You are free." He made out free papers, deposited money to help him start, and left a sensible letter of advice. Such nobility saves us from utter despair of our kind; but are such characters common anywhere?

For many years the author avoided the subject, trusting advancing light would live it down; but, after the act of 1850, when she heard Christian and humane people recommending the remanding of fugitives as a citizen's duty, she could only think, They cannot know what slavery is. Hence her desire to exhibit it in a living dramatic reality. She has shown it fairly, in its best and worst phases. In its best aspect, perhaps she has been successful; but, oh! who shall say what yet remains untold in that valley and shadow of death?

To you, generous men and women of the South, tried by severer discipline, is her appeal. In your secret souls, have you not felt woes and evils, in this accursed system, far beyond what is here shadowed? Is man ever to be trusted with wholly irresponsible power? Does not the slave code, by denying the slave all legal right of testimony, make every owner an irresponsible despot? If there be a public sentiment of honor and humanity among you, is there not also another among the ruffian and debased? And cannot the ruffian own as many slaves as the purest? Are the honorable the majority anywhere?

American law calls the slave-trade piracy; yet a trade as systematic as any on the African coast inevitably attends American slavery, with heartbreaks and horrors untold. There are those living who know the mothers whom this traffic has driven to the murder of their children, and themselves to death as a refuge from woes worse than death. Nothing of tragedy can be written or conceived that equals the frightful reality, daily and hourly enacting beneath the shadow of American law and of the cross of Christ.

And now, men and women of America, is this to be trifled with, apologized for, passed in silence? And you, mothers of America,—by the sacred love you bear your child; by the motherly pity with which you guide his years; by the sick hour; by those dying eyes you can never forget; by the desolation of the empty cradle,—pity the mother who has all your affections and not one legal right to protect the child of her bosom! Is this a thing to be defended?

Say not the free states have nothing to do with it. They have defended, encouraged, participated; merchants in northern cities temporarily own and sell again; the traffic in souls is made an equivalent to money. Northern men, mothers, Christians, must look to the evil among themselves. What can any individual do? This every one can do,—see to it that they feel right. An atmosphere of sympathy encircles every soul; the man or woman who feels strongly, healthily, justly, on the great interests of humanity, is a constant benefactor. And you can pray. You pray for the heathen abroad; pray also for the heathen at home, and for those distressed Christians whose adherence to the morals of Christianity is, in many cases, an impossibility, unless grace from above give them the courage of martyrdom.

On our free shores are emerging poor, shattered remnants of families, escaped by providences from the surge of slavery, feeble in knowledge, shaken in moral constitution. Do we owe them nothing? Shall church and school-house be shut? Some say, "Let them go to Africa." That God has provided a refuge in Africa is notable; but that is no reason for the church to throw off the responsibility her profession demands. Receive them to the educating advantages of Christian republican society and schools; when they have attained some maturity, then assist them to those shores where they may put in practice what they have learned. A body of men at the North have done this, and the country has seen former slaves rapidly acquire property, reputation, and education. On the authority of Professor C. E. Stowe, cases in Cincinnati show men, full black and three-fourths, worth from ten to thirty thousand dollars, all earned by themselves, often after paying heavy sums for freedom. The writer remembers an aged washerwoman whose daughter, by thrift and self-denial, raised nine hundred dollars for her husband's freedom, paying it as she raised it; he died before the last hundred, and she never recovered the money. The colored man, by Ohio law, cannot be a voter, and was till lately denied the right of testimony; yet we see men, but yesterday from shackles, rise by a self-educating force to highly respectable stations,—Pennington among clergymen, and Douglass and Ward among editors. If, with every discouragement, they have done thus much, how much more might they do if the Christian church would act towards them in the spirit of her Lord!

This is an age when nations tremble. A mighty influence is abroad, surging the world as with an earthquake. O Church of Christ, read the signs of the times! Is not this power the Spirit of Him whose kingdom is to come? But who may abide the day of his appearing? "for that day shall burn as an oven: and he shall appear as a swift witness against those that oppress the hireling in his wages, the widow and the fatherless, and that turn aside the stranger in his right: and he shall break in pieces the oppressor." Christians! every time you pray that the kingdom may come, forget not that prophecy links, in dread fellowship, the day of vengeance with the year of his redeemed.

A day of grace is yet held out to us. Both North and South have been guilty before God; and the Christian church has a heavy account to answer. Not by combining together to protect injustice and cruelty, and making a common capital of sin, is this Union to be saved,—but by repentance, justice and mercy; for, not surer is the eternal law by which the millstone sinks in the ocean, than that stronger law, by which injustice and cruelty shall bring on nations the wrath of Almighty God!

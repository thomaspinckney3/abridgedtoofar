Cassy

“And behold, the tears of such as were oppressed, and they had no comforter; and on the side of their oppressors there was power, but they had no comforter.”—ECCL. 4:1

It took but a short time to familiarize Tom with all that was to be hoped or feared in his new way of life. An expert and faithful workman, quiet, prompt, and peaceable, he resolved, by unremitting diligence, to avert, at least from himself, some portion of the evils around him. He saw enough of abuse and misery to make him sick and weary; yet he toiled on with religious patience, committing himself to Him that judgeth righteously, not without hope that some way of escape might yet be opened.

Legree, taking a silent note of Tom’s availability, rated him as a first-class hand; and yet he felt for him the native antipathy of bad to good. He saw plainly that, when his violence fell on the helpless, Tom took notice of it; for, so subtle is the atmosphere of opinion, that it will make itself felt without words. He had purchased Tom with a view of making him, in time, a sort of overseer; the first, second, and third requisite for that place, in his view, was hardness. Finding Tom not hard to his hand, he resolved forthwith to harden him; and, some few weeks after Tom had been on the place, he determined to commence.

One morning, as the hands were mustered for the field, Tom noticed, with surprise, a new comer among them. A woman, tall and slenderly formed, with remarkably delicate hands and feet, dressed with neatness, walked erect beside him in the dim gray of dawn. Her face, that might once have been beautiful, was seamed with lines of pain and of proud endurance; her whole form was emaciated; but her eye—so large, so heavily black, overshadowed by long lashes—held a wild, mournful despair; beneath the scorn that sat on the flexible lip and in every proud motion, there lay in that eye a deep, settled night of anguish. Where she came from, Tom did not know. To the gang, however, she was known; and there was much looking and turning of heads, with a smothered exultation among the miserable, ragged, half-starved creatures.

“Got to come to it, at last,—glad of it!” said one.

“He! he! he! you’ll know how good it is, Misse!” said another.

The woman took no notice, but walked on with the same expression of angry scorn. Tom, who had always lived among the refined, felt intuitively that she belonged by nature to that class; how or why fallen to these degrading circumstances, he could not tell. She neither looked at him nor spoke, though all the way to the field she kept close at his side.

At work, Tom often glanced towards her. A native adroitness made the task easy to her; she picked fast and clean, and with an air that despised alike the work and the humiliation of doing it.

In the course of the day Tom worked near a mulatto woman, bought in the same lot with himself. She was evidently in great suffering; he often heard her praying, as she wavered and trembled, and seemed about to fall. Silently, as he came near, he transferred several handfuls of cotton from his own sack to hers.

“O, don’t, don’t!” said the woman, looking surprised; “it’ll get you into trouble.”

Just then one of the drivers, Sambo, came up. He seemed to have a special spite against this woman. Flourishing his whip—“What dis yer, foolin’?”—and, with the word, kicking her with his heavy cowhide, he struck Tom across the face.

Tom resumed his task; but the woman, already at the last point of exhaustion, fainted.

“I’ll bring her to!” said the driver, with a brutal grin. “I’ll give her something better than camphire!” Taking a pin from his sleeve, he buried it to the head in her flesh. The woman groaned, and half rose. “Get up, you beast, and work, will yer, or I’ll show yer a trick more!”

For a few moments, an unnatural strength seemed to seize her, and she worked with desperate eagerness.

“See dat ye keep to dat ar,” said he, “or yer’ll wish yer’s dead, tonight, I reckin!”

“That I do now!” Tom heard her say; and again,—“O, Lord, how long! O, Lord, why don’t you help us?”

At the risk of all he might suffer, Tom came forward, and put all the cotton in his sack into the woman’s.

“O, you mustn’t! you donno what they’ll do to ye!” said she.

“I can bar it,” said Tom, “better ’n you.” In a moment he was at his place again.

The stranger woman had, in the course of her work, come near enough to hear. She raised her heavy black eyes, fixed them for a second on him; then, taking a quantity of cotton from her basket, placed it in his.

“You know nothing about this place,” she said, with her lip’s scornful curl. “When you’ve been here a month, you’ll be done helping anybody; you’ll find it hard enough to take care of your own skin!”

“The Lord forbid, Missis!” said Tom, using instinctively the respectful form proper to the high-bred among whom he had lived.

“The Lord never visits these parts,” said the woman, bitterly; and went nimbly forward with her work.

But her action had been seen by the driver across the field, and, flourishing his whip, he came up.

“What! what!” he cried, with an air of triumph. “You a foolin’? Go ’long! yer under me now,—mind yerself, or yer’ll cotch it!”

A glance like sheet-lightning flashed from those black eyes. Facing him, with quivering lip and dilated nostril, she drew herself up, and fixed on him a blaze of rage and scorn.

“Dog!” she said, “touch me, if you dare! I’ve power enough, yet, to have you torn by the dogs,—burnt alive,—cut to inches! I’ve only to say the word!”

“What de devil you here for, den?” stammered the man, cowed, retreating a step or two. “Didn’t mean no harm, Misse Cassy!”

“Keep your distance, then!” she answered.

He was greatly inclined to attend to something at the other end of the field, and started off in quick time.

The woman turned to her work, and labored with a despatch that astonished Tom. She seemed to work by magic. Before the day was through, her basket was filled, crowded down, and piled, and she had several times put largely into Tom’s. Long after dusk, the weary train, with baskets on their heads, defiled up to the building where the cotton was stored and weighed. Legree was there, busily conversing with his two drivers, Sambo and Quimbo; there was ugly talk of “breakin’ in” hands that had “notions,” and a brutal jest that a soft-spoken fellow should get his hand in by flogging a sick wench. “I do the weighing!” said Legree, when there was mention that certain baskets had been filled by friendly hands.

Slowly the dispirited creatures wound into the room, and, with crouching reluctance, presented their baskets.

Tom’s was weighed and approved; he looked, with an anxious glance, for the success of the woman he had befriended.

Tottering, she came forward and delivered her basket. It was of full weight, as Legree well perceived; but, affecting anger, he said,

“What, you lazy beast! short again! stand aside,—you’ll catch it, pretty soon!”

She gave a groan of utter despair, and sat down on a board.

The person who had been called Misse Cassy now came forward, and, with a haughty, negligent air, delivered her basket. As she did so, Legree looked in her eyes with a sneering yet inquiring glance.

She fixed her black eyes steadily on him; her lips moved slightly, and she said something in French. What it was no one knew; but Legree’s face became perfectly demoniacal as she spoke. He half raised his hand, as if to strike,—a gesture she regarded with fierce disdain, turning away.

“And now,” said Legree, “come here, you Tom. You see, I telled ye I didn’t buy ye jest for the common work; I mean to promote ye, and make a driver of ye; and tonight ye may jest as well begin to get yer hand in. Now, ye jest take this yer gal and flog her; ye’ve seen enough on’t to know how.”

“I beg Mas’r’s pardon,” said Tom; “hopes Mas’r won’t set me at that. It’s what I an’t used to,—never did,—and can’t do, no way possible.”

“Ye’ll larn a pretty smart chance o’ things ye never did know, before I’ve done with ye!” said Legree; and, taking up a cowhide, he struck Tom a heavy blow across the cheek, following by a shower of blows.

“There!” he said, stopping to rest; “now, will ye tell me ye can’t do it?”

“Yes, Mas’r,” said Tom, putting up his hand to wipe the blood that trickled down his face. “I’m willin’ to work, night and day, and work while there’s life and breath in me; but this yer thing I can’t feel it right to do;—and, Mas’r, I never shall do it,—never!”

The mild, smooth voice and habitually respectful manner had made Legree think Tom cowardly and easy to subdue. When he spoke these last words, a thrill of amazement went through all. The poor woman clasped her hands, and said, “O Lord!” All involuntarily looked at each other, drawing in their breath, as if to prepare for the storm about to burst.

Legree looked stupefied; then broke forth,—“What! ye blasted black beast! tell me ye don’t think it right to do what I tell ye! What have any of you cussed cattle to do with thinking what’s right? I’ll put a stop to it! May be ye think ye’r a gentleman master, Tom, to be a tellin’ your master what’s right, and what ain’t! So you pretend it’s wrong to flog the gal!”

“I think so, Mas’r,” said Tom. “The poor crittur’s sick and feeble; ’t would be downright cruel, and it’s what I never will do, nor begin to. Mas’r, if you mean to kill me, kill me; but, as to my raising my hand agin any one here, I never shall,—I’ll die first!”

Legree shook with rage; his greenish eyes glared; but, like some ferocious beast that plays with its victim before devouring, he kept back his impulse, and jeered.

“Well, here’s a pious dog, at last, let down among us sinners!—a saint, a gentleman, to talk to us about our sins! Powerful holy critter, he must be! Here, you rascal, you make believe to be so pious,—didn’t you never hear, out o’ yer Bible, ‘Servants, obey yer masters’? An’t I yer master? Didn’t I pay down twelve hundred dollars, cash, for all there is inside yer old cussed black shell? An’t yer mine, now, body and soul?” He gave Tom a violent kick with his heavy boot. “Tell me!”

In the very depth of bodily suffering, bowed by oppression, that question shot a gleam of joy and triumph through Tom’s soul. He suddenly stretched himself up, and, looking earnestly to heaven, while tears and blood mingled on his face, he exclaimed,

“No! no! no! my soul an’t yours, Mas’r! You haven’t bought it,—ye can’t buy it! It’s been bought and paid for, by one that is able to keep it;—no matter, no matter, you can’t harm me!”

“I can’t!” sneered Legree. “We’ll see,—we’ll see! Here, Sambo, Quimbo, give this dog such a breakin’ in as he won’t get over, this month!”

The two gigantic negroes, with fiendish exultation in their faces, laid hold of Tom. The poor woman screamed with apprehension; all rose, as by a general impulse, while they dragged him unresisting from the place.

“De balena vero sufficit, si rex habeat caput, et regina caudam.”—Bracton. That is: of all whales captured on England’s coast, the King, as Honorary Grand Harpooneer, must have the head, and the Queen the tail—a division much like halving an apple; no remainder. Being still in force, and anomalous to Fast- and Loose-Fish, it is here noted.

Certain mariners of the Cinque Ports killed and beached a fine whale. Now the Cinque Ports are under the jurisdiction of a sort of beadle, called a Lord Warden. By some writers this office is called a sinecure. But not so. Because the Lord Warden is busily employed at times in fobbing his perquisites; which are his chiefly by virtue of that same fobbing of them. Now when these poor sun-burnt mariners, bare-footed, and with their trowsers rolled high up on their eely legs, had wearily hauled their fat fish high and dry, promising themselves £150 from the precious oil and bone; and in fantasy sipping their tea and ale upon the strength of their shares; up steps a very learned gentleman, with a copy of Blackstone under his arm; and laying it upon the whale’s head, he says—“Hands off! this fish, my masters, is a Fast-Fish. I seize it as the Lord Warden’s.” Upon this the poor mariners in their respectful consternation—so truly English—knowing not what to say, fall to vigorously scratching their heads all round; meanwhile ruefully glancing from the whale to the stranger. “Please, sir, who is the Lord Warden?”—“The Duke.” “But the Duke had nothing to do with taking this fish.”—“It is his.” “We have been at great trouble, peril, expense—are all to go to the Duke’s benefit?”—“It is his.” “Is the Duke so very poor?”—“It is his.” “I thought to relieve my old bed-ridden mother by part of my share of this whale.”—“It is his.” “Won’t the Duke be content with a quarter or a half?”—“It is his.” In a word, the whale was seized and sold; his Grace the Duke of Wellington received the money. Thinking the case rather hard, an honest clergyman wrote begging consideration for the mariners. His Grace replied he had already done so, received the money, and would be obliged if the reverend gentleman would decline meddling with other people’s business. Is this the still militant old man, standing at the corners of the three kingdoms, on all hands coercing alms of beggars?

Plowdon gives the reason: the whale belongs to King and Queen “because of its superior excellence.” But why should the King have the head, and the Queen the tail? William Prynne, in “Queen-Gold,” says, “Ye tail is ye Queen’s, that ye Queen’s wardrobe may be supplied with ye whalebone.” A sagacious lawyer’s sad mistake; the black limber bone is in the head, not the tail. Is the Queen a mermaid, to be presented with a tail? Allegory may lurk.

Two royal fish the law styles—the whale and the sturgeon—both royal property under certain limitations. By inference, the sturgeon must be divided as the whale; the King receiving the highly dense elastic head—congeniality symbolical, perhaps. There is a reason in all things, even in law.

“De balena vero sufficit, si rex habeat caput, et regina caudam.”—Bracton. That is: of all whales captured on England’s coast, the King, as Honorary Grand Harpooneer, must have the head, the Queen the tail—a division much like halving an apple; no remainder. Being still in force, and anomalous to Fast- and Loose-Fish, it here has a separate car.

Within two years certain mariners of the Cinque Ports killed and beached a fine whale. Now when these poor sun-burnt mariners, bare-footed, and with their trowsers rolled high up on their eely legs, had wearily hauled their fat fish high and dry, promising themselves a good £150 from the precious oil and bone; and in fantasy sipping rare tea with their wives, and good ale with their cronies, upon the strength of their respective shares; up steps a very learned and most Christian and charitable gentleman, with a copy of Blackstone under his arm; and laying it upon the whale’s head, he says—“Hands off! this fish, my masters, is a Fast-Fish. I seize it as the Lord Warden’s.” “Who is the Lord Warden?”—“The Duke.” “But the Duke had nothing to do with taking this fish.”—“It is his.” “We have been at great trouble, peril, expense—are all to go to the Duke’s benefit?”—“It is his.” “Is the Duke so very poor?”—“It is his.” “Won’t the Duke be content with a quarter or a half?”—“It is his.” In a word, the whale was seized and sold; his Grace the Duke of Wellington received the money. Thinking that viewed in some particular lights, the case might by a bare possibility in some small degree be deemed, under the circumstances, a rather hard one, an honest clergyman wrote begging consideration for the mariners. His Grace replied he had already done so, received the money, and would be obliged if the reverend gentleman would decline meddling with other people’s business. Is this the still militant old man, standing at the corners of the three kingdoms, on all hands coercing alms of beggars?

Plowdon gives the reason: the whale belongs to King and Queen “because of its superior excellence.” But why should the King have the head, and the Queen the tail? William Prynne, in “Queen-Gold,” says, “Ye tail is ye Queen’s, that ye Queen’s wardrobe may be supplied with ye whalebone.” A sagacious lawyer’s sad mistake; the black limber bone is in the head, not the tail. Is the Queen a mermaid, to be presented with a tail? Allegory may lurk.

Two royal fish the law styles—the whale and the sturgeon—both royal property under certain limitations. By inference, the sturgeon must be divided as the whale; the King receiving the highly dense elastic head—congeniality symbolical, perhaps. There is a reason in all things, even in law.

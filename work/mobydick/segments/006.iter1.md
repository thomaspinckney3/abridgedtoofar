Entering that gable-ended Spouter-Inn, you found yourself in a wide, low, straggling entry with old-fashioned wainscots, reminding one of the bulwarks of some condemned old craft. On one side hung a very large oil-painting so thoroughly besmoked, and every way defaced, that in the unequal crosslights by which you viewed it, it was only by diligent study and a series of systematic visits that you could any way arrive at an understanding of its purpose. Such unaccountable masses of shades and shadows, that at first you almost thought some ambitious young artist, in the time of the New England hags, had endeavored to delineate chaos bewitched. But what most puzzled and confounded you was a long, limber, portentous, black mass of something hovering in the centre of the picture over three blue, dim, perpendicular lines floating in a nameless yeast. A boggy, soggy, squitchy picture truly, enough to drive a nervous man distracted. Yet was there a sort of indefinite, half-attained, unimaginable sublimity about it that fairly froze you to it, till you involuntarily took an oath with yourself to find out what that marvellous painting meant. Ever and anon a bright, but, alas, deceptive idea would dart you through.—It’s the Black Sea in a midnight gale.—It’s the unnatural combat of the four primal elements.—It’s a blasted heath.—It’s a Hyperborean winter scene.—It’s the breaking-up of the icebound stream of Time. But stop; does it not bear a faint resemblance to a gigantic fish? even the great leviathan himself? A final theory: the picture represents a Cape-Horner in a great hurricane, the half-foundered ship weltering there with its three dismantled masts alone visible, and an exasperated whale purposing to spring clean over the craft, in the enormous act of impaling himself upon the three mast-heads.

The opposite wall was hung all over with a heathenish array of monstrous clubs and spears. Mixed with these were rusty old whaling lances and harpoons all broken and deformed. Others were tufted with knots of human hair; and one was sickle-shaped, with a vast handle sweeping round like the segment made in the new-mown grass by a long-armed mower. You shuddered as you gazed, and wondered what monstrous cannibal and savage could ever have gone a death-harvesting with such a hacking, horrifying implement. Some were storied weapons: with this once long lance, now wildly elbowed, fifty years ago did Nathan Swain kill fifteen whales between a sunrise and a sunset; and that harpoon—so like a corkscrew now—was flung in Javan seas, and years afterwards slain off the Cape of Blanco was the whale in whose hump the original iron was found imbedded.

Through the entry and into the public room: low ponderous beams above, old wrinkled planks beneath, such that you would almost fancy you trod some old craft's cockpit on a howling night. In the far corner stood a dark-looking den—the bar—a rude attempt at a right whale's head. Within the vast arched jaw of the whale, wide enough that a coach might almost drive beneath it, a little withered old man bustles among old decanters and bottles—another cursed Jonah who, for their money, dearly sells the sailors deliriums and death. Abominable are the tumblers into which he pours his poison. Though true cylinders without—within, the villanous green goggling glasses deceitfully tapered downwards to a cheating bottom. Parallel meridians rudely pecked into the glass, surround these footpads’ goblets. Fill to this mark, and your charge is but a penny; to this a penny more; and so on to the full glass—the Cape Horn measure, which you may gulp down for a shilling.

The landlord told me his house was full, not a bed unoccupied—but I was welcome to share a harpooneer's blanket. Rather than wander further on so bitter a night, I agreed, on condition the harpooneer was not decidedly objectionable. We supped in a cold adjoining room, nothing but two dismal tallow candles, each in a winding sheet—but dumplings, good heavens! dumplings for supper. The dark-complexioned harpooneer had not yet appeared. He eats nothing but steaks, the landlord told me, and he likes 'em rare.

The bar-room filled after supper; then suddenly a tramping of sea boots, and in rolled the Grampus's crew—three years out, a full ship—enveloped in shaggy watch coats, beards stiff with icicles, like an eruption of bears from Labrador. They made straight for the whale's jaw of a bar. Among them I observed one man who held somewhat aloof, his sober face unwilling to spoil his shipmates' hilarity. He stood full six feet in height, with noble shoulders, and a chest like a coffer-dam; and in the deep shadows of his eyes floated some reminiscences that did not seem to give him much joy. When the revelry had mounted to its height, this man slipped away unobserved. Presently he was missed, and a cry went up—"Bulkington! Bulkington! where's Bulkington?"—and they darted out of the house in pursuit.

No man prefers to sleep two in a bed. In fact, you would a good deal rather not sleep with your own brother. I don't know how it is, but people like to be private when they are sleeping. And when it comes to sleeping with an unknown stranger, in a strange inn, in a strange town, and that stranger a harpooneer, then your objections indefinitely multiply. Nor was there any earthly reason why I as a sailor should sleep two in a bed, more than anybody else; for sailors no more sleep two in a bed at sea, than bachelor Kings do ashore. To be sure they all sleep together in one apartment, but you have your own hammock, and cover yourself with your own blanket, and sleep in your own skin.

It grew late, and still no harpooneer. At midnight the landlord offered the cryptic intelligence that the man had gone out peddling and couldn't sell his head. "Can't sell his head?" I cried. "Landlord," said I, going up to him as cool as Mt. Hecla in a snow-storm—"landlord, stop whittling. You and I must understand one another, and that too without delay. I come to your house and want a bed; you tell me you can only give me half a one; that the other half belongs to a certain harpooneer... I now demand of you to speak out and tell me who and what this harpooneer is, and whether I shall be in all respects safe to spend the night with him... and you, sir, by trying to induce me to do so knowingly, would thereby render yourself liable to a criminal prosecution." Whereupon he fetched a long breath and explained that the man had but lately arrived from the South Seas, had bought a lot of ’balmed New Zealand heads—great curios—and having sold all but one, was trying to sell the last that night, it being the Sabbath on the morrow.

This cleared up the mystery, though it hardly settled my unease: what was I to think of a harpooneer who kept Saturday-night hours engaged in selling the heads of dead idolaters?

Unwilling still to sleep two in a bed, I declared I’d try the bench; whereupon the landlord, “I’ve got a carpenter’s plane,” procured the plane; and with his old silk handkerchief first dusting the bench, vigorously set to planing away at my bed, the while grinning like an ape. The shavings flew right and left; till at last the plane-iron came bump against an indestructible knot. I begged him to quit—“I did not know how all the planing in the world could make eider down of a pine plank.”

The landlord eventually persuaded me upstairs to a small room cold as a clam, furnished with a prodigious bed almost big enough for any four harpooneers to sleep abreast. I examined the room: a hammock lashed up and thrown in one corner, a large seaman's bag, outlandish bone fish hooks on a shelf, a tall harpoon standing at the head of the bed. On the chest I found a strange object I could compare to nothing but a large door mat, ornamented at the edges with little tinkling tags, with a hole or slit in the middle like a South American poncho. I put it on to try it, and never saw such a sight in my life. I tore myself out of it in a hurry and gave myself a kink in the neck.

Eventually I blew out the light and tumbled into bed. I had nearly made a good offing towards the land of Nod when I heard a heavy footfall in the passage, and saw a glimmer of light under the door.

The stranger entered holding a light in one hand and that identical New Zealand head in the other. He was a dark, purplish, yellow colour, here and there stuck over with large blackish squares. Not sticking-plasters—tattoos. His bald, purplish head looked for all the world like a mildewed skull. He fumbled in his bag and produced a sort of tomahawk and a seal-skin wallet. Then he took off his hat—a new beaver hat—and there was no hair on his head, nothing but a small scalp-knot twisted up on his forehead.

I am no coward, but ignorance is the parent of fear, and being completely nonplussed about the stranger, I confess I was now much afraid of him. He continued undressing: his chest and arms and back and very legs were checkered with the same dark squares as his face. He must be some abominable savage shipped aboard a whaleman in the South Seas. A peddler of heads—perhaps the heads of his own brothers. He might take a fancy to mine—heavens! look at that tomahawk!

But now the savage produced from his coat pocket a curious little deformed image with a hunch on its back, exactly the colour of a three days' old Congo baby—a wooden idol, as I presently determined. He set it up like a tenpin between the andirons of the empty fire-place, then placed shavings carefully before it, laid a bit of ship biscuit on top, and applied a flame. He made a polite offer of the scorched biscuit to the little negro. The idol never moved his lips. All these strange antics were accompanied by still stranger guttural noises, the devotee praying in a sing-song, his face twitching in the most unnatural manner. At last he extinguished the fire and bagged the idol again in his coat pocket as carelessly as if it were a dead woodcock.

He then took up his tomahawk, examined it, held it to the light, put the handle to his mouth—and puffed out great clouds of tobacco smoke. The next moment the light was extinguished, and this wild cannibal, tomahawk between his teeth, sprang into bed with me. I sang out; he began feeling me; guttural responses; and then—"Who-e debel you?—you no speak-e, dam-me, I kill-e." The lighted tomahawk flourished about me in the dark, scattering hot tobacco ashes till I thought my linen would get on fire.

"Landlord, for God's sake, Peter Coffin!" shouted I. "Landlord! Watch! Coffin! Angels! save me!"

At that moment the landlord came in with a light. "Don't be afraid now," said he, grinning, "Queequeg here wouldn't harm a hair of your head."

The landlord spoke to the harpooneer, and Queequeg, puffing at his pipe, motioned me into the bed with his tomahawk and threw the clothes to one side—in not only a civil but a really kind and charitable way.

The man's a human being just as I am, thought I; he has just as much reason to fear me, as I have to be afraid of him. Better sleep with a sober cannibal than a drunken Christian.

I told the landlord to instruct Queequeg to stash his tomahawk and stop smoking, and I would turn in with him. Queequeg complied, and again politely motioned me to bed, rolling over to one side as much as to say—"I won't touch a leg of ye."

I turned in, and never slept better in my life.

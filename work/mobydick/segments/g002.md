## Chapter 1

At daybreak I woke with Queequeg’s arm flung over me in the most loving and affectionate manner—you had almost thought I had been his wife. The patchwork counterpane—odd little parti-coloured squares and triangles—so blended with his tattooed arm, a Cretan labyrinth of lines, that I could scarce tell quilt from limb till the weight of it told me he hugged me.

Strange sensations visited me, like a remembered childhood terror when, lying wide awake in daylight-punishment, I had dozed into a nightmare and felt, for ages piled on ages, a nameless supernatural hand placed in mine; I dared not stir a finger lest the spell break. Take away the awful fear, and my sensations now, feeling the pagan arm in mine, were very similar in their strangeness. But the past night’s realities returned; and I lay only alive to the comicality of the posture. I wriggled and expostulated on the unbecomingness of his bridegroom clasp; a grunt at last; then he drew back his arm, shook himself like a Newfoundland dog, and sat up, stiff as a pike-staff, rubbing his eyes, as if half-unconscious how I came there. With marvellous native politeness he made signs to dress first and leave me the room anon.

I watched him—rude enough in me—through his toilette. He began at top, donning his very tall beaver hat; next he hunted up his boots; then, hat on, boots in hand, he crushed himself under the bed, where, from gaspings and strainings, he was hard at work booting himself. He was in the transition stage—neither caterpillar nor butterfly; just civilized enough to show off his outlandishness. Emerging, his hat dented down over his eyes, he limped about, the damp cowhides pinching him of a bitter cold morning.

Observing the window undraped, the street close by, and his figure—hat and boots chiefly—I begged him to accelerate his toilet, and particularly to get into his trowsers. He complied; then to washing; but, to my amazement, contented himself with ablutions of chest, arms, and hands. Next, waistcoat; then, taking hard soap, he lathered his face, and—lo!—from the bed-corner drew the harpoon; slipped out the stock; unsheathed the head; whetted it on his boot; and, striding to the bit of mirror, began harpooning his cheeks. Thinks I, this is using Rogers’s best cutlery with a vengeance; but I afterwards knew the fine steel of a harpoon’s head, and how exceedingly sharp its long straight edges are always kept. His toilet soon achieved, he proudly marched out in his pilot monkey jacket, sporting his harpoon like a marshal’s baton.

## Chapter 2

Descending to the bar-room, I bore no malice to the grinning landlord who had skylarked me in the bedfellow business. A good laugh is a mighty good thing; and the man who affords stuff for it, let him spend and be spent that way.

The room was full of boarders—chief, second, and third mates; sea carpenters, coopers, blacksmiths; harpooneers; ship keepers; a brown, bosky-bearded company in monkey jackets for morning gowns. You could tell how long each had been ashore by the hue on his cheek—sun-toasted pears, satin-wood touches, a tropic tawn slightly bleached. But who could show a cheek like Queequeg’s? barred with various tints like the Andes’ western slope—contrasting climates, zone by zone.

“Grub, ho!” cried the landlord; in we went. I had looked to hear good whaling yarns; but to my surprise, nearly every man maintained a profound silence, and looked embarrassed—bashful bears, timid warrior whalemen, who had boarded great whales without winking, now sheepish at a social table. Curious sight!

As for Queequeg—he sat at the head by chance, as cool as an icicle. His breeding might not be boasted: he brought his harpoon to breakfast, used it without ceremony, reaching over heads and grappling the beefsteaks. But that was coolly done; and to do anything coolly is, with most, to do it genteelly. He eschewed coffee and hot rolls, and applied undivided attention to beefsteaks, done rare. Breakfast over, he retired to the public room, lighted his tomahawk-pipe, and sat quietly digesting with his inseparable hat on; I sallied out for a stroll.

## Chapter 3

If Queequeg among the polite astonished me by candle-light, daylight in New Bedford quickly accustomed me. Nigh the docks, you see the queerest nondescripts; but New Bedford beats Wapping: actual cannibals stand chatting at corners; savages outright—many who yet carry on their bones unholy flesh. It makes a stranger stare.

Besides Feegeeans, Tongatobooarrs, Erromanggoans, and all the wild whaling specimens reeling unheeded, there arrive weekly scores of green Vermonters and New Hampshire men—stalwart fellows who have felled forests and now drop the axe to snatch the whale-lance. No town-bred dandy matches a downright bumpkin dandy—buckskin-gloved mower in dog-days—who, bespeaking a sea-outfit, orders bell-buttons to his waistcoats and straps to his canvas trowsers. Ah, poor Hay-Seed! how bitterly will burst those straps in the first howling gale.

Think not the town has only harpooneers, cannibals, and bumpkins. It is a land of oil, true; but no Canaan. Yet nowhere in America will you find more patrician-like houses, parks and gardens more opulent, than in New Bedford. Whence came they, planted on this once scraggy scoria? Go gaze upon the iron emblematical harpoons round yonder lofty mansion, and your question is answered. Yes; “all these brave houses and flowery gardens came from the Atlantic, Pacific, and Indian oceans. One and all, they were harpooned and dragged up hither from the bottom of the sea.” Can Herr Alexander perform a feat like that?

In summer the town is sweet; fine maples; in August the horse-chestnuts, candelabra-wise, proffer upright cones of blossoms; art has superinduced bright terraces of flowers upon barren refuse rocks. And the women bloom like their own red roses—carnation perennial as sunlight in the seventh heavens.

## Chapter 4

There stands in New Bedford a Whaleman’s Chapel; few moody fishermen, bound for the Indian or Pacific, fail to make a Sunday visit. In driving sleet and mist I fought my way in. A small scattered congregation sat apart, insular and incommunicable; the chaplain not yet arrived. All steadfastly eyed several black-bordered marble tablets masoned into the wall: youths washed overboard off Patagonia; a boat’s crew towed out of sight by a whale; a captain killed in the bows by a sperm whale off Japan.

Whether any of their kin sat there I knew not; but faces spoke unceasing grief; the bleak tablets made old wounds bleed afresh. “Oh! ye whose dead lie buried beneath the green grass… ye know not the desolation that broods in bosoms like these. What bitter blanks in those black-bordered marbles which cover no ashes!”

All the living strive to hush all the dead; yet “Faith, like a jackal, feeds among the tombs, and even from these dead doubts she gathers her most vital hope.”

On the eve of a Nantucket voyage, I regarded those tablets and read the whalemen’s fate. Yes, Ishmael, the same fate may be thine. But somehow I grew merry again. There is death in this business—a speechlessly quick bundling into Eternity. But what then? “Methinks that what they call my shadow here on earth is my true substance… Methinks my body is but the lees of my better being.” So three cheers for Nantucket; and “come a stove boat and stove body when they will, for stave my soul, Jove himself cannot.”

## Chapter 5

Presently there entered a man of venerable robustness, sleet running from his tarpaulin; coat and overshoes shed, he came decently to the pulpit. It was Father Mapple—sailor and harpooneer once; now, in the hardy winter of a healthy old age, with mild gleams of a second flowering youth in his wrinkles.

The pulpit was lofty; no common stairs, but a perpendicular side ladder with red worsted man-ropes, like mounting a ship from a boat. Hand over hand, sailor-like, he ascended; then, turning, stooped and deliberately dragged up the ladder, depositing it within—impregnable in his little Quebec. It symbolized, I thought, his spiritual withdrawal for the time from all worldly ties; “this pulpit… a self-containing stronghold.”

Between marble cenotaphs behind him hung a large painting: a gallant ship beating against a lee-coast storm; high above the scud a little isle of sunlight with an angel’s face shedding a distinct radiance upon the tossed deck—“Beat on, thou noble ship… serenest azure is at hand.”

The pulpit’s panelled front showed a ship’s bluff bows; the Holy Bible rested on a fiddle-headed beak. What could be fuller of meaning? “Yes, the world’s a ship on its passage out, and not a voyage complete; and the pulpit is its prow.”

## Chapter 6

He rose and, with mild authority, ordered the people to condense: “Starboard gangway, there! side away to larboard—larboard gangway to starboard! Midships! midships!” Kneeling in the bows, hands folded, eyes uplifted, he prayed as if at the bottom of the sea. Then, in prolonged solemn tones, he read and sang a hymn, the congregation joining till the storm without seemed but an accompaniment:

“The ribs and terrors in the whale,
Arched over me a dismal gloom…
In black distress, I called my God…
With speed he flew to my relief…
My song for ever shall record
That terrible, that joyful hour…”

Turning the leaves, he clinched his text: “Beloved shipmates, clinch the last verse of the first chapter of Jonah—‘And God had prepared a great fish to swallow up Jonah.’”

“Shipmates, this little book—four chapters, four yarns—sounds great depths. What is its lesson? Two-stranded: to us all as sinful men; and to me, as a pilot of the living God. As men, it is the story of sin, disobedience, fears, swift punishment, repentance, prayers, deliverance, and joy. All God would have us do is hard for us to do; and if we obey God, we must disobey ourselves.

“With his sin of disobedience in him, Jonah flouts God by fleeing. He skulks at Joppa, finds a ship for Tarshish—Cadiz—world-wide away; miserable man! worthy of scorn, with slouched hat and guilty eye. The mariners mark him; he looks the fugitive—no baggage, no adieux. In the cabin, ‘We sail with the next coming tide,’ says the Captain, eyeing him: ‘Soon enough for any honest man that goes a passenger.’ Stab! But Jonah answers, ‘I’ll sail with ye… I’ll pay now.’ Note it well, shipmates: ‘he paid the fare thereof.’ Sin that pays its way can travel freely; Virtue, if a pauper, is stopped at all frontiers. The Captain rings every coin; not a forger; Jonah is put down.

“In his close state-room a swinging lamp, straight in itself, shows all else awry; so his conscience hangs in him. ‘Straight upwards, so it burns; but the chambers of my soul are all in crookedness!’ At last, the tide: the ship glides to sea—the first of smugglers; the contraband is Jonah. A dreadful storm comes on; in all this raging tumult, Jonah sleeps his hideous sleep, till the master cries, ‘What meanest thou, O, sleeper! arise!’

“The sailors cast lots; the lot is Jonah’s. ‘What is thine occupation? whence comest thou?’ He answers more than asked: ‘I am a Hebrew; I fear the Lord, the God of heaven, who hath made the sea and the dry land!’ Fear him then indeed! He confesses; and when he cries, ‘Take me and cast me forth,’ they mercifully try all else; in vain. Invoking God, with the other hand they lay hold of Jonah; and behold him taken as an anchor and dropped into the sea. Instantly an oily calmness floats from the east; the sea is still; he goes down in the whirling heart of the commotion into the yawning jaws; the whale shoots-to all his ivory teeth.

“Then Jonah prays out of the fish’s belly. Mark his prayer: he does not clamour for pardon; he leaves deliverance to God; spite of all pains, he will still look towards His holy temple. Here is true repentance—grateful for punishment. God speaks to the fish; from the blackness the whale breeches toward the warm and pleasant sun, and ‘vomited out Jonah upon the dry land.’

“Shipmates,”—here the storm’s howling seemed to add power to him; his chest heaved as with a ground-swell; his tossed arms were warring elements; thunder rolled from his brow—“I place Jonah not for copying in sin, but for copying in repentance.”

He turned the leaves, communed silently; then leaned and bowed with manly humility: “God has laid but one hand upon you; both his hands press upon me. Hear the other, more awful lesson to me, a pilot of the living God. Bidden to sound unwelcome truths in wicked Nineveh, Jonah fled from his mission—would pour oil when God had brewed a gale. Woe to that pilot who slights this! Woe to him whom this world charms from Gospel duty! Woe to him who seeks to please rather than to appal! Woe to him whose good name is more to him than goodness! Woe to him who, while preaching to others, is himself a castaway!”

Then joy leaped in his eyes: “But oh! shipmates! on the starboard hand of every woe there is a sure delight; and higher the top of that delight than the bottom of the woe is deep. Delight is to him—a far, far upward and inward delight—who against the proud gods and commodores of this earth, ever stands forth his own inexorable self. Delight is to him whose strong arms yet support him, when the ship of this base treacherous world has gone down beneath him. Delight is to him who gives no quarter in the truth, and kills, burns, and destroys all sin though he pluck it from under Senators’ and Judges’ robes. Delight,—top-gallant delight is to him, who acknowledges no law or lord but the Lord his God, and is only a patriot to heaven. And eternal delight will be his, who, laying him down, can say—O Father! chiefly known to me by Thy rod—mortal or immortal, here I die. I have striven to be Thine more than this world’s, or mine own.”

He waved a benediction, covered his face with his hands, and so remained kneeling till all had departed, and he was left alone.

## Chapter 7

Returning to the Spouter-Inn, I found Queequeg alone before the fire, feet on the stove hearth, holding close to his face his little negro idol, gently whittling at its nose with a jack-knife, humming in his heathenish way. Interrupted, he put up the image; then took a large book, and, placing it on his lap, began counting the pages with deliberate regularity; at every fiftieth, as I fancied, he stopped, looked vacantly around, and gave a long gurgling whistle of astonishment; then recommenced at one—as if he could not count beyond fifty, and only the multitude of fifties excited him.

Savage though he was and hideously marred to my taste, something in his countenance by no means displeased. You cannot hide the soul: through the unearthly tattooings I thought I saw traces of a simple honest heart; in his large deep eyes, fiery black and bold, tokens of a spirit that would dare a thousand devils; and a certain lofty bearing—he looked like a man who had never cringed, and never had had a creditor. Phrenologically, his head reminded me of General Washington’s—the same retreating slope and projecting brows: Queequeg was George Washington cannibalistically developed.

He heeded me not; he consorted little with the other seamen; made no advances; seemed content with his own companionship—equal to himself. Twenty thousand miles from home, cast among strangers as in Jupiter, yet serene—there was fine philosophy there, unknowing itself. So sitting in that lonely room, fire now in its mild glow, storm booming without, I felt a melting in me. No more were heart and hand turned against the wolfish world. This soothing savage redeemed it. I drew my bench near; made signs; spoke of his last night’s hospitalities. He asked if we were again to be bedfellows; yes; he seemed pleased.

We turned the book together; I showed him the printing and the pictures; then proposed a social smoke. He produced his pouch and tomahawk; we exchanged puffs, passing that wild pipe between us. If any ice yet lurked, the pleasant smoke thawed it. He pressed his forehead to mine, clasped me round the waist, and said henceforth we were married—bosom friends—he would gladly die for me. Premature in a countryman; in this simple savage, trustworthy.

After supper and more talk and smoke, we went to our room. He made me a present of his embalmed head; drew forth his enormous tobacco wallet, then, groping under the tobacco, some thirty silver dollars, which he divided mechanically into two equal portions and poured one into my trowsers’ pocket. I would have remonstrated; he silenced me. Then his evening prayers: out came the idol; paper fireboard removed. By signs, he wished me to join.

I was a good Presbyterian. How unite with a wild idolator worshipping his bit of wood? But what is worship? “to do the will of God—that is worship.” And what is the will of God? “to do to my fellow man what I would have my fellow man to do to me.” Queequeg is my fellow man. I would wish him to unite with me in my form; ergo, I must unite with him in his. So I kindled the shavings; helped prop the innocent idol; offered burnt biscuit; salamed; kissed his nose; and so we went to bed at peace with our consciences and all the world—nor slept without some little chat; in our hearts’ honeymoon lay I and Queequeg—a cosy, loving pair.

## Chapter 8

Thus we lay, chatting and napping by turns; Queequeg now and then affectionately throwing his brown tattooed legs over mine and drawing them back—so sociable and free were we—till, by our confabulations, what little nappishness remained altogether departed. Wearied of our recumbent posture, we found ourselves sitting up, the clothes tucked well about us, leaning against the head-board; four knees drawn up close, two noses bending over them as if kneepans were warming-pans. It was chilly; there was no fire; yet we felt very nice and snug. Truly to enjoy bodily warmth, some small part of you must be cold; “there is no quality in this world that is not what it is merely by contrast.” For the height of deliciousness, have nothing but the blanket between you and the cold; then you lie like the one warm spark in the heart of an arctic crystal.

I keep my eyes shut between sheets, to concentrate the snugness; “no man can ever feel his own identity aright except his eyes be closed;” but opening them to the coarse outer gloom of midnight, I felt a revulsion, and did not object to Queequeg’s hint to strike a light; he much desired a few quiet puffs from his tomahawk. Prejudices are elastic when love once bends them. I liked nothing better than his smoking by me, serene with household joy. We drew our shaggy jackets about our shoulders, passed the tomahawk to and fro, till a blue hanging tester of smoke slowly grew over us, illuminated by the new-lit lamp. Whether that undulating canopy rolled him to far scenes I know not, but he now spoke of his native island; eager to hear, I begged him to go on; he gladly complied.

## Chapter 9

Queequeg was a native of Rokovoko, far West and South. “It is not down in any map; true places never are.” A new-hatched savage in a grass clout, followed by nibbling goats, he yet longed to see more of Christendom than a specimen whaler. His father a High Chief, a King; his uncle a High Priest; aunts wedded to unconquerable warriors—royal stuff, though vitiated in youth by cannibal propensity.

A Sag Harbor ship visited his father’s bay. Denied a passage, he vowed a vow; alone in his canoe he hid in a mangrove-thicketed strait the ship must pass; as she glided by he darted out, gained her side; with one backward dash capsized and sank his canoe; climbed the chains; grappled a ring-bolt, and swore not to let go though hacked in pieces. Cutlass over his naked wrists availed not; struck by his dauntless desire, the Captain relented; but this sea Prince of Wales never saw the cabin—put among sailors, they made a whaleman of him. Like Czar Peter, he disdained no seeming ignominy, if thereby he might learn the arts to make his people happier and better. But Christians, as he found them in Sag Harbor and Nantucket, could be miserable and wicked—infinitely more so than all his father’s heathens. He gave up Christendom for lost: ’tis a wicked world in all meridians; he would die a pagan.

Thus an old idolator at heart, he wore Christian clothes and tried their gibberish. He feared Christians had unfitted him for ascending the undefiled throne of thirty pagan kings; by and by he would return—as soon as he felt himself baptized again; for the nonce, sail about and sow his wild oats in four oceans. They had made a harpooneer of him; that barbed iron was in lieu of a sceptre now. His immediate purpose: to go to sea again in his old vocation. Whaling was mine too; Nantucket my port. He resolved to accompany me; same vessel, watch, boat, and mess; to share my every hap; with both my hands in his, boldly dip into the Potluck of both worlds. I joyously assented; his tale ended with his pipe’s last dying puff; he embraced me, pressed his forehead to mine; blowing out the light, we rolled from each other this way and that, and very soon were sleeping.

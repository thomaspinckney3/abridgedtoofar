The wind and sea then betokened storms—Ahab’s ostensible reason for not boarding the Goney; but, as subsequent conduct showed, had he, by hailing, got a negative to his one absorbing question, he would scarce have cared to consort five minutes with any stranger captain. All this were ill estimated without something said of the peculiar usages of whalers meeting in foreign seas, especially on a common cruising-ground.

If two strangers crossing the Pine Barrens or Salisbury Plain cannot avoid a salute and bit of news, how much more natural, upon the illimitable Barrens and Plains of the sea, that two whalers descrying each other off lone Fanning’s or far King’s Mills should come into close sociable contact—especially if owned in one seaport, with letters and papers to swap, and the latest whaling intelligence to exchange. Nor would difference of country much alter this; though English whalers, being few, meet seldom; and when they do, there’s apt to be a shyness—your Englishman reserved; your Yankee, he allows that sort of thing to none but himself. The English sometimes affect a metropolitan superiority over the long lean Nantucketer—sea-peasant, they think; wherein that superiority lies, hard saying, seeing the Yankees in one day kill more whales than all the English in ten years; but this foible the islander little minds—knowing he hath a few of his own.

So of all ships separately sailing the sea, whalers have most reason to be sociable—and they are so. Merchantmen oft pass like a brace of dandies in Broadway, mutually cutting each other on the high seas; and all the time indulging, perhaps, in finical criticism upon each other’s rig. Men-of-War duck ensigns in such a string of silly bowings and scrapings there’s little hearty good-will. Slave-ships hurry past; Pirates, meeting, hail “How many skulls?”—as whalers hail “How many barrels?”—and steer apart, not loving to behold each other’s villanous likeness.

But look at the godly, honest, unostentatious, hospitable, sociable, free-and-easy whaler! What does the whaler do when she meets another in any decent weather? She has a “Gam,” a thing so unknown to other ships they never heard the name; and if they did, they grin about “spouters” and “blubber-boilers.” Why it is that all Merchant-seamen, and also all Pirates and Man-of-War’s men, and Slave-ship sailors, cherish such a scornful feeling towards Whale-ships, is hard to answer. Because, in the case of pirates, say, I should like to know whether that profession of theirs has any peculiar glory about it. It sometimes ends in uncommon elevation, indeed; but only at the gallows. Hence, I conclude, that in boasting himself to be high lifted above a whaleman, in that assertion the pirate has no solid basis to stand on. But what is a Gam? You might wear out your index-finger running up and down the columns of dictionaries, and never find the word. Dr. Johnson never attained to that erudition; Noah Webster’s ark does not hold it. Nevertheless, this same expressive word has now for many years been in constant use among some fifteen thousand true born Yankees. Certainly, it needs a definition, and should be incorporated into the Lexicon. With that view, let me learnedly define it.

GAM. NOUN—_A social meeting of two_ (_or more_) _Whaleships, generally on a cruising-ground; when, after exchanging hails, they exchange visits by boats’ crews: the two captains remaining, for the time, on board of one ship, and the two chief mates on the other._

There is another item about Gamming. In a pirate, Man-of-War, or slave-ship, the captain, rowed in his boat, sits in cushioned stern-sheets and often steers with a pretty milliner’s tiller. The whale-boat has no such sofa, no tiller at all. High times indeed, if whaling captains were wheeled about the water on castors like gouty aldermen. As a complete crew must leave the ship, and the boat-steerer is of the number, that subordinate is steersman; the captain, having no place to sit, is pulled off all standing like a pine tree—conscious of all eyes levelled on him from the two ships’ sides; alive to the importance of sustaining his dignity by maintaining his legs. No easy matter: behind him the immense projecting steering-oar hits him in the small of the back; the after-oar raps his knees in front; he is wedged before and behind, and can only expand sideways by settling on his stretched legs; a sudden pitch will near topple him, for length of foundation is naught without breadth. And it would never do, in the world’s riveted gaze, to be seen steadying himself by catching hold with his hands; token of entire buoyant self-command, he carries them in his trowsers’ pockets—perhaps, being very large, heavy hands, for ballast. Nevertheless, there are instances, well authenticated, when, in an uncommonly critical squall, the straddling captain has seized the nearest oarsman’s hair, and held on there like grim death.

Ere the English ship fades from sight, be it set down that she hailed from London and bore the name of Samuel Enderby, original of that famous whaling house; a house which, in a whaleman’s account, comes not far behind Tudors and Bourbons for historical interest. Long before 1775 our valiant Coffins and Maceys of Nantucket and the Vineyard had pursued the Sperm Whale in Atlantic waters; but in 1775 Enderby & Sons fitted the first regular English sperm-cruisers. Be it distinctly recorded, that the Nantucketers were the first among mankind to harpoon with civilized steel the great Sperm Whale; and for half a century they alone so harpooned him.

In 1778 the Amelia, sent by the vigorous Enderbys, boldly rounded Cape Horn, first to lower in the great South Sea; her lucky hold full of sperm opened the Pacific grounds to English and Americans. Then the Rattler, a sloop-of-war, was sent whaling by British inducement—how much she did, who knows? In 1819 their discovery-ship Syren tasted the remote waters of Japan; commanded by Captain Coffin of Nantucket, she made known that great ground.

The ship named after Samuel was worthy—fast, noble. I boarded her once at midnight off Patagonia and drank good flip in the forecastle. It was a fine gam; we flipped ten gallons the hour; then a squall, topsails to reef—so top-heavy we must swing each other aloft in bowlines; we ignorantly furled the skirts of our jackets into the sails, and hung there, reefed fast in the howling gale, a warning to drunken tars. Down again, so sober we had to pass the flip once more, though the savage spray pickled it. The beef was tough, with body in it; some swore it bull, others dromedary. The dumplings—small, substantial, indestructible—seemed to roll within you like billiard-balls. The bread was anti-scorbutic and best eaten in a dark corner. All in all, the Samuel Enderby was a jolly ship: good fare and plenty; crack fellows all.

Why such abounding good cheer in certain English whalers? The English merchant-ship scrimps; not so the English whaler. The custom, incidental and particular, has a special origin. In my Leviathanic researches I stumbled on an ancient Low Dutch “Dan Coopman” (The Merchant), by Fitz Swackhammer—so Dr. Snodhead averred—whose chapter “Smeer” or “Fat” transcribed the larders of 180 Dutch whalemen: 400,000 lbs. beef; 60,000 lbs. Friesland pork; 150,000 lbs. stock-fish; 550,000 lbs. biscuit; 72,000 lbs. soft bread; 2,800 firkins butter; 20,000 lbs. Texel & Leyden cheese; 144,000 lbs. inferior cheese; 550 ankers Geneva; 10,800 barrels beer. Reckoning thirty men to a ship, for a three-month cruise, that is two barrels of beer per man, exclusive of his share of gin. High livers, those Hollanders; and the English have not neglected so excellent an example. For say they: if cruising in an empty ship, if you can get nothing better out of the world, get a good dinner out of it, at least. And this empties the decanter.

## Chapter 1

Ere the English ship fades from sight, be it set down that she hailed from London and bore the name of Samuel Enderby, original of that famous whaling house; a house which, in a whaleman’s account, comes not far behind Tudors and Bourbons for historical interest. Long before 1775 our valiant Coffins and Maceys of Nantucket and the Vineyard had pursued the Sperm Whale in Atlantic waters; but in 1775 Enderby & Sons fitted the first regular English sperm-cruisers. Be it distinctly recorded, that the Nantucketers were the first among mankind to harpoon with civilized steel the great Sperm Whale; and for half a century they alone so harpooned him.

In 1778 the Amelia, sent by the vigorous Enderbys, boldly rounded Cape Horn, first to lower in the great South Sea; her lucky hold full of sperm opened the Pacific grounds to English and Americans. Then the Rattler, a sloop-of-war, was sent whaling by British inducement—how much she did, who knows? In 1819 their discovery-ship Syren tasted the remote waters of Japan; commanded by Captain Coffin of Nantucket, she made known that great ground.

The ship named after Samuel was worthy—fast, noble. I boarded her once at midnight off Patagonia and drank good flip in the forecastle. It was a fine gam; we flipped ten gallons the hour; then a squall, topsails to reef—so top-heavy we must swing each other aloft in bowlines; we ignorantly furled the skirts of our jackets into the sails, and hung there, reefed fast in the howling gale, a warning to drunken tars. Down again, so sober we had to pass the flip once more, though the savage spray pickled it. The beef was tough, with body in it; some swore it bull, others dromedary. The dumplings—small, substantial, indestructible—seemed to roll within you like billiard-balls. The bread was anti-scorbutic and best eaten in a dark corner. All in all, the Samuel Enderby was a jolly ship: good fare and plenty; crack fellows all.

Why such abounding good cheer in certain English whalers? The English merchant-ship scrimps; not so the English whaler. The custom, incidental and particular, has a special origin. In my Leviathanic researches I stumbled on an ancient Low Dutch “Dan Coopman” (The Merchant), by Fitz Swackhammer—so Dr. Snodhead averred—whose chapter “Smeer” or “Fat” transcribed the larders of 180 Dutch whalemen: 400,000 lbs. beef; 60,000 lbs. Friesland pork; 150,000 lbs. stock-fish; 550,000 lbs. biscuit; 72,000 lbs. soft bread; 2,800 firkins butter; 20,000 lbs. Texel & Leyden cheese; 144,000 lbs. inferior cheese; 550 ankers Geneva; 10,800 barrels beer. Reckoning thirty men to a ship, for a three-month cruise, that is two barrels of beer per man, exclusive of his share of gin. High livers, those Hollanders; and the English have not neglected so excellent an example. For say they: if cruising in an empty ship, if you can get nothing better out of the world, get a good dinner out of it, at least. And this empties the decanter.

## Chapter 2

Hitherto I have dwelt upon the marvels of the whale’s outer aspect; but to a thorough comprehension, it behooves me now to unbutton him to his ultimatum—his unconditional skeleton. “But how now, Ishmael?”—I confess, since Jonah, few whalemen have gone far beneath the skin of the adult; yet once, a small cub Sperm was bodily hoisted to deck for sheath-making; I used hatchet and jack-knife and read that young volume entire. And for exact knowledge of the full-grown bones I am beholden to my late royal friend Tranquo, king of Tranque, of the Arsacides.

At Pupella, a palm-glenn sea-side villa near Bamboo-Town, Tranquo, great lover of barbaric vertu, had gathered rare carvings and natural wonders which the tribute-rendering waves had cast ashore. Chief among them: a great Sperm Whale, found dead with his head against a cocoa-nut tree; stripped of his fathom-deep enfoldings, bones dust-dry, the skeleton was borne up the glen and sheltered in a temple of lordly palms. The ribs were hung with trophies; the vertebræ were carved with Arsacidean annals; in the skull the priests kept an unextinguished aromatic flame, so that the mystic head sent forth a vapory spout; while, from a bough, the terrific lower jaw vibrated like Damocles’ hair-hung sword.

It was wondrous. The wood was a weaver’s loom; through the lacings of leaves the great sun, a flying shuttle, wove the verdure. Oh, busy weaver—whither flows the fabric? But the weaver-god, he weaves, and by that humming we, too, are deafened; only when we escape it shall we hear the thousand voices that speak through it. Amid that green, life-restless loom, the white worshipped skeleton lay lounging—a gigantic idler—yet seemed the cunning weaver, himself all trellised with vines; Life folded Death; Death trellised Life; the grim god wived with youthful Life and begat curly-headed glories.

I paced to and fro—brushed vines, broke through ribs, eddied long in its shaded colonnades with a ball of Arsacidean twine; but soon my line was out, and I emerged. Cutting a green measuring-rod, I dived again. From their arrow-slit in the skull, the priests spied me taking the altitude of the final rib: “Dar’st thou measure this our god!” A fierce contest rose—feet and inches—yard-sticks cracked, the skull echoed; seizing that chance, I concluded my own admeasurements.

In this matter I am not free to fancy; refer to skeleton authorities—Hull’s Leviathanic Museum; Manchester, New Hampshire, with its Greenland specimen; and Burton Constable in Yorkshire, where Sir Clifford’s Sperm Whale stands articulated like a chest of drawers; locks upon trap-doors; a footman with his bunch of keys; twopence for a peep at the whispering gallery in the spinal column; sixpence for the unrivalled view from his forehead. My own dimensions I copied verbatim from my right arm, tattooed; being crowded for space, I left the odd inches—nor should inches enter a congenial admeasurement of the whale.

## Chapter 3

First, a plain statement touching the living bulk. On careful calculation (partly upon Scoresby’s seventy tons for a sixty-foot Greenland), a Sperm Whale of largest magnitude—eighty-five to ninety feet long, somewhat under forty in girth—will weigh at least ninety tons; he would outweigh a whole village of eleven hundred inhabitants at thirteen men to the ton. Brains must be yoked like cattle to budge him to any landsman’s imagination.

At Tranque, the skeleton measured seventy-two feet; fully invested in life, he must have been ninety, for the skeleton loses about one fifth. Of the seventy-two, skull and jaw comprised some twenty, leaving fifty feet of back-bone, and, for something less than a third of that, the mighty circular basket of ribs.

The ribs were ten a side; the first near six feet; the second, third, and fourth successively longer, to the climax of the fifth—eight feet and some inches; thence they diminished, the tenth spanning a little over five. Consider well: the skeleton is no mould of the invested form. Where the fish is greatest in depth, the corresponding rib conveyed but half the living magnitude. For the long naked spine had been once wrapped round with tons of flesh and viscera; for ample fins, a few disordered joints; for majestic, boneless flukes, an utter blank.

The spine—pile its bones on end with a crane; it looks like Pompey’s Pillar. There are forty and odd vertebræ, not locked; they lie like great knobbed blocks in Gothic courses. The largest, a middle one, is under three feet in width, more than four in depth; the smallest, where the spine tapers to the tail, but two inches wide—like a white billiard-ball. Some smaller were lost by little cannibal urchins who played marbles with them. Thus the spine of the hugest of living things tapers off at last into simple child’s play.

## Chapter 4

From his mighty bulk the whale invites amplification. Would you, you could not compress him. Give me a condor’s quill! Give me Vesuvius’ crater for an inkstand! Writing of this Leviathan, my thoughts weary me with their comprehensiveness, as if to include all whales and men and mastodons, and empire’s panoramas. To produce a mighty book, choose a mighty theme.

Credentials as geologist? I have been a stone-mason; digger of ditches and wells and vaults. In the Tertiary formations—last preceding the superficial—are found all the Cetacean fossils; none precisely answer to living species, yet sufficiently akin to rank as whale relics. Detached fragments have been found at the base of the Alps; in France, England, Scotland; in Louisiana, Mississippi, and Alabama. In 1779, part of a skull was disinterred in the Rue Dauphine, Paris; in Napoleon’s time, bones at Antwerp. Cuvier pronounced them of an unknown Leviathanic species.

By far most wonderful: in 1842, on Judge Creagh’s plantation, Alabama, an almost complete vast skeleton—by the awe-struck slaves taken for bones of a fallen angel. Doctors dubbed it Basilosaurus; but Owen, crossing specimen bones, rechristened Zeuglodon; a whale of a departed species; another illustration that the skeleton furnishes little clue to the invested form.

Standing among these mighty skulls and ribs, I am borne back to a period ere time itself; Saturn’s chaos rolls; bastions of ice wedge the future tropics; in all the world’s circumference not an inhabitable hand’s breadth. Then the whole world was the whale’s; he left his wake along the Andes and the Himmalehs. Who can show a pedigree like Leviathan? Methuselah seems a school-boy; I look to shake hands with Shem.

Not alone in limestone and marl has he left his bust; upon Egyptian tablets, too. In Denderah’s temple, upon a granite ceiling planisphere of centaurs and dolphins, old Leviathan swims as of yore—centuries before Solomon was cradled. And John Leo, venerable Barbary traveller, tells of a Sea-side Temple whose Rafters and Beams were Whale-Bones; a Rib arched so high a man on a camel could not reach its crown; the Common People imputing a secret Power of God that no Whale could pass without death. In this Afric Temple of the Whale I leave you; and if you be a Nantucketer and a whaleman, you will silently worship there.

## Chapter 5

Since Leviathan comes floundering down from the head-waters of the Eternities, inquire we whether, in his generations, he has not degenerated. Upon investigation, we find the whales of the present day superior in magnitude to those of the Tertiary; and of those fossils, the later exceed the earlier. The largest pre-adamite yet exhumed—the Alabama monster—was less than seventy feet in skeleton; we have seventy-two feet for a large modern skeleton, and whalemen swear they have taken Sperm Whales near a hundred feet long at capture.

“But since Adam’s time?” Pliny and the ancients tell of whales embracing acres, Aldrovandus of eight hundred feet—Rope Walks and Thames Tunnels! Lacépède sets the Right Whale at a hundred metres. Will any whaleman believe it? No. As Egyptian mummies do not outmeasure modern Kentuckians; as oldest cattle carvings prove Pharaoh’s kine not exceeding Smithfield’s prize beeves; so will I not admit that of all animals the whale alone has degenerated.

Another inquiry: must not Leviathan at last be exterminated—like the buffalo herds that so lately shook the prairies and are now gone from Illinois and Missouri? Look in every light. Forty men in one ship, hunting Sperm Whales for forty-eight months, thank God if they carry home the oil of forty fish; while the moccasined hunters of the old West slew forty thousand buffalo in the same span. Moreover, whales now move in immense caravans; the scattered pods of other days are aggregated into vast but widely separated armies. Whale-bone whales, driven from promontory to cape, can retreat at last to their Polar citadels; diving under glassy barriers, they come up among floes, and in a charmed circle of everlasting December bid defiance to pursuit.

“But forty harpooned for one cachalot—does not havoc diminish?” Americans alone on the nor’ west coast have taken not less than 13,000 yearly; yet consider the populousness of the enormous—Harto tells of Siam’s king taking 4,000 elephants at one hunting; hunted for thousands of years, they still survive. Much more may the great whale outlast all hunting, with a pasture precisely twice as large as all the continents and isles combined. And from their presumed longevity—century and more—several adult generations must be contemporary. Wherefore, we account the whale immortal in his species, however perishable in his individuality. He swam the seas before the continents broke water; in Noah’s flood he despised Noah’s Ark; and if ever the world be flooded again, then the eternal whale will still survive, and rearing upon the topmost crest of the equatorial flood, spout his frothed defiance to the skies.

## Chapter 6

Ahab’s precipitate quitting of the Samuel Enderby had not been unattended by violence to his own person. Leaping into his boat, he lighted with such energy upon a thwart that his ivory leg half-splintered; and, wheeling upon the Pequod’s deck with vehement command, he gave that shaken bone such additional wrench that, though entire, it seemed to him no longer wholly trustworthy.

Small wonder that even in his mad recklessness he heeded, at times, the condition of that dead bone whereon he partly stood. Not long before sailing, he had been found one night prone and insensible; by some unimaginable casualty, the ivory limb so violently displaced that it stake-wise smote and all but pierced his groin; the agonizing wound was cured with extreme difficulty. Then and thereafter, in his monomaniac mind, he traced the present anguish to a former woe; all miserable events beget their like; yea, more than equally than felicities, thought Ahab. While even the highest earthly joys have a pettiness lurking, at bottom all heart-woes a mystic significance, and in some men an archangelic grandeur; to trail their genealogies carries us among the sourceless primogenitures of the gods; so that we must give in to this: the gods themselves are not for ever glad.

To many it had been a mystery why, for a certain period before and after sailing, Ahab hid himself with Grand-Lama-like exclusiveness; Captain Peleg’s bruited reasons never sufficed. In the end it came out: this dire mishap lay at the bottom of his temporary recluseness; to the timid circle who approached him, the unaccounted casualty invested itself with terrors not undrawn from the land of spirits. Be all this as it may, in the present matter of his leg he took plain practical procedures—he called the carpenter. He bade him, without delay, set about making a new leg; the mates were to supply all studs and joists of Sperm jaw-ivory for careful selection; the forge was hoisted from idleness; and the blacksmith commanded at once to forge whatever iron contrivances might be needed.

## Chapter 7

Seat thyself sultanically among Saturn’s moons and take abstracted man alone; he seems a wonder and a woe. But take mankind in mass, and they are a mob of unnecessary duplicates. Yet the Pequod’s carpenter was no duplicate. Like all sea-going carpenters—especially in whalers—he was, to an off-handed, practical extent, experienced in numerous collateral trades; but singularly efficient, too, in those thousand nameless emergencies that befall a large ship on a three or four years’ voyage in far seas.

His grand stage was his vice-bench, a long, rude, ponderous table with several vices, iron and wood, lashed athwartships against the Try-works’ rear. A belaying pin too large for its hole—into the vice and filed smaller. A lost land-bird made captive—out of right-whale bone and sperm ivory he made a pagoda cage. An oarsman sprains his wrist—he concocts a lotion. Stubb longs for vermilion stars upon his oar-blades—each oar screwed fast, the constellation supplied. A sailor with toothache—the carpenter out pincers and bids him sit; the fellow winces; the carpenter whirls the handle of his wooden vice and signs him to clap his jaw in that, if he would have the tooth drawn. Shark-bone ear-rings? He drills the ears.

Prepared at all points, indifferent and without respect in all; teeth to him were bits of ivory; heads but top-blocks; men capstans. This would argue uncommon vivacity of intelligence; but not precisely so. He was remarkable for a certain impersonal stolidity, shading into the infinite stolidity of the visible world—pauselessly active, yet holding its peace. Oddly dashed at times with an old, crutch-like, antediluvian wheezing humorousness, streaked with a grizzled wit such as might pass the midnight watch on Noah’s beardy forecastle. A life-long wanderer, a stript abstract; an unfractioned integral; living without premeditated reference to this world or the next.

He did not work by reason, nor instinct, nor schooling, but by a deaf and dumb, spontaneous literal process. A pure manipulator; his brain, if ever he had one, must early have oozed into the muscles of his fingers. Like one of those unreasoning yet highly useful multum in parvo Sheffield contrivances—pocket-knife outwardly, but containing screw-drivers, cork-screws, tweezers, awls, pens, rulers, nail-filers, countersinkers. If superiors wanted a screw-driver, they opened that part of him and the screw was fast; if tweezers, took him up by the legs, and there they were. And yet some subtle life-principle—quicksilver or hartshorn—kept him a great part of the time soliloquizing; his body the sentry-box; the soliloquizer on guard, talking to keep himself awake.

## Chapter 8

The Deck—First Night Watch. By two lanterns the carpenter files an ivory joist; bone-dust makes him sneeze. “Drat the file, and drat the bone! That is hard which should be soft, and that is soft which should be hard.” He calls for ferule and buckle-screw; lucky there’s no knee-joint. Enter Ahab. “Well, manmaker!” The length is marked; Ahab feels the cogent vice—“It does pinch some.”—“It will break bones—beware!” The forge glows forward. “Prometheus—what’s he about? He must have white heat.” Ahab deems it meaning that the old Greek who made men should have been a blacksmith, animating them with fire. “While he’s about it, I’ll order a complete man: fifty feet high in his socks; chest modelled after the Thames Tunnel; legs with roots; arms three feet through the wrist; no heart; brass forehead; a quarter-acre of fine brains; and—shall I order eyes to see outwards?—no; put a sky-light on top to illuminate inwards.”

“’Tis but indifferent architecture to make a blind dome; here’s one. No, no, no; I must have a lantern.” The carpenter thrusts a light; Ahab knocks it aside. Then, grave: “Look ye, carpenter. Will it speak well for thy work if, when I mount this leg, I still feel another leg in the same place—my old lost one? Canst thou not drive that old Adam away?” The carpenter has heard a dismasted man never loses the feeling of his old spar. “It is, man. Put thy live leg where mine once was; here is but one leg to the eye, yet two to the soul. Where thou feelest tingling life, there do I. Is’t a riddle?” He drives deeper: “How dost thou know that some entire, living, thinking thing may not be invisibly standing precisely where thou standest, aye, and in thy despite? And if I still feel the smart of my crushed leg, though long dissolved; why mayst not thou feel the fiery pains of hell for ever, and without a body?”

The carpenter quails; Ahab snaps: “Pudding-heads should never grant premises. How long before the leg is done?” “Perhaps an hour.” “Bungle away and bring it.” Then, alone: “Oh, Life! Here I am, proud as Greek god, and yet standing debtor to this blockhead for a bone to stand on! Cursed be that mortal inter-indebtedness which will not do away with ledgers. I would be free as air; and I’m down in the whole world’s books. By heavens! I’ll get a crucible, and dissolve myself down to one small, compendious vertebra. So.”

The carpenter resumes: Stubb ever says he’s queer—queer, queer. Here’s his bedfellow—a stick of whale’s jaw-bone for a wife! He drives one leg to death, spavins the other for life, and now wears out bone legs by the cord. “What a leg! like a real live leg filed to the core. Halloa! I almost forgot the little oval slate—smoothed ivory—where he figures up the latitude. So, so; chisel, file, and sand-paper, now!”

## Chapter 9

According to usage they were pumping the ship next morning; no inconsiderable oil came up with the water—the casks had sprung a bad leak. Starbuck went below to report. The Pequod was drawing nigh to Formosa and the Bashee Isles; in the cabin Ahab, snow-white new ivory leg braced against the table, traced courses on charts of the Japanese coasts.

“Who’s there? On deck! Begone!”—“Captain Ahab mistakes; it is I. The oil in the hold is leaking, sir. We must up Burtons and break out.”—“Up Burtons and break out? Now we near Japan? Heave-to a week to tinker hoops?”—“Either that, sir, or waste in one day more oil than we may make good in a year.”—“Let it leak! I’m all aleak myself. Aye! leaks in leaks! Not only full of leaky casks, but those leaky casks in a leaky ship; and that’s a worse plight than the Pequod’s. Yet I don’t stop to plug my leak.” Starbuck pressed prudence; Ahab flung owners to the Nantucket beach—his conscience was in the ship’s keel.

Starbuck, reddening, dared to be forbearing: “Shall we not understand each other better, Captain Ahab?” Ahab seized a loaded musket from the rack and levelled it: “There is one God that is Lord over the earth, and one Captain that is lord over the Pequod.—On deck!” The mate mastered his blaze: “Thou hast outraged, not insulted me; beware—let Ahab beware of Ahab; beware of thyself, old man.” Ahab paced; “What’s that he said—Ahab beware of Ahab—there’s something there!” Returning the gun, he went to the deck, low to Starbuck: “Thou art but too good a fellow.” Then aloud: “Furl the t’gallant-sails; close-reef the top-sails, fore and aft; back the main-yard; up Burton, and break out in the main-hold.” His orders were executed; and the Burtons were hoisted.

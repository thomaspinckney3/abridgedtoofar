As we walked down the wharf, Queequeg carrying his harpoon, Captain Peleg hailed from his whale-bone wigwam. He let no cannibals aboard unless they showed papers. “He must show that he’s converted,” added Captain Bildad’s hollow voice. “Son of darkness, art thou at present in communion with any Christian church?”

“He’s a member of the First Congregational Church,” said I. “First Congregational—Deacon Deuteronomy Coleman’s?” cried Bildad, taking out his spectacles, rubbing them with his bandana, and peering at Queequeg. “Not very long a member, I guess.” “Nor baptized right,” said Peleg, “or it would have washed some of that devil’s blue off his face.” “Young man,” said Bildad sternly, “thou art skylarking with me—explain thyself, thou young Hittite. What church dost thee mean? answer me.” I explained: “I mean the same ancient Catholic Church to which you and I and Captain Peleg and Queequeg and all of us belong; the great and everlasting First Congregation of this whole worshipping world; only some of us cherish queer crotchets that touch not the grand belief. In that we all join hands.” “Splice, thou mean’st splice hands,” cried Peleg, drawing nearer. “Young man, you’d better ship for a missionary, instead of a fore-mast hand; I never heard a better sermon. Deacon Deuteronomy—why Father Mapple himself couldn’t beat it, and he’s reckoned something. Come aboard, come aboard; never mind about the papers. Tell Quohog there—what’s his name?—to step along. By the great anchor, what a harpoon! Quohog, ever stand in a whale-boat’s head? ever strike a fish?”

Without a word, Queequeg leaped to a boat in the davits; bracing his left knee, he poised his iron: “Cap’ain, you see him small drop tar on water dere? you see him?—well, spose him one whale eye—well, den!” He darted—the iron hissed over Bildad’s broad brim; the glistening spot vanished. “Spos-ee him whale-e eye; why, dad whale dead.”

“Quick, Bildad,” said Peleg, retreating aghast, “get the ship’s papers. We must have Hedgehog—Quohog—in one of our boats. The ninetieth lay, and that’s more than ever was given a harpooneer yet out of Nantucket.”

Down we went; soon Queequeg was enrolled with me. “Quohog there don’t write, does he?” said Peleg. “Dost thou sign thy name or make thy mark?” Unabashed, Queequeg took the pen and copied in the proper place the queer round figure tattooed on his arm—so that, through Peleg’s obstinate mistake, it stood: Quohog. his X mark.

Bildad, steadfastly eyeing him, rose and drew from his drab pockets a tract—“The Latter Day Coming; or No Time to Lose”—and, grasping book and hands together, said, “Son of darkness, remain not for aye a Belial bondsman. Spurn the idol Bell; turn from the wrath to come; steer clear of the fiery pit!” Something of the salt sea yet lingered in old Bildad’s language, heterogeneously mixed with Scriptural and domestic phrases.

“Avast there, Bildad,” cried Peleg, “avast spoiling our harpooneer. Pious harpooneers never make good voyagers; it takes the shark out of ’em. Young Nat Swaine joined the meeting and never came to good; he sheered from whales for fear of after-claps.” “Peleg!” said Bildad, lifting eyes and hands, “thou knowest the fear of death. When this same Pequod had her three masts overboard in that typhoon on Japan, did’st thou not think of Death and the Judgment then?” “Think of Death and the Judgment then?” cried Peleg, marching the cabin, hands deep in pockets. “No! Life was what Captain Ahab and I thought of—how to save all hands; rig jury-masts; make port—that was what I was thinking of.”

Bildad buttoned up and stalked on deck; we followed. He stood quietly overlooking the sailmakers—now and then stooping to save a patch, or an end of tarred twine.

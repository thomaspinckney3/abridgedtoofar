Under easy sail we swept four cruising-grounds—the Azores; the Cape de Verdes; the Plate off the Rio de la Plata; and the Carrol Ground southerly from St. Helena—when, one serene and moonlight night, when all the waves rolled by like scrolls of silver; and, by their soft, suffusing seethings, made what seemed a silvery silence, not a solitude; on such a silent night a silvery jet was seen far in advance of the white bubbles at the bow. Lit by the moon, it looked celestial; seemed some plumed and glittering god uprising from the sea. Fedallah first saw it; for on such nights he stood mast-head look-out with day’s precision—his turban and the moon, companions in one sky. Silent for many nights, he at last let fall that unearthly cry—“There she blows!” Had the trump of judgment blown, they could not have quivered more; yet still they felt no terror; rather pleasure. Every man started as if a winged spirit hailed us; though unwonted the hour, so delirious was the cry that almost every soul desired a lowering.

Ahab, pacing with side-lunging strides, commanded t’gallants, royals, stunsails—every rag—to be set. With mast-heads manned, the piled craft rolled before the wind; the upbearing taffrail-breeze made the hovering deck feel like air, as though two powers warred in her—one to mount to heaven, one to drive yawingly on. Had you watched Ahab’s face, you had seen two things at strife there too; his live leg made lively echoes; his dead limb struck coffin-taps. On life and death this old man walked. But though we shot swiftly, the silvery jet was no more seen that night. Each swore he saw it once, but never twice.

Some days later, again at the same silent hour, again it was announced, again it vanished; so night after night it served us—mysteriously jetted into moon or starlight, disappearing for a day or three, and each time still further in our van—ever alluring us on. With the superstition of their race, and the Pequod’s preternaturalness, some swore that wherever seen—that unnearable spout was cast by one self-same whale; and that whale, Moby Dick. For a time a peculiar dread reigned, as if treacherously beckoning us on that he might turn and rend us in remotest savage seas.

The blue blandness of the weather sharpened this awe; for days we voyaged through seas so lonesomely mild that all space, in repugnance to our vengeful errand, seemed vacating itself of life before our urn-like prow. But turning eastward, the Cape winds howled; the ivory-tusked Pequod bowed to the blast and gored the dark waves till, like showers of silver chips, the foam flew over the bulwarks; the vacuity of life fled, but gave place to sights more dismal. Strange forms darted at our bows; sea-ravens thickened our wake and, spite of our hootings, perched on the stays as if we were some drifting, uninhabited craft. And heaved and heaved, still unrestingly heaved the black sea, as if its vast tides were a conscience; and the great mundane soul were in anguish and remorse for the long sin and suffering it had bred.

Cape of Good Hope, do they call ye? Rather Cape Tormentoso, as of yore; for long allured by the perfidious silences that before had attended us, we found ourselves launched into this tormented sea, where guilty beings transformed into those fowls and these fish, seemed condemned to swim on everlastingly without any haven in store, or beat that black air without any horizon. But calm, snow-white, and unvarying, the solitary jet at times would be descried—its feathered fountain to the sky; still beckoning.

In such blackness Ahab took almost continual command of the drenched and dangerous deck; gloomily reserved; seldom addressing his mates. When all is secured aloft, captains and crews become practical fatalists; and there, with ivory leg in its hole and one hand on a shroud, for hours he gazed dead to windward, sleet half congealing his eyelashes. The crew, driven from the forecastle by bursting seas, swung wordless in bowlines along the waist; by night the same muteness; wordless Ahab stood up to the blast. Once Starbuck, going below, saw him in his floor-screwed chair, hat and coat still dripping; a chart of tides and currents unrolled; his lantern swinging from his clenched hand; his head thrown back, closed eyes pointed towards the tell-tale’s needle. Terrible old man! thought Starbuck with a shudder; sleeping in this gale, still thou steadfastly eyest thy purpose.

## Chapter 1

Call me Ishmael. Some years ago—never mind how long precisely—having little or no money in my purse, and nothing particular to interest me on shore, I thought I would sail about a little and see the watery part of the world. It is a way I have of driving off the spleen and regulating the circulation. Whenever I find myself growing grim about the mouth; whenever it is a damp, drizzly November in my soul; whenever I find myself involuntarily pausing before coffin warehouses, and bringing up the rear of every funeral I meet; and especially whenever my hypos get such an upper hand of me, that it requires a strong moral principle to prevent me from deliberately stepping into the street, and methodically knocking people's hats off—then, I account it high time to get to sea as soon as I can. This is my substitute for pistol and ball. With a philosophical flourish Cato throws himself upon his sword; I quietly take to the ship.

There now is your insular city of the Manhattoes, belted round by wharves as Indian isles by coral reefs—commerce surrounds it with her surf. Look at the crowds of water-gazers there. Posted like silent sentinels all around the town, stand thousands upon thousands of mortal men fixed in ocean reveries—landsmen all, pent up in lath and plaster, tied to counters, nailed to benches, clinched to desks. Inlanders come from lanes and alleys, streets and avenues, north, east, south, and west, yet here they all unite at the water's edge.

Yes, as every one knows, meditation and water are wedded for ever. And still deeper the meaning of that story of Narcissus, who because he could not grasp the tormenting, mild image he saw in the fountain, plunged into it and was drowned. But that same image, we ourselves see in all rivers and oceans. It is the image of the ungraspable phantom of life; and this is the key to it all.

I never go to sea as a passenger, nor as Commodore, nor Cook. I go as a simple sailor, right before the mast, plumb down into the forecastle. They order me about and make me jump from spar to spar, but who ain't a slave? The act of paying is perhaps the most uncomfortable infliction that the two orchard thieves entailed upon us. But _being paid_—what will compare with it?

But wherefore it was that I should now take it into my head to go on a whaling voyage, the invisible police officer of the Fates, who secretly dogs me, can better answer than any one else. Chief among my motives was the overwhelming idea of the great whale himself. Such a portentous and mysterious monster roused all my curiosity. I am tormented with an everlasting itch for things remote. I love to sail forbidden seas, and land on barbarous coasts.

By reason of these things, then, the whaling voyage was welcome; the great flood-gates of the wonder-world swung open, and in the wild conceits that swayed me to my purpose, two and two there floated into my inmost soul, endless processions of the whale, and, mid most of them all, one grand hooded phantom, like a snow hill in the air.

## Chapter 2

I stuffed a shirt or two into my old carpet-bag, tucked it under my arm, and started for Cape Horn and the Pacific. Quitting the good city of old Manhatto, I duly arrived in New Bedford. It was a Saturday night in December. Much was I disappointed upon learning that the little packet for Nantucket had already sailed, and that no way of reaching that place would offer till the following Monday.

For my mind was made up to sail in no other than a Nantucket craft, because there was a fine, boisterous something about everything connected with that famous old island. Besides, though New Bedford has of late been gradually monopolising the business of whaling, Nantucket was her great original—the Tyre of this Carthage—the place where the first dead American whale was stranded. Where else but from Nantucket did those aboriginal whalemen, the Red-Men, first sally out in canoes to give chase to the Leviathan?

Stranded overnight with only a few pieces of silver in my pocket, I paced the streets in the biting cold. I passed the sign of "The Crossed Harpoons"—too expensive and jolly. The bright red windows of the "Sword-Fish Inn" threw fervent rays into the street—too expensive again. Go on, Ishmael. I followed the streets that took me waterward, where the inns would be cheapest, if not the cheeriest.

Such dreary streets! blocks of blackness, not houses, on either hand, and here and there a candle, like a candle moving about in a tomb. Presently I came upon a smoky light from a low, wide building with a door standing invitingly open—stumbled over an ash-box in the porch, and pushing through an interior door, found myself in what seemed the great Black Parliament sitting in Tophet. A hundred black faces turned round in their rows to peer; and beyond, a black Angel of Doom was beating a book in a pulpit. A negro church. Ha, Ishmael—wretched entertainment at the sign of "The Trap!"

Moving on, I at last came to a dim sort of light not far from the docks, and looking up, saw a swinging sign faintly representing a tall straight jet of misty spray, and these words underneath—"The Spouter Inn:—Peter Coffin."

Coffin?—Spouter?—Rather ominous in that particular connexion, thought I. But it looked quiet enough, and cheap enough, and I went in.

## Chapter 3

Entering that gable-ended Spouter-Inn, you found yourself in a wide, low, straggling entry with old-fashioned wainscots, reminding one of the bulwarks of some condemned old craft. On one side hung a very large oil-painting so thoroughly besmoked, and every way defaced, that in the unequal crosslights by which you viewed it, it was only by diligent study and a series of systematic visits that you could any way arrive at an understanding of its purpose. Such unaccountable masses of shades and shadows, that at first you almost thought some ambitious young artist, in the time of the New England hags, had endeavored to delineate chaos bewitched. But what most puzzled and confounded you was a long, limber, portentous, black mass of something hovering in the centre of the picture over three blue, dim, perpendicular lines floating in a nameless yeast. A boggy, soggy, squitchy picture truly, enough to drive a nervous man distracted. Yet was there a sort of indefinite, half-attained, unimaginable sublimity about it that fairly froze you to it, till you involuntarily took an oath with yourself to find out what that marvellous painting meant. But stop; does it not bear a faint resemblance to a gigantic fish? even the great leviathan himself? A final theory: the picture represents a Cape-Horner in a great hurricane, the half-foundered ship weltering there with its three dismantled masts alone visible, and an exasperated whale purposing to spring clean over the craft, in the enormous act of impaling himself upon the three mast-heads.

The opposite wall was hung all over with a heathenish array of monstrous clubs and spears. Mixed with these were rusty old whaling lances and harpoons all broken and deformed. Some were storied weapons. With this once long lance, now wildly elbowed, fifty years ago did Nathan Swain kill fifteen whales between a sunrise and a sunset. And that harpoon—so like a corkscrew now—was flung in Javan seas, and run away with by a whale, years afterwards slain off the Cape of Blanco.

Through the entry and into the public room: low ponderous beams above, old wrinkled planks beneath, such that you would almost fancy you trod some old craft's cockpit on a howling night. In the far corner stood a dark-looking den—the bar—a rude attempt at a right whale's head. Within the vast arched jaw of the whale, wide enough that a coach might almost drive beneath it, a little withered old man bustles among old decanters and bottles—another cursed Jonah who, for their money, dearly sells the sailors deliriums and death.

The landlord told me his house was full, not a bed unoccupied—but I was welcome to share a harpooneer's blanket. Rather than wander further on so bitter a night, I agreed, on condition the harpooneer was not decidedly objectionable. We supped in a cold adjoining room, nothing but two dismal tallow candles, each in a winding sheet—but dumplings, good heavens! dumplings for supper. The dark-complexioned harpooneer had not yet appeared. He eats nothing but steaks, the landlord told me, and he likes 'em rare.

I grew suspicious of this absent stranger and resolved that if we were to share a bed, he must undress and get into it before I did. The bar-room filled after supper; then suddenly a tramping of sea boots, and in rolled the Grampus's crew—three years out, a full ship—enveloped in shaggy watch coats, beards stiff with icicles, like an eruption of bears from Labrador. They made straight for the whale's jaw of a bar. Among them I observed one man who held somewhat aloof, his sober face unwilling to spoil his shipmates' hilarity. He stood full six feet in height, with noble shoulders, and a chest like a coffer-dam; his face deeply brown and burnt, white teeth dazzling by contrast; and in the deep shadows of his eyes floated some reminiscences that did not seem to give him much joy. When the revelry had mounted to its height, this man slipped away unobserved. Presently he was missed, and a cry went up—"Bulkington! Bulkington! where's Bulkington?"—and they darted out of the house in pursuit.

It grew late, and still no harpooneer. At midnight the landlord offered the cryptic intelligence that the man had gone out peddling and couldn't sell his head. A baffling exchange followed, ending in the revelation that the harpooneer—lately arrived from the south seas—had purchased a lot of embalmed New Zealand heads, great curiosities, and was trying to sell the last one that very night, it being too late for such commerce on the coming Sabbath. This cleared up the mystery, though it hardly settled my unease: what was I to think of a harpooneer who kept Saturday-night hours engaged in selling the heads of dead idolaters?

The landlord eventually persuaded me upstairs to a small room cold as a clam, furnished with a prodigious bed almost big enough for any four harpooneers to sleep abreast. I examined the room: a hammock lashed up and thrown in one corner, a large seaman's bag, outlandish bone fish hooks on a shelf, a tall harpoon standing at the head of the bed. On the chest I found a strange object I could compare to nothing but a large door mat, ornamented at the edges with little tinkling tags, with a hole or slit in the middle like a South American poncho. I put it on to try it, and never saw such a sight in my life. I tore myself out of it in a hurry and gave myself a kink in the neck.

Eventually I blew out the light and tumbled into bed. I had nearly made a good offing towards the land of Nod when I heard a heavy footfall in the passage, and saw a glimmer of light under the door.

The stranger entered holding a light in one hand and that identical New Zealand head in the other. He was a dark, purplish, yellow colour, here and there stuck over with large blackish squares. Not sticking-plasters—tattoos. His bald, purplish head looked for all the world like a mildewed skull. He fumbled in his bag and produced a sort of tomahawk and a seal-skin wallet. Then he took off his hat—a new beaver hat—and there was no hair on his head, nothing but a small scalp-knot twisted up on his forehead.

I am no coward, but ignorance is the parent of fear, and being completely nonplussed about the stranger, I confess I was now much afraid of him. He continued undressing: his chest and arms and back and very legs were checkered with the same dark squares as his face. He must be some abominable savage shipped aboard a whaleman in the South Seas. A peddler of heads—perhaps the heads of his own brothers. He might take a fancy to mine—heavens! look at that tomahawk!

But now the savage produced from his coat pocket a curious little deformed image with a hunch on its back, exactly the colour of a three days' old Congo baby—a wooden idol, as I presently determined. He set it up like a tenpin between the andirons of the empty fire-place, then placed shavings carefully before it, laid a bit of ship biscuit on top, and applied a flame. He made a polite offer of the scorched biscuit to the little negro. The idol never moved his lips. All these strange antics were accompanied by still stranger guttural noises, the devotee praying in a sing-song, his face twitching in the most unnatural manner. At last he extinguished the fire and bagged the idol again in his coat pocket as carelessly as if it were a dead woodcock.

He then took up his tomahawk, examined it, held it to the light, put the handle to his mouth—and puffed out great clouds of tobacco smoke. The next moment the light was extinguished, and this wild cannibal, tomahawk between his teeth, sprang into bed with me. I sang out; he began feeling me; guttural responses; and then—"Who-e debel you?—you no speak-e, dam-me, I kill-e." The lighted tomahawk flourished about me in the dark, scattering hot tobacco ashes till I thought my linen would get on fire.

"Landlord, for God's sake, Peter Coffin!" shouted I. "Landlord! Watch! Coffin! Angels! save me!"

At that moment the landlord came in with a light. "Don't be afraid now," said he, grinning, "Queequeg here wouldn't harm a hair of your head."

The landlord spoke to the harpooneer in their shared pidgin, and Queequeg, puffing at his pipe, motioned me into the bed with his tomahawk and threw the clothes to one side—in not only a civil but a really kind and charitable way.

The man's a human being just as I am, thought I; he has just as much reason to fear me, as I have to be afraid of him. Better sleep with a sober cannibal than a drunken Christian.

I told the landlord to instruct Queequeg to stash his tomahawk and stop smoking, and I would turn in with him. Queequeg complied, and again politely motioned me to bed, rolling over to one side as much as to say—"I won't touch a leg of ye."

I turned in, and never slept better in my life.

## Chapter 4

Upon waking next morning about daylight, I found Queequeg's arm thrown over me in the most loving and affectionate manner. The counterpane was of patchwork, full of odd little parti-coloured squares and triangles; and his arm, tattooed all over with an interminable Cretan labyrinth of a figure, looked for all the world like a strip of that same patchwork quilt. Indeed, partly lying on it as the arm did when I first awoke, I could hardly tell it from the quilt, they so blended their hues together; and it was only by the sense of weight and pressure that I could tell that Queequeg was hugging me.

My sensations were strange. When I was a child, a somewhat similar circumstance befell me. I had been sent to bed in disgrace on the longest afternoon of the year, and lay there dismally in the full light, the sounds of coaches and gay voices reaching me, feeling worse and worse. At last I must have fallen into a troubled doze; and slowly waking from it, I opened my eyes to find the room wrapped in outer darkness. Instantly a shock ran through all my frame: a supernatural hand seemed placed in mine, and the nameless, unimaginable, silent form to which it belonged seemed closely seated by my bed-side. I lay there frozen with awful fears, not daring to drag away my hand; to this very hour I often puzzle myself with it.

Now, take away the awful fear, and my sensations at feeling Queequeg's pagan arm thrown round me were very similar in their strangeness. I strove to rouse him—"Queequeg!"—but his only answer was a snore. Throwing aside the counterpane, there lay the tomahawk sleeping by the savage's side, as if it were a hatchet-faced baby. By dint of much wriggling and expostulation I succeeded in extracting a grunt; he drew back his arm, shook himself all over like a Newfoundland dog just from the water, and sat up stiff as a pike-staff, rubbing his eyes.

When at last his mind seemed made up touching the character of his bedfellow, he jumped out upon the floor and by certain signs gave me to understand that he would dress first and then leave the whole apartment to myself. Thinks I, Queequeg, this is a very civilized overture; but, the truth is, these savages have an innate sense of delicacy, say what you will.

He commenced dressing at top by donning his beaver hat, and then—still minus his trowsers—hunted up his boots. His next movement was to crush himself, boots in hand and hat on, under the bed; from sundry violent gaspings I inferred he was hard at work booting himself. He was just enough civilized to show off his outlandishness in the strangest possible manners. At last he emerged with his hat very much dented and crushed down over his eyes, and began creaking and limping about the room.

He proceeded to wash himself—restricting his ablutions to his chest, arms, and hands. Then, taking up a piece of hard soap, he lathered his face, and I watched to see where he kept his razor—when lo and behold, he takes the harpoon from the bed corner, slips out the long wooden stock, unsheathes the head, whets it a little on his boot, and striding up to the bit of mirror against the wall, begins a vigorous scraping, or rather harpooning of his cheeks.

The rest of his toilet was soon achieved, and he proudly marched out of the room, wrapped up in his great pilot monkey jacket, and sporting his harpoon like a marshal's baton.

## Chapter 5

I quickly followed suit, and descending into the bar-room accosted the grinning landlord very pleasantly. I cherished no malice towards him, though he had been skylarking with me not a little in the matter of my bedfellow. A good laugh is a mighty good thing, and rather too scarce a good thing; the more's the pity.

The bar-room was now full of the boarders who had been dropping in the night previous—nearly all whalemen; chief mates, and second mates, and third mates, and sea carpenters, and sea coopers, and sea blacksmiths, and harpooneers, and ship keepers; a brown and brawny company, with bosky beards; an unshorn, shaggy set, all wearing monkey jackets for morning gowns.

You could pretty plainly tell how long each one had been ashore. This young fellow's healthy cheek is like a sun-toasted pear in hue; he cannot have been three days landed from his Indian voyage. That man next him looks a few shades lighter. In the complexion of a third still lingers a tropic tawn, but slightly bleached withal. But who could show a cheek like Queequeg? which, barred with various tints, seemed like the Andes' western slope, to show forth in one array contrasting climates, zone by zone.

At the breakfast table, to my no small surprise, nearly every man maintained a profound silence—these sea-dogs, many of whom without the slightest bashfulness had boarded great whales on the high seas and duelled them dead without winking, now sat looking round as sheepishly at each other as though they had never been out of sight of some sheepfold among the Green Mountains. A curious sight; these bashful bears, these timid warrior whalemen!

But as for Queequeg—why, Queequeg sat there among them, at the head of the table, as cool as an icicle—reaching over it with his harpoon, to the imminent jeopardy of many heads, grappling the beefsteaks towards him. Every one knows that in most people's estimation, to do anything coolly is to do it genteelly.

When breakfast was over he withdrew into the public room, lighted his tomahawk-pipe, and sat there quietly digesting and smoking with his inseparable hat on, while I sallied out for a stroll.

## Chapter 6

If I had been astonished at first catching a glimpse of so outlandish an individual as Queequeg circulating among the polite society of a civilized town, that astonishment soon departed upon taking my first daylight stroll through the streets of New Bedford.

In thoroughfares nigh the docks, any considerable seaport will frequently offer to view the queerest looking nondescripts from foreign parts. But New Bedford beats all Water Street and Wapping. In these last-mentioned haunts you see only sailors; but in New Bedford, actual cannibals stand chatting at street corners; savages outright; many of whom yet carry on their bones unholy flesh. It makes a stranger stare.

Besides the Feegeeans, Tongatobooarrs, Erromanggoans, Pannangians, and Brighggians, and the wild specimens of the whaling-craft which unheeded reel about the streets, you will see other sights still more curious, certainly more comical. There weekly arrive scores of green Vermonters and New Hampshire men, all athirst for gain and glory in the fishery—mostly young, of stalwart frames, fellows who have felled forests and now seek to drop the axe and snatch the whale-lance. Look there! that chap strutting round the corner wears a beaver hat and swallow-tailed coat, girdled with a sailor-belt and sheath-knife. Here comes another with a sou'-wester and a bombazine cloak. Ah, poor Hay-Seed! how bitterly will burst those straps in the first howling gale, when thou art driven, straps, buttons, and all, down the throat of the tempest.

But think not that this famous town has only harpooneers, cannibals, and bumpkins to show her visitors. New Bedford is a land of oil—and of corn and wine, strange as it sounds. Nowhere in all America will you find more patrician-like houses, parks and gardens more opulent. Whence came they? Go and gaze upon the iron emblematical harpoons round yonder lofty mansion, and your question will be answered. Yes; all these brave houses and flowery gardens came from the Atlantic, Pacific, and Indian oceans. One and all, they were harpooned and dragged up hither from the bottom of the sea.

In New Bedford, fathers, they say, give whales for dowers to their daughters, and portion off their nieces with a few porpoises a-piece. In summer time, the town is sweet to see; full of fine maples—long avenues of green and gold. And in August, high in air, the beautiful and bountiful horse-chestnuts, candelabra-wise, proffer the passer-by their tapering upright cones of congregated blossoms.

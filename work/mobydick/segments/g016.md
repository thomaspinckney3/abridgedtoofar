## Chapter 1

They had been breaking out the hold for the leak, tierce by tierce, till the piled decks were hard to get about and the hollow hull echoed under foot like empty catacombs, the ship reeling like an air-freighted demijohn—top-heavy “as a dinnerless student with all Aristotle in his head.” Well was it the Typhoons came not then.

In that dank underworld labored poor Queequeg, the harpooneer—holder among whalemen—crawling half-naked amid slime like a green-spotted lizard at the bottom of a well. There he caught a chill that lapsed to fever, and in a few days wasted till little seemed left but frame and tattooing. Yet as all else thinned, his eyes filled with a strange soft lustre, rounding like rings on water—circles of Eternity. An awe stole over you as you watched the waning savage sway in his hammock, the rolling sea rocking him as if to his destined heaven.

Resigned to death, he asked a curious favor. In Nantucket he had seen little dark-wood canoes—coffin-canoes—where dead whalemen lay; a custom like his people’s, who send embalmed warriors seaward to the starry archipelagoes. He shuddered at a hammock-burial, tossed to the sharks; desired rather a canoe without a keel, like a whale-boat, “though that involved but uncertain steering, and much lee-way adown the dim ages.” Aft, the carpenter was bidden to do his will, and from heathenish coffin-coloured lumber took Queequeg’s measure, chalking him limb by limb. “Ah! poor fellow! he’ll have to die now,” cried the Long Island sailor.

When the last nail was driven, the coffin lid planed and hinged, Queequeg commanded it brought. Leaning from his hammock, he regarded it long; then had his harpoon’s iron laid within, with a paddle, biscuits round the sides, a flask of fresh water at the head, a small bag of woody earth at the foot, and a sail-cloth pillow. He was lifted in, called for Yojo from his bag, crossed his arms with Yojo between, and had the “hatch” placed. Only his composed countenance showed. “Rarmai” (it will do; it is easy), he murmured; and was replaced in his hammock.

Pip, hovering near, sobbed and took his hand, tambourine in the other: “Now, Queequeg, die; and I’ll beat ye your dying march.” Then, wilder: “Queequeg dies game!—mind ye that; Queequeg dies game! … but base little Pip, he died a coward.” Starbuck, looking down the scuttle, muttered of fevers and ancient tongues; “to my fond faith, poor Pip, in this strange sweetness of his lunacy, brings heavenly vouchers.”

But when every preparation for death was made, Queequeg suddenly rallied. He had “just recalled a little duty ashore,” he said, and had changed his mind about dying. Was life and death in a man’s sovereign will? “Certainly,” he answered. Mere sickness could not kill him: nothing but a whale, or a gale.

Savage and civilized differ: the savage half-well again in a day. So in good time my Queequeg leaped up, stretched, yawned a little, sprang to his boat’s head, poised a harpoon, and pronounced himself fit for a fight. With wild whimsiness he used his coffin as a sea-chest, stowing his clothes therein, and spent spare hours carving the lid with grotesques, striving rudely to copy the hieroglyphic tattooing that a departed seer had once pricked on him—a mystical treatise of heavens and earth, and on the art of attaining truth. Queequeg in his proper person was a riddle to unfold; a wondrous work in one volume; yet its live heart could not read its own text. “Oh, devilish tantalization of the gods!” cried Ahab, turning from the sight.


## Chapter 2

Emerging by the Bashee Isles upon the great South Sea, I could have greeted my dear Pacific with uncounted thanks: that serene ocean rolled a thousand leagues of blue. There is, one knows not what sweet mystery in this sea, whose gently awful stirrings seem to speak of some hidden soul beneath; it zones the world, makes all coasts one bay, the tide-beating heart of earth.

But few thoughts of Pan stirred Ahab’s brain. Standing iron beside the mizen, with one nostril he snuffed the sugary musk from the isles, with the other the salt breath of the sea where the hated White Whale must be swimming. Launched upon these almost final waters, his purpose intensified; in sleep his ringing cry ran through the hull: “Stern all! the White Whale spouts thick blood!”


## Chapter 3

Perth, the begrimed, blistered old blacksmith, kept his portable forge lashed on deck now, incessantly invoked to alter or refit irons and boat-gear. Silent, slow, and solemn, with chronically broken back, he toiled as if toil were life and his hammer its beating—most miserable.

The crew had marked a painful yawing in his gait, and by importunity drew forth his shameful story. One bitter midnight, belated and not innocently, numbness seized him; he sought a leaning barn; the issue was the loss of both feet’s extremities. Part by part came out the drama—four acts of gladness, one long uncatastrophied fifth of grief. Once an excellent artisan with house and garden, a youthful, daughter-like wife, and three blithe children, his shop ringing an iron lullaby through the floors; then the Bottle Conjuror, slid in by a fatal cork, shrivelled up his home. Blow by blow the hammer fell farther between and fainter; the bellows sank; cinders choked the forge; the house was sold; the mother dived into church-yard grass; two children followed; the houseless old man staggered off—a vagabond in crape.

Death seems the only desirable sequel; yet Death is but the salutation to the immense Remote. To eyes that long yet scruple at suicide, the all-receptive ocean spreads unimaginable terrors and wonders, singing: “Come hither, broken-hearted; here is another life without the guilt of intermediate death.” Hearkening to those voices East and West, by sunrise and eve, the blacksmith’s soul responded, Aye, I come! And so Perth went a-whaling.


## Chapter 4

About mid-day, swathed in a shark-skin apron, Perth stood between forge and anvil, when moody Ahab came with a rusty leathern bag. Sparks flew as the pike-head was hammered. “Are these thy Mother Carey’s chickens, Perth? they burn; but thou—thou liv’st among them without a scorch.” “Because I am scorched all over,” said Perth; “not easily can’st thou scorch a scar.”

Ahab’s voice turned: “Can’st thou smoothe almost any seams and dents?” “Aye; all but one.” Ahab leaned both hands on his shoulders, sweeping one hand across his ribbed brow: “Look ye here—here—can ye smoothe out a seam like this? … glad enough would I lay my head upon thy anvil, and feel thy heaviest hammer between my eyes. Answer!” “Oh! that is the one.”

Away with play. “I, too, want a harpoon made; one that a thousand yoke of fiends could not part; something that will stick in a whale like his own fin-bone. There’s the stuff.” The bag spilled gathered nail-stubs of racing horses’ steel shoes. “The best and stubbornest stuff we work,” said Perth. “I know it,” cried Ahab. “Quick! forge me the harpoon—twelve rods for its shank; then wind, and twist, and hammer these twelve together like tow-line yarns.”

A flaw rejected, worked over again; then Ahab himself took the welding. The forge shot up its intense straight flame; the Parsee passed, bowed to the fire, invoking he knew not what; Ahab looked up, and he slid aside. The tempered shank hissed in the cask; the steam scalded Ahab’s face. “Would’st thou brand me, Perth?—have I been but forging my own branding-iron, then?” “Pray God, not that; yet I fear something. Is not this harpoon for the White Whale?” “For the white fiend!”

“Now for the barbs; thou must make them thyself. Here are my razors.” For a moment Perth eyed them; “Take them, man: I now neither shave, sup, nor pray till—” To temper: “No—no water; I want it of the true death-temper. Tashtego, Queequeg, Daggoo! Will ye give me as much blood as will cover this barb?” Dark nods: three punctures. The barbs were baptized. “Ego non baptizo te in nomine patris, sed in nomine diaboli!” howled Ahab, as the malignant iron devoured the blood.

A hickory pole was fitted; a new tow-line strained like a harp-string; no strandings—“Good!”—and now for the seizings. Rope unstranded, yarns woven round the socket, the pole driven home, the line traced and bound along its length—pole, iron, and rope, like the Three Fates, made one. Ahab stalked away, the ivory leg and hickory ringing along the planks—till a light, half-bantering yet piteous sound was heard: poor Pip’s restless laugh, his strange mummeries not unmeaningly mingled with the ship’s black tragedy, and mocked it.


## Chapter 5

Penetrating the Japanese grounds, in mild weather they would be out twelve, fifteen, twenty hours, paddling, sailing, waiting sixty or seventy minutes for the whales’ uprising—little success for their pains. Under an abated sun upon smooth, slow-heaving swells, the boat light as a birch, waves purring like hearth-stone cats against the gunwale—then dreamy quietude steals on the rover. Beholding the tranquil beauty of the ocean’s skin, one forgets the tiger heart beneath the velvet paw.

Fact and fancy interpenetrate; the distant ship’s masts seem to wade through prairie grass; the very hush and hum make you swear play-wearied children lie sleeping in those solitudes. Nor did such calms fail of some effect on Ahab; but if secret golden keys seemed to open his secret treasuries, yet his breath upon them proved but tarnishing.

“Oh, grassy glades! … Would to God these blessed calms would last.” But warp and woof: “There is no steady unretracing progress in this life … we trace the round again; and are infants, boys, and men, and Ifs eternally.” The foundling’s father is hidden; our souls orphans whose paternity lies in the grave.

That day, gazing far down into the golden sea, Starbuck murmured: “Loveliness unfathomable … let faith oust fact; let fancy oust memory; I look deep down and do believe.” And Stubb, fish-like, leaped in the same light: “I am Stubb, and Stubb has his history; but here Stubb takes oaths that he has always been jolly!”


## Chapter 6

Jolly sights and sounds bore down before the wind: the Nantucket Bachelor, her last cask wedged, hatches bolted, now, in holiday apparel, sailed vain-gloriously among the ships ere pointing home. Red bunting streamed from hats; a whale-boat hung bottom down; the long lower jaw of their last whale swung from her bowsprit; signals of all colours flew. Barrels of sperm were lashed in tops and cross-trees; a brazen lamp nailed to her main truck. So full her luck that beef and bread had been given away for sperm; supplemental casks bartered and stowed even in state-rooms; the cabin table knocked into kindling, the mess dining off an oil-butt head. It was humorously said the cook had headed his boiler and filled it; the steward plugged his coffee-pot and filled it; the harpooneers headed the sockets of their irons and filled them; everything was filled with sperm except the captain’s pantaloons pockets, reserved to thrust his hands into in complacency.

As this glad ship bore down upon the moody Pequod, enormous drums roared; mates danced with olive-hued girls eloped from Isles; aloft, three ebony fiddlers presided. Others tumbled the useless try-works bricks into the sea as if pulling down the Bastille. Lord over all, her captain stood erect.

Ahab, shaggy and black, stood stubborn. The ships crossed wakes—one all jubilations for things past, the other forebodings for things to come. “Come aboard, come aboard!” cried the gay commander, lifting glass and bottle. “Hast seen the White Whale?” gritted Ahab. “No; only heard; don’t believe in him at all. Come aboard!” “Thou art too damned jolly. Sail on. Hast lost any men?” “Not enough to speak of—two islanders.” “Call me an empty ship, and outward-bound. Forward there! Set all sail!”

Thus one went cheerily before the breeze; the other fought against it. Ahab, leaning over the taffrail, eyed the homeward craft, took from his pocket a small vial of Nantucket sand, and, looking from ship to vial, brought two remote associations together.


## Chapter 7

Not seldom, when fortune’s favorites sail close, our bagging sails fill. So with the Pequod: the next day whales were seen, four slain—one by Ahab. Far down the afternoon, when the crimson spearings were done, whale and sun died together on a lovely sea. Soothed again, but only to deeper gloom, Ahab sterned off and watched the final wanings. That strange spectacle of the sperm whale’s death—the head turned sunwards—conveyed a new wondrousness.

“He turns and turns him to it,—how slowly, but how steadfastly, his homage-rendering and invoking brow … He too worships fire; most faithful, broad, baronial vassal of the sun!” Here, far water-locked, beyond all hum of human weal or woe, in candid seas where no tradition tablets rocks, life dies sunwards full of faith; yet no sooner dead than death whirls round the corpse and it heads another way.

“Oh, thou dark Hindoo half of nature … thou art an infidel, thou queen.” The whale’s lesson was not lost. “Oh, trebly hooped and welded hip of power! Oh, high aspiring, rainbowed jet!—that one strivest, this one jettest all in vain! … In vain, oh whale, dost thou seek intercedings with yon all-quickening sun.” Yet the darker half rocks him with a prouder faith. “I am buoyed by breaths of once living things.”

“Then hail, for ever hail, O sea … Born of earth, yet suckled by the sea; though hill and valley mothered me, ye billows are my foster-brothers!”


## Chapter 8

Of the four whales, one had died to windward; Ahab’s boat lay by it all night. The waif lantern cast a troubled glare on the glossy back; sharks tapped the cedar with their tails. All slept but the crouching Parsee. Ahab started: “I have dreamed it again.” “Of the hearses? Have I not said, old man, that neither hearse nor coffin can be thine?” “And who are hearsed that die on the sea?” “Ere thou canst die on this voyage, two hearses must verily be seen by thee on the sea; the first not made by mortal hands; and the visible wood of the last one must be grown in America.” “A strange sight, Parsee.” “Believe it or not, thou canst not die till it be seen.” “And thyself?” “Though it come to the last, I shall still go before thee thy pilot.” “And so appearing, pilot me still? … Well then, I have here two pledges … Take another pledge,” said the Parsee—“Hemp only can kill thee.” “The gallows, ye mean.—I am immortal then, on land and on sea.”


## Chapter 9

Near the season for the Line, the crew’s eyes fixed daily on the nailed doubloon, impatient for the equator order. It came hard upon noon. In that lacquered Japanese sky, Ahab, seated high in his hoisted boat, took his wonted observation through coloured glasses. Beneath him, the Parsee knelt, lids half-hooding his orbs, eyeing the same sun with earthly passionlessness. The sight taken, Ahab pencilled on his ivory leg, then murmured: “Thou sea-mark! thou high and mighty Pilot! thou tellest me truly where I am—but canst thou cast the least hint where I shall be? … Where is Moby Dick? This instant thou must be eyeing him; these eyes of mine look into the very eye that is even now beholding him.”

Handling his cabalistical toy, he muttered: “Foolish toy! babies’ plaything of haughty Admirals … what after all canst thou do but tell the poor, pitiful point where thou thyself happenest to be, and the hand that holds thee: no! not one jot more! … Science! Curse thee, thou vain toy … Level by nature to this earth’s horizon are the glances of man’s eyes … Curse thee, thou quadrant!” He dashed it to the deck. “No longer will I guide my earthly way by thee; the level ship’s compass, and the level dead-reckoning, by log and by line; these shall conduct me. Aye,” lighting to the deck, “thus I trample on thee, thou paltry thing that feebly pointest on high; thus I split and destroy thee!”

A sneering triumph seemed meant for Ahab, a fatalistic despair for himself, on the Parsee’s mute face as he glided away. Awestruck, the seamen clustered till Ahab shouted: “To the braces! Up helm!—square in!” The yards swung; the three masts pirouetted like the Horatii on one steed. Starbuck watched ship and man: “Of all this fiery life, what will remain but one little heap of ashes!” “Aye,” cried Stubb, “but sea-coal ashes … live in the game, and die in it!”


## Chapter 10

Warmest climes nurse the cruellest fangs; in these resplendent seas the direst storms—the Typhoon—burst from cloudless sky like a bomb. Towards evening the Pequod, torn of her canvas, bare-poled, fought a Typhoon striking her ahead. Darkness: sky and sea split with thunder, blazed with lightning that showed disabled masts fluttering with rags. Starbuck held by a shroud on the quarter-deck, glancing aloft at each flash; Stubb and Flask strained to hoist and lash the boats. A windward sea stove Ahab’s boat’s stern-sheets—left it dripping like a sieve. Stubb sang to keep his spirits: “Oh! jolly is the gale … Such a funny, sporty, gamy, jesty, joky, hoky-poky lad, is the Ocean, oh!” “Avast, Stubb,” cried Starbuck; “if thou art brave, hold thy peace.” “I am a coward; I sing or I cut my throat.”

“Markest thou not,” Starbuck seized him, pointing to weather bow, “the gale comes from the eastward, the very course to Moby Dick? And where is that boat stove? In the stern-sheets—his stand-point is stove, man!” He soliloquized: homeward it lightens to leeward. A voice answered through darkness: “Old Thunder!”—Ahab, groping to his pivot-hole, his path suddenly made plain by elbowed lances of fire.

“The rods! the rods!” cried Starbuck. “Drop them over!” “Avast!” Ahab: “Out on privileges! Yet I’ll raise rods on the Himmalehs and Andes, that all the world may be secured; but let them be.” “Look aloft! the corpusants!” All the yard-arms tipped with pallid fire; from each lightning-rod three tapering flames; the three masts burned like giant wax tapers. Daggoo loomed triple-sized; Tashtego’s shark-white teeth gleamed; Queequeg’s tattooing burned like Satanic blue flames. The spell waned; darkness wrapped them again. Stubb muttered it for a sign of luck: three spermaceti candles, masts rooted in a hold soon chock with oil. The flames leaped anew, redoubled.

At the mainmast’s base, full beneath the doubloon and flame, the Parsee knelt in Ahab’s front, head bowed away; sailors hung from rigging like numbed wasps; others stood as Herculaneum skeletons, all eyes upcast. “Aye, aye, men!” cried Ahab. “Look up; the white flame but lights the way to the White Whale! Hand me those mainmast links; I would fain feel this pulse—blood against fire!” He set his foot upon the Parsee, flung his arm high, and stood before the trinity of flames. “Oh! thou clear spirit of clear fire, whom on these seas I as Persian once did worship … I now know thee … thy right worship is defiance. To neither love nor reverence wilt thou be kind; and e’en for hate thou canst but kill; and all are killed. … In the midst of the personified impersonal, a personality stands here. Though but a point … yet while I earthly live, the queenly personality lives in me.” Lightning thickened; nine flames leaped to thrice their height; Ahab pressed his shutter-hands hard upon his aching eyes. “Thou canst blind; but I can then grope. Thou canst consume; but I can then be ashes. … Light though thou be, thou leapest out of darkness; but I am darkness leaping out of light. … Oh, thou foundling fire … thou too hast thy incommunicable riddle. Leap! leap up, and lick the sky! I leap with thee; I burn with thee; would fain be welded with thee; defyingly I worship thee!”

“The boat! the boat!” cried Starbuck. Ahab’s harpoon—the fiendish one—projected from his boat’s bow, its leather sheath gone; from the keen steel barb there came a levelled flame of pale, forked fire, a serpent’s tongue. Starbuck grasped Ahab’s arm: “God, God is against thee, old man; forbear! ’tis an ill voyage! Let me square the yards, and make a fair wind of it homewards, to go on a better voyage.” The panic-stricken crew ran to the braces—though no sail was left. But Ahab dashed the lightning links to deck, snatched the burning harpoon, and waved it like a torch: he would transfix the first man that cast off a rope-end. Petrified, shrinking from the fiery dart, they fell back. “All your oaths to hunt the White Whale are as binding as mine; and heart, soul, and body, lungs and life, old Ahab is bound. And that ye may know to what tune this heart beats; look ye here; thus I blow out the last fear!” With one blast he extinguished the flame. As in a hurricane men fly the neighborhood of a lone gigantic elm, so many mariners ran from him in terror.


## Chapter 11

Ahab by the helm; Starbuck approached. “We must send down the main-top-sail yard, sir. The band works loose and the lee lift is half-stranded. Shall I strike it, sir?” “Strike nothing; lash it. If I had sky-sail poles, I’d sway them up now.” “Sir!—in God’s name!—sir?” “Well.” “The anchors are working. Shall I get them inboard?” “Strike nothing, and stir nothing, but lash everything. The wind rises, but it has not got up to my table-lands yet. Quick—and see to it.” Then aside, scorn stinging him: “By masts and keels! he takes me for the hunch-backed skipper of some coasting smack. Send down my main-top-sail yard! Ho, gluepots! Loftiest trucks were made for wildest winds, and this brain-truck of mine now sails amid the cloud-scud. Shall I strike that? Oh, none but cowards send down their brain-trucks in tempest time. … Oh, take medicine, take medicine!”


## Chapter 12

Stubb and Flask, mounted on the forecastle bulwarks, passed additional lashings over the hanging anchors. Flask pressed him: “Didn’t you once say any ship Ahab sails should pay extra insurance, as though loaded with powder aft and lucifers forward?” “Well, suppose I did? I’ve part changed my flesh—why not my mind? And even if we are powder aft and lucifers forward, how could they get afire in this drenching spray? You’re Aquarius, Flask—might fill pitchers at your coat-collar. Besides, for extra risks the companies have extra guarantees. Here are hydrants. And what’s the mighty difference between holding a mast’s lightning-rod in a storm, and standing by a mast that hasn’t any? No harm can come to the holder unless the mast is first struck. Not one ship in a hundred carries rods; we were in no more danger than ten thousand crews. Would you have every man go about with a small lightning-rod up his hat, like a militia officer’s feather?”

They hammered and hauled. “Seems we lash these anchors as if never to be used again,” said Stubb. “Tying them is like tying a man’s hands; what big generous hands they are—iron fists! I wonder if the world is anchored anywhere; if she is, she swings with an uncommon long cable.” The knot went down. “Next to touching land, lighting on deck is most satisfactory. Wring out my jacket skirts, will ye? They laugh at long-togs; but a long-tailed coat in storms carries off the water, d’ye see. Same with cocked hats: cocks form gable-end eave-troughs. No more monkey-jackets and tarpaulins for me; I’ll mount a swallow-tail and drive down a beaver.” A squall took him: “Halloa! whew! there goes my tarpaulin overboard; Lord, Lord, that the winds that come from heaven should be so unmannerly! This is a nasty night, lad.”


## Chapter 13

Aloft on the main-top-sail yard, Tashtego passed new lashings, the thunder hard by. “Um, um, um. Stop that thunder! Plenty too much thunder up here. What’s the use of thunder? Um, um, um. We don’t want thunder; we want rum; give us a glass of rum. Um, um, um!” The black vault broke and boomed above him; he worked on, muttering the same refrain between knots and heaves—“Um, um, um!”—while the yards creaked, the canvas thrashed its tatters, and the pale fires, spent and gone, left only the rain and thunder for company.

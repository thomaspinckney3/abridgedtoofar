Queequeg’s Ramadan—his day of Fasting and Humiliation—was to last till nightfall. I respect all men’s obligations, never mind how comical, and could not find it in my heart to undervalue even a congregation of ants worshipping a toad-stool; or those other creatures in certain parts of our earth, who with a degree of footmanism quite unprecedented in other planets, bow down before the torso of a deceased landed proprietor merely on account of the inordinate possessions yet owned and rented in his name. We good Presbyterian Christians should be charitable in these things, and not fancy ourselves so vastly superior because of other mortals’ half-crazy conceits. There was Queequeg, now, certainly entertaining the most absurd notions about Yojo and his Ramadan;—but what of that? Queequeg thought he knew what he was about, I suppose; he seemed to be content; and there let him rest. Heaven have mercy on us all—Presbyterians and Pagans alike—for we are all somehow dreadfully cracked about the head, and sadly need mending.

Towards evening I knocked; no answer. The door was fast within. Peering the key-hole I saw—strange!—the wooden shaft of his harpoon leaning by the wall, though Mrs. Hussey had taken it overnight. Then surely he was inside. “Apoplexy!” cried the chamber-maid; down came Mrs. Hussey with mustard-pot and vinegar-cruet. “The axe! the axe!” I cried. She thrust out her countenance—“Do you talk of prying open any of my doors?” She felt in the closet under the landing—Queequeg’s harpoon was missing. “He’s killed himself! Paint me a sign: ‘no suicides permitted here, and no smoking in the parlor.’” Her key turned; the supplemental bolt held. I ran for a start; she caught at me; I dashed—crash went the door; and there sat Queequeg, cool and self-collected, squatting on his hams, holding Yojo on his head like a carved image.

All we said, we could drag out no word. He had sat so eight or ten hours, fasting. I wondered if this could possibly be a part of his Ramadan; do they fast on their hams that way in his native island? It can’t last for ever, and his Ramadan only comes once a year. I left him; supped; listened to plum-pudding tales till near eleven; returned—there he squatted still. I besought him to shake himself; no reply. I threw my bearskin over him against the cold night and lay down; Think of it; sleeping all night in the same room with a wide awake pagan on his hams in this dreary, unaccountable Ramadan! But at last I slept. With dawn’s first glimpse he rose—stiff joints—limped to me, pressed his forehead to mine: his Ramadan was over.

Now, as I before hinted, I have no objection to any person’s religion, be it what it may, so long as that person does not kill or insult any other person, because that other person don’t believe it also. But when a man’s religion becomes really frantic; when it is a positive torment to him; and, in fine, makes this earth of ours an uncomfortable inn to lodge in; then I think it high time to take that individual aside and argue the point with him. I lectured him—rise and progress of primitive religions; present sects; how Lents, Ramadans, and prolonged ham-squattings in cold rooms are stark nonsense; bad for health; useless for soul; opposed to common sense. Besides, fasting makes the body cave in; hence the spirit caves in; and all thoughts born of a fast must necessarily be half-starved. This is the reason why most dyspeptic religionists cherish such melancholy notions about their hereafters. In one word, hell is an idea first born on an undigested apple-dumpling; and since then perpetuated through the hereditary dyspepsias nurtured by Ramadans. Had he dyspepsia? “Once,” he said—after his father the king’s great feast: fifty enemies killed, cooked and eaten that evening. “No more,” I cried, shuddering; I knew the rest.

My sermon little moved him; he looked at me with a sort of condescending concern and compassion, as though he thought it a great pity that such a sensible young man should be so hopelessly lost to evangelical pagan piety. At last we dressed; and Queequeg, taking a hearty breakfast of chowders, we sallied toward the Pequod, picking our teeth with halibut bones.

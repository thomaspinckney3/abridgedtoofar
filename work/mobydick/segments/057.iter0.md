The Town-Ho’s Story.

(As told at the Golden Inn.)

The Cape of Good Hope, and all the watery region round about there, is like a noted four corners of a great highway, where you meet more travellers than in any other part. Not long after speaking the Goney, we encountered another homeward-bound whaleman, the Town-Ho,* manned almost wholly by Polynesians. In the short gam that ensued she gave us strong news of Moby Dick. To some the general interest in the White Whale was wildly heightened by a circumstance of the Town-Ho’s story, which seemed obscurely to involve with the whale a wondrous, inverted visitation of one of those so called judgments of God. This secret part, with its particular accompaniments, never reached Ahab or his mates; the captain of the Town-Ho himself knew it not. It was the private property of three confederate white seamen of that ship; one of whom, it seems, communicated it to Tashtego with Romish injunctions of secrecy; but the following night Tashtego rambled in his sleep, and revealed so much that, when he was wakened, he could not well withhold the rest. Such the strange delicacy of those Pequod seamen who came to the full knowledge of it, that it never transpired abaft the main-mast. Interweaving, in its proper place, this darker thread with the story publicly narrated on the ship, I now proceed to put the whole on lasting record.

*The ancient whale-cry upon first sighting a whale from the mast-head, still used by whalemen.

For my humor’s sake, I shall preserve the style in which I once narrated it at Lima, to a lounging circle of my Spanish friends, one saint’s eve, smoking upon the thick-gilt tiled piazza of the Golden Inn. Of those fine cavaliers, the young Dons, Pedro and Sebastian, were on the closer terms with me; hence the interluding questions they occasionally put, and which are duly answered at the time.

“Some two years prior to my first learning the events which I am about rehearsing to you, gentlemen, the Town-Ho, Sperm Whaler of Nantucket, was cruising in your Pacific here, not very many days’ sail eastward from the eaves of this good Golden Inn—somewhere to the northward of the Line. One morning, upon handling the pumps, according to daily usage, it was observed that she made more water in her hold than common. They supposed a sword-fish had stabbed her. The captain, having some unusual reason for believing that rare good luck awaited him in those latitudes, was averse to quit them; the leak, not then deemed dangerous, could not be found; still they cruised, the men relieving at the pumps at wide and easy intervals. But no luck came; the leak sensibly increased; so, taking alarm, the captain made all sail for the nearest harbor among the islands, to heave out and repair.

“Though no small passage lay before her, yet, if the commonest chance favored, he feared not foundering; his pumps were of the best, and six-and-thirty men could keep her free. In truth, she had all but certainly arrived in safety at her port, had it not been for the brutal overbearing of Radney, the mate, a Vineyarder, and the bitterly provoked vengeance of Steelkilt, a Lakeman and desperado from Buffalo.”

“Lakeman!—Buffalo! Pray, what is a Lakeman, and where is Buffalo?” said Don Sebastian.

“On the eastern shore of our Lake Erie, Don; but—you shall soon hear further. This Lakeman, though an inlander, had been wild-ocean nurtured upon our grand fresh-water seas—Erie, Ontario, Huron, Superior, Michigan—ocean-like in expansiveness and peril; their shores as various as any Atlantic; their fleets wrecked out of sight of land, however inland. He was a tall and noble animal, with a head like a Roman, a flowing golden beard; in him a brain, and heart, and soul which had made Steelkilt Charlemagne, had he been born son to Charlemagne’s father. Radney, the mate, was ugly as a mule; hardy, stubborn, malicious; and he did not love Steelkilt, and Steelkilt knew it.”

“Canallers!” cried Don Pedro, when, anon, I named the two comrades Steelkilt presently shook from aloft. “We have seen many whale-ships, but never heard of your Canallers.”

“Boatmen of our grand Erie Canal, Don; a Venetianly corrupt and lawless life flows three hundred and sixty miles through cities and forests; ‘Corrupt as Lima,’ say your own; and the world’s one Lima, Don Pedro cried, ‘No need to travel!’—but the story.”

It was when the leak gained so as to require an hour or more at the pumps every day, that Radney betrayed solicitude; being a part owner, said some. That evening, as they wrought at the brakes, the clear water from the pumps rippled like a mountain spring across the deck. Steelkilt, gay as a cricket, bantered: “Aye, it’s a lively leak; hold a cannikin, and let’s have a taste. Old Rad’s investment must go for it! He had best cut away his part of the hull and tow it home. The sword-fish has come back with saw-fish and file-fish; improvements below, boys. If old Rad were here, I’d tell him to jump overboard and scatter ’em.”

“Damn your eyes! what’s that pump stopping for?” roared Radney, pretending not to hear. “Thunder away at it!”

Quitting the pump at last, Steelkilt went forward, panting, and sat on the windlass, his face fiery, his eyes bloodshot. Then, intolerably striding along the deck, the mate commanded him to get a broom and sweep down, and also a shovel to remove certain piggish offensiveness.

Sweeping at sea is boy’s work; and Steelkilt, captain of a pump-gang, was by usage exempt. He answered in his ordinary tone, broken only by exhaustion, that sweeping was not his business, and he would not do it; pointing to three lads, the customary sweepers. With an oath, Radney reiterated his command, advancing with an uplifted cooper’s club-hammer.

For all his ire, Steelkilt’s strange forbearance—felt most by valiant men—held him rooted. The hammer shook within an inch of his face.

Steelkilt rose, slowly retreating round the windlass, followed by the mate. “Mr. Radney, I will not obey you. Take that hammer away, or look to yourself.”

The predestinated man came closer; Steelkilt, stabbing him with his eye, hissed that if the hammer but grazed his cheek he would murder him. Immediately the hammer touched the cheek; the next instant the lower jaw of the mate was stove in his head; he fell on the hatch spouting blood like a whale.

Ere the cry could go aft, Steelkilt was shaking a backstay to where two of his comrades stood their mast-heads—both Canallers. Down they slid like baleful comets. The three junior mates and four harpooneers crowded the Lakeman to the deck; but Steelkilt and his desperadoes broke through, gained the forecastle, hastily slewed three or four large casks in line with the windlass, and entrenched themselves behind the barricade.

“Come out of that, ye pirates!” roared the captain, menacing with a pistol in each hand. Steelkilt leaped on the casks and strode, defying the worst, but declared distinctly that his death would be the signal for a murderous mutiny. Fearing lest this might be true, the captain desisted a little, yet commanded them to turn to.

“Will you promise not to touch us, if we do?” demanded Steelkilt.

“Turn to!—I make no promise. Do you want to sink the ship? Turn to!”

“Sink the ship?” cried Steelkilt. “Aye, let her sink. Not a man of us turns to, unless you swear not to raise a rope-yarn against us.”

They parleyed; at last, Steelkilt led his men down into the forecastle, like bears into a cave. The captain leaped the barricade; the slide of the scuttle was drawn; the heavy brass padlock clicked upon ten in number, leaving on deck some twenty neutral.

Twice a day for three days the key turned; water and a couple handfuls of biscuit were lowered; yells refused the summons. On the fourth morning four burst up ready to turn to; on the fifth, three others. Only three were left.

Enraged by the defection of seven, maddened by long entombment, Steelkilt proposed to the two Canallers to burst out at the next call, armed with the keen crescentic mincing knives, and run amuck from bowsprit to taffrail; if possible, seize the ship. The scheme met with no opposition; each swore to be first up the ladder; but the leader reserved that priority. Then the foul play came out: each, in his soul, had lit on the same treachery—to be foremost in breaking out, in order to be the first to surrender, and so purchase pardon. They bound the sleeping Lakeman with cords; gagged him; shrieked for the captain; and, shoving him up bound, claimed the honor of securing one ripe for murder. Collared, they were dragged like dead cattle; seized up into the mizzen rigging side by side, like quarters of meat, and there they hung till morning.

At sunrise, the captain summoned all hands; he would flog the rebels round, he said, but, considering their timely surrender, let them go with a reprimand. “But as for you, ye carrion rogues,” turning to the two in the rigging—“for you, I mean to mince ye up for the try-pots;” and he laid on till they lifelessly hung their heads sideways, as the crucified thieves are drawn. “There is still rope enough left for you, my fine bantam,” he cried to Steelkilt. “Take that gag from his mouth.”

The exhausted mutineer painfully twisted his head: “What I say is this—and mind it well—if you flog me, I murder you!”

“Say ye so? then see how ye frighten me,” said the captain, drawing off.

“Best not,” hissed the Lakeman.

“But I must”—and the rope was raised—when Steelkilt hissed something inaudible to all but the captain. To the amazement of all, the captain started back, paced, threw down his rope, and said, “I won’t do it—let him go—cut him down!”

But a pale man with a bandaged head arrested the mates—Radney, the chief mate. Mumbling that he was willing and able to do what the captain dared not, he snatched the rope and advanced.

“You are a coward!” hissed the Lakeman.

“So I am, but take that.” He made good his word, spite of the threat—whatever it had been. The three were cut down; all hands turned to; and sullenly the iron pumps clanged as before.

After dark, the two trembling traitors besieged the cabin door, durst not consort with the crew, and were put down in the run for salvation. No further mutiny appeared. Mainly at Steelkilt’s instigation, the men resolved to obey all orders, and, when the ship reached port, desert in a body. To end the voyage speedily, they agreed not to sing out for whales. The Town-Ho still maintained her mast-heads; the captain would lower for a fish that moment as on the first day; and Radney, with his bandaged mouth, would change berth for boat.

Meanwhile, Steelkilt kept his own counsel concerning private revenge. Radney had an unseamanlike way of sitting on the bulwarks at night, dozing between the boat and ship with the sea below. Steelkilt plaited, at his leisure, a close netting for an iron ball. “What are you making there?” said a shipmate. “Like a lanyard for your bag.” “Yes, rather oddish; but it will answer.” Lacking twine, he went to Radney: he asked quietly for some to mend his hammock; it was given; and twine and lanyard were seen no more. In the fore-ordaining soul of Steelkilt the mate lay already stark and stretched, his forehead crushed in.

But a fool saved the would-be murderer. Just between daybreak and sunrise of the second day, when washing down decks, a stupid Teneriffe man, drawing water in the main-chains, shouted all at once, “There she rolls! there she rolls!” Jesu, what a whale! It was Moby Dick.

“Moby Dick!” cried Don Sebastian. “St. Dominic! Sir sailor, do whales have christenings? Whom call you Moby Dick?”

“A very white, and famous, and most deadly immortal monster, Don;—but that were too long now.”

All was a phrensy. “The White Whale!—the White Whale!” was the cry. The dogged crew eyed askance the vast milky mass that, lit by a horizontal sun, shifted and glistened like a living opal in the blue morning sea. A strange fatality pervades these events. The mutineer was bowsman to the mate; when fast, it was his duty to sit next him and haul line at command. The mate’s boat got the start; none howled more fiercely than Steelkilt as he strained at his oar. Their harpooneer got fast; spear in hand, Radney sprang to the bow—ever a furious man in a boat—crying, through bandage, to beach him on the whale’s topmost back. His bowsman hauled him up and up through blinding foam that blent two whitenesses together, till suddenly the boat struck like against a sunken ledge, and, keeling, spilled out the standing mate. The boat righted and was dashed aside by the swell; Radney was tossed into the sea on the other flank. For an instant, dim through spray, he strove to remove himself from the eye of Moby Dick. But the whale rushed round in a sudden maelstrom; seized the swimmer between his jaws; rearing high, plunged headlong, and went down.

At the first tap of the boat’s bottom, the Lakeman had slackened the line to drop astern from the whirlpool; calmly looking on, he thought his own thoughts. A sudden, terrific, downward jerk quickly brought his knife to the line: he cut it; the whale was free. At some distance Moby Dick rose again, with tatters of Radney’s red woollen shirt caught in the teeth that had destroyed him. All four boats gave chase; the whale eluded them and wholly disappeared.

In good time the Town-Ho reached her port—a savage, solitary place. Headed by the Lakeman, all but five or six of the foremastmen deserted among the palms; eventually seizing a large double war-canoe and setting sail for some other harbor. The ship’s company being reduced to a handful, the captain called upon Islanders to assist in heaving down to stop the leak; but unresting vigilance over their dangerous allies, and extreme hard work, so weakened the whites that, the vessel ready again, he durst not put off in so heavy a craft. He anchored as far off shore as possible; ran out two cannon from the bows; stacked muskets on the poop; warned the Islanders not to approach; then, with one man, set sail in his best whale-boat for Tahiti, five hundred miles, to procure men.

On the fourth day he descried a large canoe; he steered away; but it bore down. The voice of Steelkilt hailed him to heave to, or be run under. The captain presented a pistol. With one foot on each prow of the yoked canoes, the Lakeman laughed him to scorn; if the pistol so much as clicked, he would bury him in bubbles and foam.

“What do you want of me?” cried the captain.

“Where are you bound? and for what are you bound?” demanded Steelkilt; “no lies.”

“I am bound to Tahiti for more men.”

“Very good. Let me board you a moment—I come in peace.” He swam to the boat; climbed the gunwale; stood face to face. “Cross your arms, sir; throw back your head. Now, repeat after me: As soon as Steelkilt leaves me, I swear to beach this boat on yonder island, and remain there six days. If I do not, may lightnings strike me!”

“A pretty scholar,” laughed the Lakeman. “Adios, Senor!” He swam back; watched the boat fairly beached and drawn to the cocoa-nut roots; then made sail again, and in due time arrived at Tahiti, his own place of destination. There luck befriended him: two ships about to sail for France wanted precisely his number; they embarked, and so for ever got the start of their former captain. Some ten days after the French sailed, the whale-boat arrived; the captain enlisted certain more civilized Tahitians; chartered a small native schooner; returned; found all right; and again resumed his cruisings.

Where Steelkilt now is, none know; but upon Nantucket, the widow of Radney still turns to the sea which refuses to give up its dead; still in dreams sees the awful white whale that destroyed him.

“Are you through?” said Don Sebastian, quietly.

“I am, Don.”

“Then I entreat you, tell me if to the best of your own convictions, this your story is in substance really true? It is so passing wonderful! Did you get it from an unquestionable source?”

“Is there a copy of the Holy Evangelists in the Golden Inn, gentlemen?”

“Nay,” said Don Sebastian; “but I know a worthy priest near by, who will quickly procure one for me. I go for it.—Will you be so good as to bring the priest also, Don?”

“This is the priest; he brings you the Evangelists,” said Don Sebastian, gravely, returning with a tall and solemn figure.

“Let me remove my hat. Now, venerable priest, further into the light, and hold the Holy Book before me that I may touch it.

“So help me Heaven, and on my honor the story I have told ye, gentlemen, is in substance and its great items, true. I know it to be true; it happened on this ball; I trod the ship; I knew the crew; I have seen and talked with Steelkilt since the death of Radney.”

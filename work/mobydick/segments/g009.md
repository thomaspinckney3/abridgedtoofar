## Chapter 1

Ere long I shall paint to you, as well as one may without canvas, something like the true form of the whale when his absolute body is moored alongside, and you may fairly step upon him. But first, let us advert to those curious imaginary portraits which even to-day confidently challenge the landsman’s faith. It is time to set the world right by proving such pictures all wrong.

From the primal Hindoo, Egyptian, and Grecian sculptures, down to the Christian painters, license has prevailed. In the famous cavern-pagoda of Elephanta, the Matse Avatar—half man, half whale—gives a tail more like an anaconda’s taper than the broad palms of the true flukes. Guido’s Perseus and Hogarth’s “Perseus Descending” do no better: howdahs on backs; a distended tusked mouth like the Traitors’ Gate; a huge corpulence undulating without drawing one inch of water. The book-binder’s dolphin-whale, winding like a vine-stalk round an anchor, picturesque but fabulous, is a bequeathed revival trick; dolphins once were popularly supposed a species of Leviathan.

Go to old voyagers and “scientific” delineators. In Harris’s plates from a Dutch Spitzbergen voyage (1671), white bears run over raft-like whales; elsewhere, the prodigious blunder of perpendicular flukes. Captain Colnett’s “Physeter,” drawn by scale, supplies an eye which, applied to a full-grown sperm whale, would be a bow-window some five feet long—why not Jonah looking out of it? Goldsmith’s “whale” is much like an amputated sow, and his “narwhale” a hippogriff palmed upon schoolboys. Lacépède’s systems are incorrect; even Scoresby says his Mysticetus has no counterpart in nature. But to cap all—Frederick Cuvier’s Sperm Whale (1836) is not a Sperm Whale, but a squash.

As for sign-painters’ Richard III. whales, with dromedary humps and boats for breakfast, let them hang over oil-shops. Yet are these manifold mistakes not so surprising. Most drawings are from stranded fish: as a wrecked ship with broken back ill-images her proud hull at sea, so the stranded whale falsifies his living swells and undulations. The living Leviathan has never yet fairly floated himself for his portrait; afloat, his vast bulk is out of sight; and out of that element no mortal can hoist him whole into the air. Nor does the skeleton yield his contour. As the great Hunter says, the skeleton bears the same relation to the fully invested animal as the insect to the chrysalis that roundly envelopes it. Mark, too, the side fin: its bones almost exactly answer to the human hand, minus only the thumb—index, middle, ring, and little finger—yet all forever mittened in flesh. “However recklessly the whale may sometimes serve us,” said humorous Stubb, “he can never be truly said to handle us without mittens.”

So conclude: the great Leviathan must remain unpainted to the last. One portrait may hit nearer than another; none can hit with any considerable exactness. The only tolerable idea of his living contour is by going a-whaling yourself; but in so doing, you run no small risk of being eternally stove and sunk. Wherefore, be not too fastidious in your curiosity touching this Leviathan.

## Chapter 2

Waiving monstrous stories, take the outlines. Of the Sperm Whale, Colnett’s and Frederick Cuvier’s are poor; Huggins’s better; by great odds Beale’s are best. His “boats attacking Sperm Whales,” however it may stir parlor scepticism, is admirably correct and life-like in general effect. Some of J. Ross Browne’s sperm drawings are pretty in contour, ill-engraved. Of the Right Whale, Scoresby’s best outlines are drawn too small to convey a desirable impression, and he has but one scene—lamentably few, since only good scenes give any truthful idea of the living whale as seen by his hunters.

By far the finest, though in some details not the most correct, are two large French engravings after Garnery—attacks on Sperm and Right Whale. In the first, a noble Sperm Whale, just risen from the profundities, bears high upon his back the wreck of a stoven boat, its prow balancing upon the spine; in that prow, for one single incomputable flash, stands an oarsman, half shrouded by the incensed boiling spout, leaping as from a precipice. The half-emptied line-tub floats; spilled harpoon-poles bob; swimmers’ heads scatter in affright; the ship bears down in the black distance. In the second, a boat draws alongside a barnacled Right Whale rolling his weedy bulk like a mossy rock-slide; jets erect and black as soot; sea-fowl pick at his pestilent back; he leaves tons of tumultuous white curds in his wake; while behind, in admirable contrast, a glassy calm, drooping sails, and an inert dead whale with the capture-flag in his spout-hole.

Who Garnery was I know not; but my life for it he had practical converse with his subject, or else was marvellously tutored. The French are the lads for painting action: see Versailles’ hall of battles. Not wholly unworthy of a place there are these sea battle-pieces. With not one tenth England’s experience and not the thousandth of America’s, they have furnished the only finished sketches conveying the spirit of the hunt. English and American draughtsmen present mechanical outlines—the vacant profile of a whale; Scoresby, renowned, after Greenland full-lengths and narwhal miniatures, gives boat-hooks, chopping knives, grapnels, and ninety-six magnified Arctic snow crystals.

Add H. Durand’s two: a quiet noon among Pacific isles—a French whaler anchored, lazily taking water; loosening sails and palm leaves droop in the breezeless air; the hardy fishermen under oriental repose. The other—hove-to on the open sea with a Right Whale alongside; cutting-in; a boat pushing off, mast just stepped, harpoons and lances levelled; the little craft rearing like a horse; the smoke of the boiling whale going up like smithies; to windward, a black cloud with earnest of squalls quickens the seamen.

## Chapter 3

On Tower-hill a kedger holds up a painted board of the tragic scene in which he lost his leg—three whales, three boats; one boat, presumed to contain the missing leg in all its integrity, crunched by jaws. Ten years he has shown that picture and that stump to an incredulous world; his time of justification is come: his whales are as good as any published in Wapping; his stump unquestionable; but he makes no stump-speech—only ruefully contemplates his own amputation.

Throughout the Pacific and in Nantucket, New Bedford, and Sag Harbor, you will come across lively sketches graven by the fishermen themselves on Sperm Whale-teeth and ladies’ busks of Right Whale-bone—skrimmshander—wrought with the jack-knife alone. Long exile restores a man to savagery; your true whale-hunter is as much a savage as an Iroquois. With the savage’s wonderful patience, with one poor shark’s-tooth—his knife—he carves bone sculpture, as close-packed in mazy design as Achilles’s shield, and full of barbaric suggestiveness as Albert Durer.

Wooden whales from South Sea war-wood hang in forecastles; brass whales by the tail knock at gable doors; sheet-iron whales weather-cock on spires. In bony, ribby regions of earth, at the base of broken cliffs, you will discover Leviathan petrified in grass; in mountainous lands, along amphitheatrical ridges, the profiles of whales; but you must be a thorough whaleman, and mark your intersecting latitude and longitude, else you will lose your Soloma.

Nor fail to trace great whales in the starry heavens, with boats pursuing them; round and round the Pole I have chased him with the revolutions of the bright points; beneath the Antarctic skies I have boarded Argo-Navis, and joined the chase against Cetus beyond Hydrus and the Flying Fish. With a frigate’s anchors for bridle-bitts and fasces of harpoons for spurs, would I could mount that whale and leap the topmost skies!

## Chapter 4

Steering north-east from the Crozetts, we fell in with vast meadows of brit—the minute, yellow substance whereon the Right Whale largely feeds—so that for leagues we seemed to sail through golden wheat. Numbers of Right Whales, secure from a Sperm Whaler like the Pequod, swam with open jaws through the brit, which, adhering to the wondrous Venetian blind of their mouths, was thus strained from the water.

From the mast-heads they looked like lifeless masses of rock, as strangers in India pass recumbent elephants for blackened mounds; and even when recognized, their bulk makes it hard to believe such overgrowth can be instinct with the same life that lives in a dog. In specialties, where in the sea do you find a fish answering to the sagacious kindness of the dog? The accursed shark alone bears any generic analogy.

Though to landsmen the sea’s natives are unsocial and repelling; though the sea is an everlasting terra incognita; though the most terrific disasters indiscriminately befall those who go upon the waters; though, for ever and for ever, the sea will insult and murder man, pulverizing the stateliest frigate; nevertheless, repetition has dulled our sense of its full awfulness. The first boat floated on an ocean that with Portuguese vengeance had whelmed a world without leaving even a widow. That same ocean rolls now; that same ocean destroyed the wrecked ships of last year. Yea, foolish mortals, Noah’s flood is not yet subsided; two thirds of the fair world it yet covers.

Wherein differ sea and land, that a miracle upon one is not a miracle upon the other? The live ground opened under Korah and swallowed them for ever; yet not a modern sun sets, but the live sea in precisely the same manner swallows ships and crews. Nor is the sea only a foe to alien man; it is a fiend to its own offspring, dashing the mightiest whales against the rocks, and leaving them with the split wrecks of ships. No mercy, no power but its own controls it; the masterless ocean overruns the globe.

Consider its subtleness; its most dreaded creatures glide unapparent beneath loveliest azure. Consider the devilish brilliance and beauty of its remorseless tribes; consider its universal cannibalism. Consider all this; then turn to the green, docile earth; consider both; and do you not find an analogy to something in yourself? For as this appalling ocean surrounds the verdant land, so in the soul of man there lies one insular Tahiti, full of peace and joy, but encompassed by all the horrors of the half-known life. God keep thee! Push not off from that isle, thou canst never return!

## Chapter 5

Wading slowly through brit, the Pequod held on toward Java, a gentle air impelling her; the long burnished sun-glade lay like a golden finger enjoining secrecy; even the slippered waves whispered. In this hush Daggoo, aloft, saw a strange spectre: a great white mass lazily rose, disentangling from the azure, gleaming like a snow-slide new slid from the hills; subsiding; rising again. “There! there again! there she breaches! right ahead! The White Whale, the White Whale!” he cried with a stiletto-like yell.

Bare-headed on the bowsprit, Ahab flung his eager glance where the outstretched arm pointed, and with quick intensity gave orders for lowering. The four boats pulled swiftly; the white mass sank; rose in the same spot. Almost forgetting Moby Dick, we gazed at the most wondrous phenomenon yet revealed: a vast pulpy mass, furlongs in length and breadth, of a glancing cream-colour, lying on the water; innumerable long arms radiating, curling and twisting like a nest of anacondas; no face or front; undulating there—an unearthly, formless, chance-like apparition of life. With a low sucking sound it slowly disappeared. “Almost rather had I seen Moby Dick and fought him, than to have seen thee, thou white ghost!” cried Starbuck. Ahab said nothing; turned back to the ship; the rest following silently.

So rarely beheld is the great live squid that it has been invested with portentousness. One and all declare it the largest animated thing in the ocean, yet few have any but vague ideas of its true nature; notwithstanding, they believe it furnishes the sperm whale his only food. Pursued, he will disgorge what are supposed detached arms of the squid, some exceeding twenty and thirty feet. The Kraken of Bishop Pontoppodan may resolve itself into Squid; but abate his prodigious bulk. By some, who have vaguely heard rumors, it is included among the cuttle-fish; to which, indeed, in certain externals, it seems to belong—the Anak of the tribe.

## Chapter 6

For the better understanding of whaling scenes, speak now of the magical, sometimes horrible whale-line. Once of the best hemp, slightly vapored with tar, it is now in American fishery almost entirely of Manilla—less durable, stronger, far more soft and elastic, and, I will add, much more handsome to behold; hemp, dusky as an Indian; Manilla, a golden-haired Circassian.

Only two-thirds of an inch thick, by experiment its one and fifty yarns each suspend one hundred and twenty pounds; the whole rope bears nearly three tons. In length, the common sperm line measures a little over two hundred fathoms. Towards the stern it is spirally coiled in the tub as a round, cheese-shaped mass of densely bedded sheaves; the least kink, in running out, would infallibly take somebody’s arm, leg, or entire body off; hence the utmost precaution in stowing. In English boats, two smaller tubs hold one continuous line; the American single tub, three feet in diameter, makes a prodigious freight for a craft with a half-inch bottom—like critical ice which bears a distributed but not a concentrated weight. With the painted canvas cover clapped on, the boat looks as if pulling off with a great wedding-cake for the whales.

Both ends of the line are exposed. The lower end, eye-spliced, hangs disengaged over the tub’s edge—first, to bend on an additional line from a neighboring boat should the whale sound beyond the first supply; second, for common safety’s sake, lest the doomed boat be dragged down after him. Before lowering, the upper end runs aft round the loggerhead, forward the boat’s length, jogs every oarsman’s wrist, passes between the men to the leaded chocks in the pointed prow where a wooden pin prevents its escape, hangs in a slight festoon over the bows, returns within, and, after a box-coil in the bows, is attached to the short-warp and thence to the harpoon.

Thus the whale-line folds the whole boat in its complicated coils, twisting and writhing round it in almost every direction. To the timid landsman’s eye, the crew seem Indian jugglers, festooned with deadliest snakes. No son of mortal woman first seated amid those hempen intricacies, while thinking the harpoon at any instant may be darted and all these horrible contortions set in play like ringed lightnings, but shudders to his marrow like a shaken jelly. Yet habit—strange thing!—breeds gayer sallies and better jokes over the half-inch white cedar than over your mahogany, while, like the six burghers of Calais, the six men pull into the jaws of death with a halter round every neck.

Hence those repeated disasters of men taken out by the line. When it darts out, to be seated is like being among the whizzings of a steam-engine in full play; only here the boat rocks like a cradle, pitching you without warning; only by a buoyant simultaneousness of volition and action can you escape being made a Mazeppa of, and run away with where the all-seeing sun could never pierce you out. More awful than the storm is the profound calm that precedes it—the wrapper and envelope of the storm—as the graceful repose of the line before play carries truer terror than any other aspect. But why say more? All men live enveloped in whale-lines. All are born with halters round their necks; but it is only when caught in the swift, sudden turn of death, that mortals realize the silent, subtle, ever-present perils of life. And if you be a philosopher, though seated in the whale-boat, you would not at heart feel one whit more terror than before your evening fire with a poker, and not a harpoon, by your side.

## Chapter 7

If to Starbuck the Squid was portent, to Queequeg it was omen. “When you see him ’quid,” said the savage, honing his harpoon in the hoisted bow, “then you quick see him ’parm whale.”

The next day was still and sultry; the crew, spell-bound by vacancy, drowsed. It was my turn at the foremast-head. Idly swaying in enchanted air, my soul went out of my body, though the body swung like a pendulum. Then bubbles burst beneath my closed eyes; gracious agency preserved me; I started—and lo! close under our lee, not forty fathoms off, a gigantic Sperm Whale rolled like a capsized frigate’s hull; his broad glossy back, Ethiopian hue, glistening like a mirror; tranquilly spouting like a portly burgher smoking his pipe. But that pipe, poor whale, was thy last. All sleepers woke; more than a score of voices with the three notes from aloft shouted as the fish regularly spouted sparkling brine.

“Clear away the boats! Luff!” cried Ahab, dashing the helm down ere the helmsman could touch it. The whale turned majestically to leeward with steady tranquillity, making few ripples. Thinking him not yet alarmed, Ahab ordered no oar to be used, no word but whispers. So, seated like Ontario Indians, we silently paddled. Presently, the monster perpendicularly flirted his tail forty feet and sank. “There go flukes!” cried all. Stubb struck his match, lit his pipe—the respite of a sounding. When he rose, Stubb’s boat had the vantage; caution was cast; paddles dropped; oars came loudly into play. Still puffing, Stubb cheered on.

A mighty change had come over the fish—“head out,” obliquely elevating that buoyant, sharp-cutting head, transforming from bluff-bowed galliot to sharp-pointed pilot-boat. “Start her, start her, my men! Don’t hurry—take plenty of time—but start her; start her like thunder-claps, that’s all,” spluttered Stubb through smoke. “Start her, Tash, my boy—keep cool—cucumbers is the word—only start her like grim death and grinning devils!” “Woo-hoo! Wa-hee!” screamed Tashtego; “Kee-hee! Kee-hee!” yelled Daggoo; “Ka-la! Koo-loo!” howled Queequeg. With oars and yells the keels cut the sea.

“Stand up, Tashtego!—give it to him!” The harpoon flew. “Stern all!” Back-water! Something went hot and hissing along every wrist—the magical line. Stubb had caught two extra turns round the loggerhead; a hempen blue smoke jetted up and mingled with his pipe. The blistering line passed through both his hands—like holding an enemy’s sharp two-edged sword by the blade, while he strives to wrest it free. “Wet the line! wet the line!” he cried. The tub-oarsman dashed sea-water with his hat; more turns; the line held; the boat flew like a shark all fins. Stubb and Tashtego changed places—stern for stem—a staggering business in such rocking commotion. The vibrating line, tight as a harpstring, made you think the craft had two keels—one cleaving water, the other air; a continual cascade at the bows; a whirling eddy in the wake; the tall Gay-Header crouched low to bring down his centre of gravity.

The whale slackened. “Haul in—haul in!” Hands pulled the boat up even while towed on; ranging by his flank, Stubb, knee in the clumsy cleat, darted dart after dart; at the word, the boat alternately sterned from the horrible wallow, then ranged for another fling. Red tide poured from all sides like brooks down a hill; he rolled not in brine but in blood, which bubbled and seethed for furlongs. The slanting sun on that crimson pond sent its reflection back into every face, so all glowed like red men. Jet after jet of white smoke shot from his spiracle; vehement puff after puff from Stubb’s mouth; at each dart he hauled in upon his crooked lance, straightened it by blows against the gunwale, and sent it again.

“Pull up—pull up!—close to!” Reaching far over, Stubb slowly churned his long sharp lance into the fish, carefully churning, as if feeling after a gold watch swallowed, fearful of breaking it ere hooking it out. That gold watch was the innermost life. Struck—he started into his “flurry,” horribly wallowing in blood, overwrapping himself in mad, boiling spray; the imperilled craft, dropping astern, struggled blindly from that frenzied twilight to clear air. Abating, he rolled out again; surging; spasmodically dilating and contracting his spout-hole with sharp, cracking respirations. At last, gush after gush of clotted red gore, purple lees of wine, shot into the frighted air and ran down his motionless flanks. His heart had burst. “He’s dead, Mr. Stubb,” said Daggoo. “Yes; both pipes smoked out!” Stubb scattered his dead ashes over the water, and thoughtfully eyed the vast corpse he had made.

## Chapter 8

According to usage, the whale-boat pushes off with the headsman (killer) as temporary steersman, and the harpooneer (fastener) pulling the foremost oar. It needs a strong, nervous arm to strike the first iron; often, in a long dart, flung twenty or thirty feet. Yet, however prolonged the chase, the harpooneer is expected to pull to the uttermost and set an example of superhuman activity—by incredible rowing and loud, intrepid cries. What it is to keep shouting at the top of one’s compass while every other muscle is strained—none know but those who have tried it. In this straining, bawling state, with his back to the fish, he hears, “Stand up, and give it to him!”—drops and secures his oar, turns half round, snatches the iron from the crotch, and with what little strength remains, essays to pitch it. No wonder, out of fifty fair chances, not five are successful; no wonder harpooneers are cursed and disrated; no wonder some burst blood-vessels; no wonder some ships are four years with four barrels; for it is the harpooneer that makes the voyage, and if you take the breath out of his body, how find it there when most wanted?

At the second critical instant, when the whale starts to run, headsman and harpooneer likewise start to running fore and aft, changing places to the jeopardy of all. This is foolish and unnecessary. The headsman should stay in the bows from first to last; he should both dart the harpoon and the lance; no rowing should be expected of him except where obvious. Long experience convinces me that more failures come of the harpooneer’s exhaustion than of the whale’s speed. To insure the greatest efficiency in the dart, the harpooneers of this world must start to their feet from out of idleness, and not from out of toil.

## Chapter 9

The crotch—two feet of peculiarly notched stick—stands perpendicularly in the starboard gunwale near the bow, a rest for the wooden extremity of the harpoon whose naked, barbed end slopingly projects from the prow. Two harpoons repose there, the first and second irons, each by its own cord connected to the line, that both may be darted instant after instant into the same whale; if one draw, the other holds. But at the whale’s convulsive run, the second often cannot be pitched; yet, being bent, must be anticipatorily tossed overboard, else the most terrible jeopardy involves all hands. Thenceforth a dangling, sharp-edged terror, it skittishly curvets about boat and whale, entangling or cutting lines, and is not secured again till the fish is fairly a corpse. Imagine four boats engaging one strong, knowing whale, and eight or ten loose second irons simultaneously dancing about him. Such particulars will elucidate scenes hereafter to be painted.

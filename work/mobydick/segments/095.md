Now this ambergris is a very curious substance, and so important as an article of commerce, that in 1791 a certain Nantucket-born Captain Coffin was examined at the bar of the English House of Commons on that subject. Though the word ambergris is but the French compound for grey amber, yet the two substances are quite distinct. Amber is hard, transparent, brittle, odorless; found on coasts and in some inland soils; for mouth-pieces, beads, ornaments. Ambergris is soft, waxy, highly fragrant and spicy; used in perfumery, in pastiles and pomatum; the Turks use it in cooking; some drop a few grains into claret to flavor it.

Who would think, then, that such fine ladies and gentlemen should regale themselves with an essence found in the inglorious bowels of a sick whale! Yet so it is. By some, ambergris is cause; by others, effect, of the whale’s dyspepsia. How to cure such a dyspepsia it were hard to say, unless by administering three or four boat loads of Brandreth’s pills, and then running out of harm’s way, as laborers do in blasting rocks.

Certain hard, round, bony plates were found in this ambergris; Stubb took them for sailors’ trowsers buttons; they proved pieces of small squid bones embalmed.

That incorruption should be found in the heart of such decay—is this nothing? Bethink thee of St. Paul to the Corinthians—sown in dishonor, raised in glory; remember Paracelsus on what maketh the best musk; forget not that Cologne-water, in rudimental stages, is of all ill savors the worst.

I should like to conclude the chapter with the above appeal, but cannot, owing to my anxiety to repel a charge often made against whalemen. One charge to repel. It is slanderously hinted that all whales always smell bad. The stigma is traceable to the first Greenland whalemen in London, who, not trying out at sea as the Southern ships do, but thrusting fresh blubber through cask bung-holes, unload in the dock a savor like excavating an old city grave-yard for the foundations of a Lying-in Hospital. Add Schmerenburgh or Smeerenberg on Greenland’s coast—the latter name used by the learned Fogo Von Slack, in his great work on Smells, a text-book on that subject—a place for trying out the Dutch fleet’s blubber; when in full operation, no pleasant savor.

But all this is different with a South Sea Sperm Whaler; in a four years’ voyage, after filling her hold, she perhaps consumes not fifty days boiling; and casked, the oil is nearly scentless. The truth is, living or dead, if but decently treated, whales as a species are by no means of ill odor; nor can whalemen be recognised, as the people of the middle ages affected to detect a Jew in the company, by the nose. Indeed, the motion of a Sperm Whale’s flukes above water dispenses a perfume, as when a musk-scented lady rustles her dress in a warm parlor. What then liken the Sperm Whale to for fragrance, considering his magnitude? To that famous elephant, with jewelled tusks, redolent with myrrh, led out of an Indian town to do honor to Alexander the Great.

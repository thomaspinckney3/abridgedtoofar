## Chapter 1 — The Musket.

The Typhoon had hurled the helmsman repeatedly to the deck, and set the compass-needles whirling on their cards. By midnight the gale abated enough that Starbuck and Stubb cut the shivered sails adrift and bent fresh ones; the wind came round astern, and the crew sang out "_Ho! the fair wind! oh-ye-ho, cheerly men!_" Bound by standing orders to report any decided change on deck, Starbuck went below to tell Ahab.

He paused before the cabin door. The lamp cast fitful shadows on the bolted wood. The loaded muskets in the rack stood shiningly revealed.

"He would have shot me once," he murmured. "Yes, there's the very musket that he pointed at me—that one with the studded stock. Loaded? Aye, and powder in the pan. Best spill it?—wait. I'll cure myself of this. I'll hold the musket boldly while I think.—I come to report a fair wind to him. But how fair? Fair for death and doom,—*that's* fair for Moby Dick. It's a fair wind that's only fair for that accursed fish.—The very tube he pointed at me! Aye and he would fain kill all his crew. Does he not say he will not strike his spars to any gale? Has he not dashed his heavenly quadrant? and in these same perilous seas, gropes he not his way by mere dead reckoning of the error-abounding log? But shall this crazed old man be tamely suffered to drag a whole ship's company down to doom with him? Only a fool would try to make him prisoner. I stand alone here upon an open sea, with two oceans and a whole continent between me and law.—Is heaven a murderer when its lightning strikes a would-be murderer in his bed? And would I be a murderer, then, if"—and slowly, stealthily, he placed the loaded musket's end against the door.

"On this level, Ahab's hammock swings within; his head this way. A touch, and Starbuck may survive to hug his wife and child again.—Oh Mary! Mary!—boy! boy! boy!—The wind has gone down and shifted, sir; the fore and main topsails are reefed and set; she heads her course."

"Stern all! Oh Moby Dick, I clutch thy heart at last!"

Such were the sounds from the old man's tormented sleep. The levelled musket shook like a drunkard's arm; Starbuck seemed wrestling with an angel; but turning from the door, he placed the death-tube in its rack, and left the place.

"He's too sound asleep, Mr. Stubb; go thou down, and wake him, and tell him. I must see to the deck here. Thou know'st what to say."

## Chapter 2 — The Needle.

Next morning the sea rolled in long slow billows; the wind abounded so that sky and air seemed vast outbellying sails; the whole world boomed before the wind. The sea was as a crucible of molten gold, that bubblingly leaps with light and heat.

Ahab stood apart, watching how the sun's rays reached ahead and how the same yellow rays blended with his undeviating wake. He exulted—"Ho, ho! all ye nations before my prow, I bring the sun to ye!"—then hurried to the helm, demanding the heading.

"East-sou-east, sir," said the steersman.

"Thou liest!" smiting him. "Heading East, and the sun astern?"

Every soul was confounded. Thrusting his head into the binnacle, Ahab caught one glimpse of the compasses; his uplifted arm slowly fell. The two needles pointed East, and the Pequod was going West.

"I have it! It has happened before. Mr. Starbuck, last night's thunder turned our compasses—that's all."

"Aye; but never before has it happened to me, sir," said the pale mate, gloomily.

Lightning has in more than one case struck vessels and inverted their needles, annihilating all loadstone virtue so that the steel was of no more use than an old wife's knitting needle; nor does the needle ever of itself recover. Ahab coolly ordered the course corrected by the transpointed compasses, and the Pequod thrust her bows back into the opposing wind. Then, spying the crushed sight-tubes of his smashed quadrant underfoot, he called for a lance-head, a top-maul, and a sail-maker's needle.

"My men, the thunder turned old Ahab's needles; but out of this bit of steel Ahab can make one of his own, that will point as true as any."

He struck the iron rod repeatedly, placed the blunted needle endwise upon it, hammered again, then suspended it by linen thread over a compass-card. The needle swung, quivered, and settled. Ahab stepped back and stretched his arm toward it: "Look ye, for yourselves, if Ahab be not lord of the level loadstone! The sun is East, and that compass swears it!"

One after another the crew peered in, and one after another slunk away. In his fiery eyes of scorn and triumph, you then saw Ahab in all his fatal pride.

## Chapter 3 — The Log and Line.

The log and line had lain long untouched beneath the after bulwarks—warped by sun, dampened by spray, rotted by idleness. His mood seizing him after the magnet scene, Ahab remembered his quadrant was gone and his oath about the log and line, and ordered it hove.

The Tahitian and the grizzly Manxman came aft. Ahab unwound thirty turns to toss overboard, when the old Manxman spoke.

"Sir, I mistrust it; this line looks far gone, long heat and wet have spoiled it."

"'Twill hold, old gentleman. Life holds thee; not thou it."

The log was heaved. The loose coils straightened out astern, the reel whirled—and then: snap! The overstrained line sagged down. The log was gone.

"I crush the quadrant, the thunder turns the needles, and now the mad sea parts the log-line. But Ahab can mend all. Let the carpenter make another log, and mend thou the line. See to it."

The Manxman muttered to himself—"To him nothing's happened; but to me, the skewer seems loosening out of the middle of the world"—when Pip came babbling, speaking of himself in the third person as already lost, crying that the line drags hard and perhaps hauls Pip up from the deep. Ahab advanced and hushed the Manxman.

"Hands off from that holiness! Who art thou, boy? I see not my reflection in the vacant pupils of thy eyes."

"Bell-boy, sir; ship's-crier; ding, dong, ding! Pip! Pip! Pip! One hundred pounds of clay reward for Pip; five feet high—looks cowardly—quickest known by that!"

"There can be no hearts above the snow-line. Oh, ye frozen heavens! look down here. Ye did beget this luckless child, and have abandoned him, ye creative libertines. Here, boy; Ahab's cabin shall be Pip's home henceforth, while Ahab lives. Thou touchest my inmost centre, boy; thou art tied to me by cords woven of my heart-strings. Come, let's down."

Pip felt Ahab's hand and murmured, "Ah, now, had poor Pip but felt so kind a thing as this, perhaps he had ne'er been lost! This seems to me, sir, as a man-rope; something that weak souls may hold by. Oh, sir, let old Perth now come and rivet these two hands together; the black one with the white, for I will not let this go."

"Oh, boy, nor will I thee. Come, then, to my cabin. I feel prouder leading thee by thy black hand, than though I grasped an Emperor's!"

"There go two daft ones now," muttered the old Manxman. "One daft with strength, the other daft with weakness."

## Chapter 4 — The Life-Buoy.

Steering by Ahab's levelled steel and his own contrived log, the Pequod held toward the Equator through unfrequented waters, over waves monotonously mild—the strange calm things preluding some riotous and desperate scene.

Near the outskirts of the Equatorial fishing-ground, in deep darkness before the dawn, the watch was startled by a cry so plaintively wild and unearthly—like half-articulated wailings of the ghosts of all Herod's murdered Innocents—that they stood transfixedly listening. The civilised sailors said it was mermaids; the grey Manxman declared they were voices of newly drowned men.

At sunrise a sailor went from his hammock to his mast-head at the fore; and looking up, the crew saw a falling phantom in the air, and looking down, a little tossed heap of white bubbles in the blue of the sea. The life-buoy dropped from the stern, but the sun had so shrunk and parched the cask that it slowly filled and followed the sailor to the bottom.

Thus the first man of the Pequod who mounted the mast to look out for the White Whale, on the White Whale's own peculiar ground, was swallowed up in the deep.

The lost buoy must be replaced; but no cask of sufficient lightness could be found. Then, by certain strange signs, Queequeg hinted a hint concerning his coffin.

"A life-buoy of a coffin!" cried Starbuck, starting.

"It will make a good enough one," said Flask.

"Bring it up; there's nothing else for it," said Starbuck. "Rig it, carpenter."

"And shall I nail down the lid, sir?"

"Aye."

"And shall I caulk the seams, sir?"

"Aye."

"And shall I then pay over the same with pitch, sir?"

"Away! Make a life-buoy of the coffin, and no more."

The carpenter, left to himself, grumbled at this cobbling sort of business. He liked clean, virgin, fair-and-square mathematical jobs; not work that was at an end in the middle and at the beginning at the end. But he would do it tenderly—nail down the lid, caulk the seams, pay it with pitch, batten it tight, and hang it with the snap-spring over the stern; and he would rig thirty separate Turk's-headed life-lines round it, so that if the hull went down, thirty lively fellows would all fight for one coffin.

## Chapter 5 — The Deck.

*The coffin laid upon two line-tubs; the Carpenter caulking its seams; Ahab comes from the cabin-gangway, Pip following.*

"Back, lad; I will be with ye again presently."

He stops, seeing the coffin.

"Life-buoy, sir. Mr. Starbuck's orders."

"Thy coffin lies handy to the vault. Art not thou the leg-maker? And art thou not also the undertaker?"

"Aye, sir; I patched up this thing as a coffin for Queequeg, but they've set me now to turning it into something else."

"Then tell me; art thou not an arrant, all-grasping, intermeddling, monopolising, heathenish old scamp, to be one day making legs, and the next day coffins to clap them in, and yet again life-buoys out of those same coffins? Thou art as unprincipled as the gods, and as much of a jack-of-all-trades."

"But I do not mean anything, sir. I do as I do."

Ahab muses alone: "There's the very dreaded symbol of grim death, by a mere hap, made the expressive sign of the help and hope of most endangered life. A life-buoy of a coffin! Does it go further? Can it be that in some spiritual sense the coffin is, after all, but an immortality-preserver! I'll think of that. But no. So far gone am I in the dark side of earth, that its other side, the theoretic bright one, seems but uncertain twilight to me."

He goes below; Pip is left alone in the great cabin, and in his madness seats himself in Ahab's screwed chair and plays host to imaginary admirals, asking each one whether they have seen Pip.

## Chapter 6 — The Pequod Meets The Rachel.

Next day a large ship, the Rachel, bore directly down upon the Pequod, her spars thickly clustering with men.

"Bad news; she brings bad news," muttered the old Manxman.

"Hast seen the White Whale?" cried Ahab.

"Aye, yesterday. Have ye seen a whale-boat adrift?"

The Rachel's captain came aboard—a Nantucketer Ahab knew. The day before, while three of his boats were in chase of a shoal, Moby Dick had loomed up and the fourth boat, the swiftest, had lowered and seemingly fastened. Then a swift gleam of bubbling white water, and nothing more. The ship had searched through the night, kindling a fire in her try-pots for a beacon, but no trace of the missing boat had been found. Now the captain begged the Pequod to join the search, sailing parallel lines to sweep a double horizon.

"I will wager something," whispered Stubb, "that some one in that missing boat wore off that Captain's best coat—"

"My boy, my own boy is among them. For God's sake—I beg, I conjure"—the stranger Captain turned to Ahab—"For eight-and-forty hours let me charter your ship—you must, and you *shall* do this thing."

"His son!" cried Stubb. "We must save that boy."

What made it more melancholy: not only was one of the captain's sons among the missing boat's crew, but a second son had been separated from the ship in the other boat; for a time the wretched father could not know which to seek. His chief mate had resolved the cruel perplexity by the ordinary rule—pick up the majority first. The missing boy was but twelve years old.

Ahab stood like an anvil, receiving every shock without the least quivering.

"Captain Gardiner, I will not do it. Even now I lose time. Good-bye, good-bye. God bless ye, man, and may I forgive myself, but I must go."

Gardiner silently stepped into his boat and returned to his ship. Long after the two vessels diverged, the Rachel could be seen yawing hither and thither at every dark spot on the sea, her masts and yards thickly clustered with men, as three tall cherry trees when the boys are cherrying among the boughs. But by her still halting course and winding, woeful way, you plainly saw that this ship that so wept with spray, still remained without comfort. She was Rachel, weeping for her children, because they were not.

## Chapter 7 — The Cabin.

*Ahab moving to go on deck; Pip catches him by the hand to follow.*

"Lad, lad, I tell thee thou must not follow Ahab now. The hour is coming when Ahab would not scare thee from him, yet would not have thee by him. There is that in thee, poor lad, which I feel too curing to my malady. Like cures like; and for this hunt, my malady becomes my most desired health. Do thou abide below here, where they shall serve thee, as if thou wert the captain."

"No, no, no! ye have not a whole body, sir; do ye but use poor me for your one lost leg; only tread upon me, sir; I ask no more, so I remain a part of ye."

"If thou speakest thus to me much more, Ahab's purpose keels up in him. I tell thee no; it cannot be."

"Oh good master, master, master!"

"Weep so, and I will murder thee! have a care, for Ahab too is mad. Listen, and thou wilt often hear my ivory foot upon the deck, and still know that I am there. Thy hand!—Met! True art thou, lad, as the circumference to its centre. So: God for ever bless thee; and if it come to that,—God for ever save thee, let what will befall."

Ahab goes. Pip, left alone, stands in the spot where Ahab stood, then sinks into his chair, the vast cabin empty around him, listening for the ivory step above.

## Chapter 8 — The Hat.

Now that the Pequod had chased her foe into these latitudes; now that a vessel had spoken which the very day before had actually encountered Moby Dick; there lurked a something in the old man's eyes which it was hardly sufferable for feeble souls to see. As the unsetting polar star sustains its piercing, steady, central gaze through the livelong arctic night, so Ahab's purpose now fixedly gleamed down upon the constant midnight of the gloomy crew. All humor, forced or natural, vanished. Stubb no more strove to raise a smile; Starbuck no more strove to check one. Alike, joy and sorrow, hope and fear, seemed ground to finest dust, and powdered in the clamped mortar of Ahab's iron soul.

Even as Ahab's eyes so awed the crew's, the inscrutable Parsee's glance awed his. Fedallah was never known to slumber; his wan but wondrous eyes said plainly—*We two watchmen never rest.* The crew saw him standing motionless for hours and doubted whether he were mortal substance or a tremulous shadow cast by some unseen body. Nor could the mariners now step upon the deck unless Ahab was before them—standing in his pivot-hole, pacing between main-mast and mizen, or stationed in the cabin-scuttle with his hat slouched heavily over his eyes, unmoving through day and night, the damp gathering in beads upon his stone-carved coat. He ate in the open air—breakfast and dinner only; never supper; never reaped his beard, which darkly grew all gnarled, as unearthed roots of trees blown over. He went no more below; whatever he wanted from the cabin, that thing he sent for.

Distrustful that even Stubb and Flask might willingly overlook the whale he sought, Ahab resolved to be first aloft. He rigged a nest of basketed bowlines, sent the rope up to the main-mast head, and prepared to be hoisted. Then, sweeping his glance across the crew—long upon Daggoo, Queequeg, Tashtego; shunning Fedallah—he settled his firm relying eye upon the chief mate: "Take the rope, sir—I give it into thy hands, Starbuck." And thus, with one hand clinging round the royal mast, Ahab gazed abroad upon the sea for miles and miles.

It was strange, that of all men Starbuck—almost the one who had ever ventured to oppose him—should be chosen watchman. Yet freely Ahab gave his whole life into that distrusted person's hands.

The first time aloft, a red-billed sea-hawk came wheeling and screaming round his head, then darted a thousand feet straight up, then spiralized back—and before the Sicilian seaman's cry of warning reached him, the sable wing was before the old man's eyes, the long hooked bill at his head, and with a scream the black hawk darted away with his hat.

An eagle flew thrice round Tarquin's head, removing his cap to replace it; and thereupon Tanaquil declared Tarquin would be king of Rome. But only by the replacing of the cap was that omen accounted good. Ahab's hat was never restored; the wild hawk flew on and on with it; far in advance of the prow: and at last disappeared; while from that point of disappearance a minute black spot was dimly discerned, falling from that vast height into the sea.

## Chapter 9 — The Pequod Meets The Delight.

The intense Pequod sailed on; the life-buoy-coffin still lightly swung at her stern; and another ship, most miserably misnamed the Delight, was descried. Across her quarter-deck shears hung the shattered white ribs and splintered planks of what had once been a whale-boat.

"Hast seen the White Whale?"

"Look!" replied the hollow-cheeked captain, pointing to the wreck with his trumpet.

"Hast killed him?"

"The harpoon is not yet forged that ever will do that," answered the other, glancing upon a rounded hammock on the deck where silent sailors were sewing up the canvas. "I bury but one of five stout men, who were alive only yesterday; but were dead ere night. Only *that* one I bury; the rest were buried before they died; you sail upon their tomb."

Snatching Perth's levelled iron, Ahab held it aloft: "Look ye, Nantucketer; here in this hand I hold his death! Tempered in blood, and tempered by lightning are these barbs; and I swear to temper them triply in that hot place behind the fin, where the White Whale most feels his accursed life!"

"Brace forward! Up helm!" cried Ahab as the burial words began.

But the Pequod was not quick enough to escape the splash of the corpse as it struck the sea; and as Ahab glided away, a voice from the Delight's wake cried after him: "In vain, oh, ye strangers, ye fly our sad burial; ye but turn us your taffrail to show us your coffin!"

## Chapter 10 — The Symphony.

It was a clear steel-blue day. The firmaments of air and sea were hardly separable in that all-pervading azure; only, the pensive air was transparently pure and soft, with a woman's look, and the robust and man-like sea heaved with long, strong, lingering swells, as Samson's chest in his sleep. The snow-white wings of small, unspeckled birds glided on high—the gentle thoughts of the feminine air; but down in the bottomless blue rushed mighty leviathans, sword-fish, and sharks—the strong, troubled, murderous thinkings of the masculine sea.

Tied up and twisted; gnarled and knotted with wrinkles; haggardly firm and unyielding; his eyes glowing like coals that still glow in the ashes of ruin; untottering Ahab stood forth in the clearness of the morn, lifting his splintered helmet of a brow to the fair girl's forehead of heaven.

For a moment the lovely aromas of that enchanted air seemed to dispel the cankerous thing in his soul. The step-mother world, so long cruel—forbidding—now threw affectionate arms round his stubborn neck, as if over one that, however wilful and erring, she could yet find it in her heart to save and to bless. From beneath his slouched hat Ahab dropped a tear into the sea; nor did all the Pacific contain such wealth as that one wee drop.

Starbuck drew near and stood behind him. Ahab turned.

"Starbuck! it is a mild, mild wind, and a mild looking sky. On such a day—very much such a sweetness as this—I struck my first whale—a boy-harpooneer of eighteen! Forty—forty—forty years ago!—ago! Forty years of continual whaling! forty years of privation, and peril, and storm-time! for forty years has Ahab forsaken the peaceful land, to make war on the horrors of the deep! And out of those forty years I have not spent three ashore. When I think of this life I have led; the desolation of solitude it has been; the masoned, walled-town of a Captain's exclusiveness—oh, weariness! heaviness! Guinea-coast slavery of solitary command! and how for forty years I have fed upon dry salted fare—away, whole oceans away, from that young girl-wife I wedded past fifty, and sailed for Cape Horn the next day, leaving but one dent in my marriage pillow—wife? wife?—rather a widow with her husband alive! Aye, I widowed that poor girl when I married her, Starbuck. What a forty years' fool—fool—old fool, has old Ahab been! Why this strife of the chase? Oh, Starbuck! is it not hard, that with this weary load I bear, one poor leg should have been snatched from under me? Do I look very old, so very, very old, Starbuck? I feel deadly faint, bowed, and humped, as though I were Adam, staggering beneath the piled centuries since Paradise. God! God! God!—crack my heart!—stave my brain!—mockery! mockery! bitter, biting mockery of grey hairs, have I lived enough joy to wear ye? Close! stand close to me, Starbuck; let me look into a human eye; it is better than to gaze into sea or sky; better than to gaze upon God. I see my wife and my child in thine eye. No, no; stay on board, on board!—lower not when I do; when branded Ahab gives chase to Moby Dick. That hazard shall not be thine."

"Oh, my Captain! my Captain! noble soul! grand old heart, after all! why should any one give chase to that hated fish! Away with me! let us fly these deadly waters! let us home! Wife and child, too, are Starbuck's—wife and child of his brotherly, sisterly, play-fellow youth; even as thine, sir, are the wife and child of thy loving, longing, paternal old age! Away! let us away!—this instant let me alter the course!"

"They have, they have. I have seen them—some summer days in the morning. About this time—yes, it is his noon nap now—the boy vivaciously wakes; sits up in bed; and his mother tells him of me, of cannibal old me; how I am abroad upon the deep, but will yet come back to dance him again."

"'Tis my Mary, my Mary herself! She promised that my boy, every morning, should be carried to the hill to catch the first glimpse of his father's sail! Yes, yes! no more! it is done! we head for Nantucket! Come, my Captain, study out the course, and let us away!"

But Ahab's glance was averted; like a blighted fruit tree he shook, and cast his last, cindered apple to the soil.

"What is it, what nameless, inscrutable, unearthly thing is it; what cozening, hidden lord and master, and cruel, remorseless emperor commands me; that against all natural lovings and longings, I so keep pushing, and crowding, and jamming myself on all the time; recklessly making me ready to do what in my own proper, natural heart, I durst not so much as dare? Is Ahab, Ahab? Is it I, God, or who, that lifts this arm? But if the great sun move not of himself; but is as an errand-boy in heaven; how then can this one small heart beat; this one small brain think thoughts; unless God does that beating, does that thinking, does that living, and not I. By heaven, man, we are turned round and round in this world, like yonder windlass, and Fate is the handspike. And all the time, lo! that smiling sky, and this unsounded sea! But it is a mild, mild wind, and a mild looking sky; and the air smells now, as if it blew from a far-away meadow; they have been making hay somewhere under the slopes of the Andes, Starbuck, and the mowers are sleeping among the new-mown hay. Sleeping? Aye, toil we how we may, we all sleep at last on the field. Sleep? Aye, and rust amid greenness; as last year's scythes flung down, and left in the half-cut swaths—Starbuck!"

But blanched to a corpse's hue with despair, the Mate had stolen away.

Ahab crossed the deck to gaze over on the other side; but started at two reflected, fixed eyes in the water there. Fedallah was motionlessly leaning over the same rail.

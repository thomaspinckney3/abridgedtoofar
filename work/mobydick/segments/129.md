Steering by Ahab’s levelled steel and his own levelled log and line, the Pequod held towards the Equator; making a long passage through unfrequented waters, over waves monotonously mild—strange calms preluding some riotous and desperate scene.

Near the outskirts of the Equatorial fishing-ground, in the deep darkness before the dawn, as she sailed by a cluster of rocky islets, the watch—then headed by Flask—was startled by a cry so plaintively wild and unearthly—like half-articulated wailings of the ghosts of all Herod’s murdered Innocents—that they stood, or sat, or leaned all transfixedly listening, like the carved Roman slave, while that wild cry remained within hearing. The Christian or civilized part of the crew said it was mermaids, and shuddered; but the pagan harpooneers remained unappalled. Yet the grey Manxman—oldest mariner of all—declared those wild thrilling sounds were the voices of newly drowned men.

Below in his hammock, Ahab heard not of it till grey dawn, when, coming to the deck, it was recounted to him by Flask, not unaccompanied with hinted dark meanings. He hollowly laughed, and thus explained the wonder. Those rocky islands the ship had passed were the resort of great numbers of seals; some young that had lost their dams, or some dams that had lost their cubs, must have risen nigh the ship and kept company with her, crying and sobbing with their human sort of wail. This but the more affected some, for most mariners cherish a very superstitious feeling about seals, from their peculiar tones in distress and the human look of their round heads peeringly uprising from the water. In the sea, under certain circumstances, seals have more than once been mistaken for men.

At sunrise a sailor went from his hammock to his mast-head at the fore; and, ere long, a cry and a rushing—and looking up, they saw a falling phantom in the air; and looking down, a little tossed heap of white bubbles in the blue of the sea. The life-buoy dropped from the stern; but the sun had so shrunken and parched the long slender cask that it slowly filled, and the studded iron-bound wood followed the sailor to the bottom, as if to yield him his pillow, though but a hard one.

Thus the first man of the Pequod who mounted the mast to look out for the White Whale, on the White Whale’s own peculiar ground, was swallowed up in the deep. But few, perhaps, thought of that then; indeed, they took it, not as a foreshadowing of evil in the future, but as the fulfilment of an evil already presaged. They declared that now they knew the reason of those wild shrieks heard the night before. But again the old Manxman said nay.

The lost buoy must be replaced; Starbuck was directed to see to it; but no cask of sufficient lightness could be found, and all hands were impatient of any toil not directly bent to the voyage’s end. They were going to leave the stern unprovided, when by certain strange signs and inuendoes Queequeg hinted a hint concerning his coffin.

“A life-buoy of a coffin!” cried Starbuck, starting.

“Rather queer, that, I should say,” said Stubb.

“It will make a good enough one,” said Flask.

“Bring it up; there’s nothing else for it,” said Starbuck, after a melancholy pause. “Rig it, carpenter.”

“And shall I nail down the lid, sir?”

“Aye.”

“And shall I caulk the seams, sir?”

“Aye.”

“And shall I then pay over the same with pitch, sir?”

“Away! Make a life-buoy of the coffin, and no more.”

“He goes off in a huff. The whole he can endure; at the parts he baulks. Now I don’t like this. I make a leg for Captain Ahab, and he wears it like a gentleman; but I make a bandbox for Queequeg, and he won’t put his head into it. Are all my pains to go for nothing with that coffin? And now I’m ordered to make a life-buoy of it. It’s like turning an old coat; going to bring the flesh on the other side now. I don’t like this cobbling sort of business—I don’t like it at all; it’s undignified; it’s not my place. Let tinkers’ brats do tinkerings; we are their betters. I like to take in hand none but clean, virgin, fair-and-square mathematical jobs, something that regularly begins at the beginning, and is at the middle when midway, and comes to an end at the conclusion; not a cobbler’s job, that’s at an end in the middle, and at the beginning at the end. Nail down the lid; caulk the seams; pay over the same with pitch; batten it tight, and hang it with the snap-spring over the stern. Were ever such things done before with a coffin? Some superstitious old carpenters, now, would be tied up in the rigging, ere they would do the job. But I’m made of knotty Aroostook hemlock; I don’t budge. Cruppered with a coffin! Sailing about with a grave-yard tray! But never mind. We workers in woods make bridal-bedsteads and card-tables, as well as coffins and hearses. Any way, I’ll have me thirty separate, Turk’s-headed life-lines, each three feet long, hanging all round to the coffin. Then, if the hull go down, there’ll be thirty lively fellows all fighting for one coffin. Come, hammer, caulking-iron, pitch-pot, and marling-spike! Let’s to it.”

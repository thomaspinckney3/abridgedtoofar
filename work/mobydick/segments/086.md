Some Nantucketers distrust Jonah’s story; but as with some sceptical Greeks and Romans who doubted Hercules and the whale, and Arion and the dolphin, their doubtings made those traditions no whit the less facts.

One old Sag-Harbor whaleman argued from a quaint Bible plate that Jonah’s whale had two spouts—a Right Whale trait, whose swallow is so small “a penny roll would choke him.” Bishop Jebb answers beforehand: Jonah need not be tombed in the belly, but temporarily lodged in some part of the mouth; the Right Whale’s mouth would accommodate a couple of whist-tables and all the players.

Sag-Harbor urged gastric juices; but a German exegetist supposes Jonah took refuge in a floating dead whale—as French soldiers in Russia turned dead horses into tents. Others divine he swam to a vessel with a whale for a figure-head; and some opined the “whale” meant a life-preserver—an inflated bag. Poor Sag-Harbor, therefore, seems worsted all round. But he had still another reason for his want of faith. It was this, if I remember right:

Jonah was swallowed in the Mediterranean, and after three days was vomited within three days’ journey of Nineveh, on the Tigris—very much more than three days’ march from the nearest Mediterranean shore. How is that? But was there no other way for the whale to land the prophet within that short distance of Nineveh? Yes. He might have carried him round by the way of the Cape of Good Hope. Such a supposition involves the complete circumnavigation of Africa in three days; and the Tigris is too shallow for whales. Besides, this idea of Jonah’s weathering the Cape of Good Hope at so early a day would wrest the honor of the discovery of that great headland from Bartholomew Diaz, its reputed discoverer, and so make modern history a liar. But all these foolish arguments of old Sag-Harbor only evinced his foolish pride of reason—a thing still more reprehensible in him, seeing that he had but little learning except what he had picked up from the sun and the sea. I say it only shows his foolish, impious pride, and abominable, devilish rebellion against the reverend clergy. For a Portuguese Catholic priest advanced this very idea of Jonah’s going to Nineveh via the Cape of Good Hope as a signal magnification of the general miracle; and so it was. Besides, to this day the highly enlightened Turks devoutly believe; and an old English traveller speaks of a Turkish Mosque built in Jonah’s honor, with a miraculous lamp that burnt without oil.

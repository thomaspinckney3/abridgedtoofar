The foregoing matter requires familiar enlargement, to take away incredulity touching the natural verity of the main points. I shall not proceed methodically, but by citations practically known to me as a whaleman.

First: I have personally known three instances in which a harpooned whale escaped, and, after an interval—even three years—was again struck by the same hand and slain; when the two irons, each marked with the same private cypher, were taken from the body. In the three-year instance I myself was in the boat both times, and recognized a huge mole under the whale’s eye.

Secondly: In the fishery there have been whales popularly cognisable at distant times and places, not solely for bodily peculiarity (for that the try-works soon end), but for their terrible prestige; most fishermen were content to recognise such a one by merely touching their tarpaulins when he lounged by on the sea, without seeking closer acquaintance. Like some poor devils ashore that happen to know an irascible great man, they make distant unobtrusive salutations to him in the street, lest if they pursued the acquaintance further, they might receive a summary thump for their presumption. Was it not so, O Timor Tom! thou famed leviathan, scarred like an iceberg, haunting the Oriental straits? Was it not so, O New Zealand Jack! thou terror of all cruisers near the Tattoo Land? Was it not so, O Morquan! King of Japan, whose lofty jet at times seemed a snow-white cross? Was it not so, O Don Miguel! thou Chilian whale, marked like an old tortoise with mystic hieroglyphics upon the back! Some of these, having wrought havoc, were gone in quest of, systematically hunted, chased, and killed by valiant captains, as Captain Butler hunted Annawon through Narragansett woods.

I do not know where I can find a better place than just here to make mention of one or two other things, which to me seem important, as in printed form establishing in all respects the reasonableness of the whole story of the White Whale, more especially the catastrophe; for this is one of those disheartening instances where truth requires full as much bolstering as error. So ignorant are most landsmen of some of the plainest and most palpable wonders of the world, that without some hints touching the plain facts they might scout at Moby Dick as a monstrous fable, or still worse and more detestable, a hideous and intolerable allegory. Not one disaster in fifty finds record; yet on one Pacific voyage, among the ships we spoke, each had a death by a whale, some more than one; three had each lost a boat’s crew. For God’s sake, be economical with your lamps and candles! not a gallon you burn but at least one drop of man’s blood was spilled for it.

People ashore admit a whale’s enormousness, but when narrating some specific example of this two-fold enormousness, they have significantly complimented me upon my facetiousness; when, I declare upon my soul, I had no more idea of being facetious than Moses, when he wrote the history of the plagues of Egypt. I will rest on independent testimony that the sperm whale can, with aforethought, stave in, utterly destroy, and sink a large ship—and has done it.

In 1820 the ship Essex, Captain Pollard, of Nantucket, lowered for a shoal of sperm whales. A very large one, escaping from the boats, bore directly down upon the ship; dashing his forehead against her hull, he so stove her in that in less than ten minutes she settled and fell over; not a plank survives. Owen Chace, the chief mate, wrote a plain narrative: “It was anything but chance which directed his operations; he made two several attacks upon the ship ahead, thereby combining the speed of the two objects for the shock; his aspect was most horrible, indicative of resentment and fury.”

In 1807 the ship Union, also of Nantucket, was totally lost off the Azores by a like onset. And Commodore J——, sceptical over a whaling-captains’ dinner in Oahu that any whale could make his stout sloop-of-war leak so much as a thimbleful, was presently met at sea by a portly sperm whale who fetched him such a thwack that, with all pumps going, he ran for the nearest port to heave down. I am not superstitious; but I consider the Commodore’s interview providential. The sperm whale will stand no nonsense.

Take Langsdorff: “An uncommon large whale, the body larger than the ship itself, lay almost at the surface; the ship, in full sail, was almost upon him; it was impossible to prevent striking. The monster, setting up its back, raised the ship three feet at least out of the water... we thought we had struck a rock; but the creature sailed off solemnly.” The Captain D’Wolf here alluded to—a New Englander, my uncle—now resides near Boston. I have particularly questioned him concerning this passage in Langsdorff. He substantiates every word. The ship, however, was by no means a large one: a Russian craft built on the Siberian coast, and purchased by my uncle after bartering away the vessel in which he sailed from home.

Lionel Wafer, Dampier’s chum, tells of a terrible shock at sea at four in the morning; guns leaped; men were shaken from hammocks; no ground found with the lead. He imputed it to an earthquake; but I should not wonder if an unseen whale vertically bumped the hull.

As for strength and malice: lines made fast to a running whale have towed a ship in a calm as a horse walks off with a cart; more than once a struck sperm whale, rallying, has pursued boats back to the ship; and upon attack he often opens his mouth and retains it so for minutes, eloquent indication of his character.

But I must be content with only one more and a concluding illustration; a remarkable and most significant one, by which you will not fail to see, that not only is the most marvellous event in this book corroborated by plain facts of the present day, but that these marvels (like all marvels) are mere repetitions of the ages; so that for the millionth time we say amen with Solomon—Verily there is nothing new under the sun.

In the sixth century Procopius, a sober historian, records a great sea-monster captured in the Propontis after destroying vessels at intervals for more than fifty years. He does not name the species; but as he stove ships, and as sperm whales have in isolated instances entered Mediterranean waters by the Dardanelles, and as squid—his food—lurks in those bottoms, I am strongly inclined to think Procopius’s monster a sperm whale. Put these statements together, and, according to all human reasoning, you will perceive the reasonableness of the whole story of the White Whale, catastrophe and all.

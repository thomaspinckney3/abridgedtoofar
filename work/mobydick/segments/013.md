Returning to the Spouter-Inn from the Chapel, I found Queequeg alone before the fire, feet on the stove hearth, holding close to his face his little negro idol, whittling at its nose with a jack-knife, humming in his heathenish way. Interrupted, he put up the image; then took a large book, and, placing it on his lap, began counting the pages with deliberate regularity; at every fiftieth, as I fancied, he stopped and gave a long gurgling whistle of astonishment; then recommenced at one—as if he could not count beyond fifty, and only the multitude of fifties excited him.

Savage though he was and hideously marred to my taste, something in his countenance by no means displeased. You cannot hide the soul: through the unearthly tattooings I thought I saw traces of a simple honest heart; in his large deep eyes, fiery black and bold, tokens of a spirit that would dare a thousand devils; and a certain lofty bearing—he looked like a man who had never cringed, and never had a creditor. Phrenologically, his head reminded me of General Washington’s—the same retreating slope and projecting brows: Queequeg was George Washington cannibalistically developed.

He heeded me not; he consorted little with the other seamen; made no advances; seemed content with his own companionship—equal to himself. Savages are strange beings. At first they are overawing; their calm self-collectedness of simplicity seems a Socratic wisdom. Here was a man some twenty thousand miles from home, by the way of Cape Horn, that is—which was the only way he could get there—thrown among people as strange to him as though he were in the planet Jupiter; and yet he seemed entirely at his ease; preserving the utmost serenity; content with his own companionship; always equal to himself. Surely there was fine philosophy there, unknowing itself. But, perhaps, to be true philosophers, we mortals should not be conscious of so living or so striving. So soon as I hear that such or such a man gives himself out for a philosopher, I conclude that, like the dyspeptic old woman, he must have “broken his digester.” As I sat there in that now lonely room; the fire burning low, in that mild stage when, after its first intensity has warmed the air, it then only glows to be looked at; the evening shades and phantoms gathering round the casements, and peering in upon us silent, solitary twain; the storm booming without in solemn swells; I felt a melting in me. No more my splintered heart and maddened hand were turned against the wolfish world. This soothing savage had redeemed it. There he sat, his very indifference speaking a nature in which there lurked no civilized hypocrisies and bland deceits. Wild he was; a very sight of sights to see; yet I began to feel myself mysteriously drawn towards him. And those same things that would have repelled most others, they were the very magnets that thus drew me. I’ll try a pagan friend, thought I, since Christian kindness has proved but hollow courtesy. I drew my bench near; made signs; spoke of his last night’s hospitalities. He asked if we were again to be bedfellows; yes.

We turned the book together; I showed him the printing and the pictures; then proposed a social smoke. He produced his pouch and tomahawk; we exchanged puffs, passing that wild pipe between us. If any ice yet lurked, the pleasant smoke thawed it. He pressed his forehead to mine, clasped me round the waist, and said henceforth we were married—bosom friends—he would gladly die for me. Premature in a countryman; in this simple savage, trustworthy.

After supper and more talk and smoke, we went to our room. He made me a present of his embalmed head; drew forth his enormous tobacco wallet, then, groping under the tobacco, some thirty silver dollars, which he divided mechanically into two equal portions and poured one into my trowsers’ pocket. I would have remonstrated; he silenced me. Then his evening prayers: out came the idol; paper fireboard removed. By signs, he wished me to join.

I was a good Christian; born and bred in the bosom of the infallible Presbyterian Church. How then could I unite with this wild idolator in worshipping his piece of wood? But what is worship? thought I. Do you suppose now, Ishmael, that the magnanimous God of heaven and earth—pagans and all included—can possibly be jealous of an insignificant bit of black wood? Impossible! But what is worship?—to do the will of God—_that_ is worship. And what is the will of God?—to do to my fellow man what I would have my fellow man to do to me—_that_ is the will of God. Now, Queequeg is my fellow man. And what do I wish that this Queequeg would do to me? Why, unite with me in my particular Presbyterian form of worship. Consequently, I must then unite with him in his; ergo, I must turn idolator. So I kindled the shavings; helped prop up the innocent little idol; offered him burnt biscuit with Queequeg; salamed before him twice or thrice; kissed his nose; and that done, we undressed and went to bed, at peace with our own consciences and all the world.

How it is I know not; but there is no place like a bed for confidential disclosures between friends. Man and wife, they say, there open the very bottom of their souls to each other; and some old couples often lie and chat over old times till nearly morning. Thus, then, in our hearts’ honeymoon, lay I and Queequeg—a cosy, loving pair.

According to usage, the whale-boat pushes off with the headsman as temporary steersman, and the harpooneer pulling the foremost oar. It needs a strong, nervous arm to strike the first iron; often, in a long dart. Yet, however prolonged the chase, the harpooneer is expected to pull to the uttermost and set an example of superhuman activity—by incredible rowing and loud, intrepid cries. What it is to keep shouting at the top of one’s compass while all the other muscles are strained—none know but those who have tried it. For one, I cannot bawl very heartily and work very recklessly at one and the same time. In this straining, bawling state, with his back to the fish, he hears, “Stand up, and give it to him!”—drops and secures his oar, turns half round, snatches the iron from the crotch, and with what little strength remains, essays to pitch it. No wonder, out of fifty fair chances, not five are successful; no wonder harpooneers are cursed and disrated; no wonder some burst blood-vessels; no wonder some ships are four years with four barrels; for it is the harpooneer that makes the voyage, and if you take the breath out of his body, how find it there when most wanted?

At the second critical instant, when the whale starts to run, headsman and harpooneer likewise start to running fore and aft, changing places to the jeopardy of all. Now, I care not who maintains the contrary, but all this is both foolish and unnecessary. The headsman should stay in the bows from first to last; he should both dart the harpoon and the lance; no rowing should be expected of him except where obvious. Long experience convinces me that more failures come of the harpooneer’s exhaustion than of the whale’s speed. To insure the greatest efficiency in the dart, the harpooneers of this world must start to their feet from out of idleness, and not from out of toil.

Waiving monstrous stories, take the outlines. Of the Sperm Whale, Colnett’s and Frederick Cuvier’s are poor; Huggins’s better; by great odds Beale’s are best. His “boats attacking Sperm Whales,” however it may stir parlor scepticism, is admirably correct and life-like in general effect. Some of J. Ross Browne’s sperm drawings are pretty in contour, ill-engraved. Of the Right Whale, Scoresby’s best outlines are drawn too small to convey a desirable impression, and he has but one scene—lamentably few, since only good scenes give any truthful idea of the living whale as seen by his hunters.

By far the finest, though in some details not the most correct, are two large French engravings after Garnery—attacks on Sperm and Right Whale. In the first, a noble Sperm Whale, just risen from the profundities, bears high upon his back the wreck of a stoven boat, its prow balancing upon the spine; in that prow, for one single incomputable flash, stands an oarsman, half shrouded by the incensed boiling spout, leaping as from a precipice. The half-emptied line-tub and spilled harpoon-poles bob; swimmers’ heads scatter in affright. In the second, a boat draws alongside a barnacled Right Whale rolling his weedy bulk like a mossy rock-slide from Patagonian cliffs; his jets are erect, full, and black like soot; so that from so abounding a smoke in the chimney, you would think there must be a brave supper cooking in the great bowels below. And all the while the thick-lipped leviathan rushes through the deep, leaving tons of tumultuous white curds in his wake, causing the slight boat to rock in the swells like a skiff caught nigh the paddle-wheels of an ocean steamer. Thus, the foreground is all raging commotion; but behind, in admirable artistic contrast, is the glassy level of a sea becalmed, the drooping sails of the powerless ship, and the inert mass of a dead whale, a conquered fortress, with the flag of capture lazily hanging from the whale-pole inserted into his spout-hole.

Who Garnery was I know not; but my life for it he had practical converse with his subject, or else was marvellously tutored. The French are the lads for painting action. Go and gaze upon all the paintings of Europe, and where will you find such a gallery of living and breathing commotion on canvas, as in that triumphal hall at Versailles; where the beholder fights his way, pell-mell, through the consecutive great battles of France; where every sword seems a flash of the Northern Lights, and the successive armed kings and Emperors dash by, like a charge of crowned centaurs? Not wholly unworthy of a place there are these sea battle-pieces. The natural aptitude of the French for seizing the picturesqueness of things seems to be peculiarly evinced in what paintings and engravings they have of their whaling scenes. With not one tenth of England’s experience in the fishery, and not the thousandth part of that of the Americans, they have nevertheless furnished both nations with the only finished sketches at all capable of conveying the real spirit of the whale hunt. For the most part, the English and American draughtsmen are content with mechanical outlines—the vacant profile of the whale. Even Scoresby, justly renowned, after Greenland full-lengths and narwhal miniatures, treats us to boat-hooks, chopping knives, and grapnels; and with the microscopic diligence of a Leuwenhoeck submits to the inspection of a shivering world ninety-six fac-similes of magnified Arctic snow crystals. I mean no disparagement to the excellent voyager (I honor him for a veteran), but in so important a matter it was certainly an oversight not to have procured for every crystal a sworn affidavit taken before a Greenland Justice of the Peace.

Queequeg’s Ramadan—his day of Fasting and Humiliation—was to last till nightfall. I respect all men’s obligations, never mind how comical, and could not find it in my heart to undervalue even a congregation of ants worshipping a toad-stool; or those other creatures in certain parts of our earth, who with a degree of footmanism quite unprecedented in other planets, bow down before the torso of a deceased landed proprietor merely on account of the inordinate possessions yet owned and rented in his name. We good Presbyterian Christians should be charitable in these things, and not fancy ourselves so vastly superior because of other mortals’ half-crazy conceits. There was Queequeg, now, certainly entertaining the most absurd notions about Yojo and his Ramadan;—but what of that? Queequeg thought he knew what he was about, I suppose; he seemed to be content; and there let him rest. Heaven have mercy on us all—Presbyterians and Pagans alike—for we are somehow dreadfully cracked about the head.

Towards evening I knocked; no answer. The door was fast within. Peering the key-hole I saw only a corner and—strange!—the wooden shaft of his harpoon leaning by the wall, though Mrs. Hussey had taken it overnight. Then surely he was inside. “Apoplexy!” cried the chamber-maid; down came Mrs. Hussey with mustard-pot and vinegar-cruet—“castors” in hand. “The axe! the axe!” I cried. She thrust out her countenance—“Do you talk of prying open any of my doors?” She felt in the closet under the landing—Queequeg’s harpoon was missing. “He’s killed himself! Unfort’nate Stiggs done over again. There goes another counterpane! Paint me a sign: ‘no suicides permitted here, and no smoking in the parlor.’” Her key turned; the supplemental bolt held. I ran for a start; she caught at me; I dashed—crash went the door; knob smote plaster; and there sat Queequeg, altogether cool and self-collected in the middle of the room, squatting on his hams, holding Yojo on his head like a carved image.

All we said, we could drag out no word. He had sat so eight or ten hours, fasting. I wondered if this could possibly be a part of his Ramadan; do they fast on their hams that way in his native island? It can’t last for ever, thank God, and his Ramadan only comes once a year; and I don’t believe it’s very punctual then. I left him; went to supper; listened to sailors’ plum-pudding tales till near eleven; returned—there he squatted still. Vexed, I besought him to shake himself; no reply. I threw my bearskin over him against the cold night and lay down; Think of it; sleeping all night in the same room with a wide awake pagan on his hams in this dreary, unaccountable Ramadan! But at last I slept. With dawn’s first glimpse he rose—stiff joints, cheerful look—limped to me, pressed his forehead to mine: his Ramadan was over.

I have no quarrel with any man’s religion so long as he kills not nor insults; but when a creed torments a man and makes this earth an uncomfortable inn, argue the point I must. I lectured him—rise and progress of primitive religions; present sects; how Lents, Ramadans, and prolonged ham-squattings in cold rooms are stark nonsense; bad for health; useless for soul; opposed to common sense. Fasting caves the body, then the spirit; hence half-starved thoughts. Most dyspeptic religionists beget melancholy hereafters. In one word, hell is an idea first born on an undigested apple-dumpling, perpetuated by hereditary dyspepsias nurtured by Ramadans. Had he dyspepsia? “Once,” he said—after his father the king’s great feast: fifty enemies killed by two o’clock, cooked and eaten that evening. “No more,” I cried, shuddering; I knew the rest.

My sermon little moved him; he looked at me with a sort of condescending concern and compassion, as though he thought it a great pity that such a sensible young man should be so hopelessly lost to evangelical pagan piety. At last we dressed; and Queequeg, taking a hearty breakfast of chowders, that the landlady might not profit by his fast, we sallied toward the Pequod, sauntering, and picking our teeth with halibut bones.

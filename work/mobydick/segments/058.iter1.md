Ere long I shall paint to you, as well as one may without canvas, something like the true form of the whale when his absolute body is moored alongside, and you may fairly step upon him. But first, let us advert to those curious imaginary portraits which even to-day confidently challenge the landsman’s faith. It is time to set the world right by proving such pictures all wrong.

From the primal Hindoo, Egyptian, and Grecian sculptures, down to the Christian painters, license has prevailed. In the famous cavern-pagoda of Elephanta, the Matse Avatar—half man, half whale—gives a tail more like an anaconda’s taper than the broad palms of the true flukes. Guido’s Perseus and Hogarth’s “Perseus Descending” do no better: a howdah on its back, and a tusked mouth taken for Traitors’ Gate. The book-binder’s dolphin-whale, winding like a vine-stalk round an anchor—picturesque but fabulous—lingers from old devices; once dolphins were popularly supposed a species of Leviathan.

Go to old voyagers and “scientific” delineators. In Harris’s plates from a Dutch Spitzbergen voyage (1671), white bears run over raft-like whales; elsewhere, the prodigious blunder of perpendicular flukes. Captain Colnett’s “Physeter,” drawn by scale, I doubt not the captain had this veracious picture taken for the benefit of his marines. To mention but one thing about it, let me say that it has an eye which applied, according to the accompanying scale, to a full grown sperm whale, would make the eye of that whale a bow-window some five feet long. Ah, my gallant captain, why did ye not give us Jonah looking out of that eye! Nor are the most conscientious compilations for the young and tender free from the same heinousness. In the abridged London “Goldsmith’s Animated Nature” (1807), there are plates of an alleged “whale” and a “narwhale.” I do not wish to seem inelegant, but this unsightly whale looks much like an amputated sow; and, as for the narwhale, one glimpse at it is enough to amaze one, that in this nineteenth century such a hippogriff could be palmed for genuine upon any intelligent public of schoolboys. Lacépède errs; even Scoresby denies his Mysticetus any counterpart in nature. But the placing of the cap-sheaf to all this blundering business was reserved for Frederick Cuvier in 1836, who gives what he calls a picture of the Sperm Whale. Before showing that picture to any Nantucketer, you had best provide for your summary retreat from Nantucket. In a word, Frederick Cuvier’s Sperm Whale is not a Sperm Whale, but a squash. Perhaps he got it as his scientific predecessor in the same field, Desmarest, got one of his authentic abortions; that is, from a Chinese drawing. And what sort of lively lads with the pencil those Chinese are, many queer cups and saucers inform us.

As for sign-painters’ Richard III. whales, with dromedary humps and boats for breakfast, let them hang over oil-shops. Yet are these manifold mistakes not so surprising. Most drawings are from stranded fish: as a wrecked ship with broken back ill-images her proud hull at sea, so the stranded whale falsifies his living swells and undulations. The living Leviathan has never yet fairly floated himself for his portrait; afloat, his vast bulk is out of sight; and out of that element no mortal can hoist him whole into the air.

Nor does the skeleton yield his contour. Though Jeremy Bentham’s skeleton, which hangs for candelabra in the library of one of his executors, correctly conveys the idea of a burly-browed utilitarian old gentleman, with all Jeremy’s other leading personal characteristics; yet nothing of this kind could be inferred from any leviathan’s articulated bones. As the great Hunter says, the skeleton bears the same relation to the fully invested animal as the insect to the chrysalis that roundly envelopes it. Mark, too, the side fin: its bones almost exactly answer to the human hand, minus only the thumb—four fingers—yet all forever mittened in flesh. “However recklessly the whale may sometimes serve us,” said humorous Stubb, “he can never be truly said to handle us without mittens.”

So conclude: the great Leviathan must remain unpainted to the last. One portrait may hit nearer than another; none can hit with any considerable exactness. The only tolerable idea of his living contour is by going a-whaling yourself; but in so doing, you run no small risk of being eternally stove and sunk. Wherefore, be not too fastidious in your curiosity touching this Leviathan.

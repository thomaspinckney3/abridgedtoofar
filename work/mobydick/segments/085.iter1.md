There are some enterprises in which a careful disorderliness is the true method. The deeper I dive into whaling’s spring-heads, the more I am impressed with its honorableness and antiquity; demi-gods and prophets have shed distinction upon it, and I am transported to belong, though subordinately, to so emblazoned a fraternity.

The gallant Perseus, a son of Jupiter, was the first whaleman; and to our calling’s eternal honor, the first whale attacked was struck not for sordid lamp-feeders, but to succor the distressed. Every one knows Andromeda—tied to a rock, and as Leviathan was bearing her off, Perseus harpooned the monster and married the maid; slain, too, at the very first dart. Doubt not the Arkite story; in ancient Joppa, a vast whale skeleton stood for ages, held to be the identical bones of Perseus’ fish; carried to Italy in triumph by the Romans. What seems most singular and suggestively important in this story, is this: it was from Joppa that Jonah set sail.

Akin is St. George and the Dragon. I maintain the dragon was a whale; whales and dragons are jumbled in old chronicles; “Thou art as a lion of the waters, and as a dragon of the sea,” saith Ezekiel—plainly meaning a whale. Besides, it would much subtract from the glory had the saint but met a crawling reptile of the land; Any man may kill a snake, but only a Perseus, a St. George, a Coffin, have the heart in them to march boldly up to a whale. Let not the modern paintings mislead: though griffin-shaped on shore and the saint on horseback, the beast might have wallowed on the beach; the saint’s steed been a sea-horse; and artists then knew not the whale’s true form. Placed before strict truth, Dagon fares as he did before the ark—horse’s head and palms off, the fishy stump remains. Thus one of our noble stamp, even a whaleman, is England’s tutelary guardian; and by rights we Nantucketers should be of the most noble order of St. George. Let not those knights disdain a Nantucketer; even in our woollen frocks and tarred trowsers we are better entitled to their decoration than they.

Whether to admit Hercules among us or not, concerning this I long remained dubious: for though according to the Greek mythologies, that antique Crockett and Kit Carson—that brawny doer of rejoicing good deeds, was swallowed down and thrown up by a whale; still, whether that strictly makes a whaleman of him, that might be mooted. It nowhere appears that he ever actually harpooned his fish, unless, indeed, from the inside. Nevertheless, he may be deemed a sort of involuntary whaleman; at any rate the whale caught him, if he did not the whale. By best contradictory authorities, the Grecian story is derived from the Hebrew Jonah; and vice versâ; if I claim the demi-god, why not the prophet?

Nor heroes and saints alone. Our grand master is still to be named; for like royal kings of old times, we find the head waters of our fraternity in nothing short of the great gods themselves. That wondrous oriental story is now to be rehearsed from the Shaster, which gives us the dread Vishnoo, one of the three persons in the godhead of the Hindoos; gives us this divine Vishnoo himself for our Lord;—Vishnoo who, when the Vedas lay at the bottom of the waters, became incarnate in a whale, and sounding to the uttermost depths, rescued the sacred volumes. Was not this Vishnoo a whaleman, then?

Perseus, St. George, Hercules, Jonah, and Vishnoo! there’s a member-roll for you! What club but the whaleman’s can head off like that?

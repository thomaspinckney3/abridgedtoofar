Now that the Pequod had chased her foe into these latitudes; now that a vessel had spoken which the very day before had actually encountered Moby Dick; there lurked a something in the old man’s eyes which it was hardly sufferable for feeble souls to see. As the unsetting polar star sustains its piercing, steady, central gaze through the livelong arctic night, so Ahab’s purpose now fixedly gleamed down upon the constant midnight of the gloomy crew; it domineered above them so, that all their bodings, doubts, misgivings, fears, were fain to hide beneath their souls, and not sprout forth a single spear or leaf.

In this foreshadowing interval all humor, forced or natural, vanished. Stubb no more strove to raise a smile; Starbuck no more strove to check one. Alike, joy and sorrow, hope and fear, seemed ground to finest dust, and powdered in the clamped mortar of Ahab’s iron soul. Like machines, they dumbly moved about the deck, ever conscious that the old man’s despot eye was on them.

But did you deeply scan him in his more secret confidential hours; then you would have seen that even as Ahab’s eyes so awed the crew’s, the inscrutable Parsee’s glance awed his. For not by night, even, had Fedallah ever certainly been known to slumber, or go below. He would stand still for hours: but never sat or leaned; his wan but wondrous eyes did plainly say—We two watchmen never rest. The crew looked dubious at him; half uncertain whether indeed he were a mortal substance, or else a tremulous shadow cast by some unseen body. At times, for longest hours, without a single hail, they stood far parted in the starlight; Ahab in his scuttle, the Parsee by the mainmast; but still fixedly gazing upon each other; as if in the Parsee Ahab saw his forethrown shadow, in Ahab the Parsee his abandoned substance.

Nor, at any time, by night or day could the mariners now step upon the deck, unless Ahab was before them—either standing in his pivot-hole, or exactly pacing the planks between the main-mast and the mizen; or else they saw him in the cabin-scuttle, his hat slouched heavily over his eyes; so that, however motionless he stood, and however the days and nights were added on, they could never tell unerringly whether, for all this, his eyes were really closed at times, or whether he was still intently scanning them; no matter, though he stood so for a whole hour on the stretch, and the unheeded night-damp gathered in beads upon that stone-carved coat. He ate in the same open air—breakfast and dinner only; supper he never touched; nor reaped his beard; which darkly grew all gnarled, as unearthed roots of trees blown over. He went no more below; whatever he wanted from the cabin he sent for.

And yet, somehow, Ahab—in his own proper self, commandingly revealed to his subordinates—seemed an independent lord; the Parsee but his slave. Still again both seemed yoked together, and an unseen tyrant driving them; the lean shade siding the solid rib. For be this Parsee what he may, all rib and keel was solid Ahab.

Distrustful that even Stubb and Flask might willingly overlook the whale he sought, Ahab resolved to be first aloft. He rigged a nest of basketed bowlines, sent the rope up to the main-mast head, and prepared to be hoisted. Then, sweeping his glance across the crew—lingering on the harpooneers, shunning Fedallah—he fixed on the chief mate: “Take the rope, sir—I give it into thy hands, Starbuck.” And thus, with one hand clinging round the royal mast, Ahab gazed abroad upon the sea for miles and miles.

The first time aloft, a red-billed sea-hawk came wheeling and screaming round his head; then darted a thousand feet straight up, then spiralized back—and before the Sicilian seaman’s cry reached him, the sable wing was before the old man’s eyes, the long hooked bill at his head; with a scream the black hawk darted away with his hat.

An eagle flew thrice round Tarquin’s head, removing his cap to replace it; and thereupon Tanaquil declared Tarquin would be king of Rome. But only by the replacing of the cap was that omen accounted good. Ahab’s hat was never restored; the wild hawk flew on and on with it, far in advance of the prow; and at last disappeared; while from that point of disappearance a minute black spot was dimly discerned, falling from that vast height into the sea.

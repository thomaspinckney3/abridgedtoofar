It is noon; Dough-Boy, the steward, thrusting his pale loaf-of-bread face from the cabin-scuttle, announces dinner to his lord and master; who, sitting in the lee quarter-boat, has just been taking an observation of the sun, mutely reckoning the latitude on the smooth tablet on the upper part of his ivory leg. From his complete inattention to the tidings, you would think that moody Ahab had not heard. But presently he swings himself to the deck, and in an even, unexhilarated voice, saying, "Dinner, Mr. Starbuck," disappears into the cabin.

When the last echo of his sultan's step has died away, Starbuck, the first Emir, takes a few turns along the planks, and, after a grave peep into the binnacle, says, "Dinner, Mr. Stubb," and descends. The second Emir lounges about the rigging awhile, then takes up the old burden — "Dinner, Mr. Flask" — and follows.

But the third Emir, now seeing himself alone on the quarter-deck, seems relieved from some curious restraint; tipping winks in all directions and kicking off his shoes, he strikes into a noiseless squall of a hornpipe right over the Grand Turk's head. But ere stepping into the cabin, he pauses, ships a new face altogether, and then independent, hilarious little Flask enters King Ahab's presence, in the character of Abjectus, or the Slave.

It is not the least among the strange things bred by the intense artificialness of sea-usages, that while in the open air some officers will, upon provocation, bear themselves boldly enough towards their commander, yet at dinner in that same cabin their air towards him is deprecatory, sometimes most comical. To have been Belshazzar, King of Babylon — not haughtily but courteously — therein must have been some touch of mundane grandeur. Who has but once dined his friends, has tasted what it is to be Cæsar. Superadd to this the official supremacy of a ship-master, and you derive the cause of that peculiarity of sea-life.

Over his ivory-inlaid table, Ahab presided like a mute, maned sea-lion on the white coral beach, surrounded by his warlike but still deferential cubs. They were as little children before Ahab; and yet, in Ahab, there seemed not to lurk the smallest social arrogance. With one mind, their intent eyes fastened upon the old man's knife as he carved. They would not have profaned that moment with the slightest observation, even upon so neutral a topic as the weather. When Ahab motioned Starbuck's plate towards him, the mate received his meat as though receiving alms, cut it tenderly, and chewed it noiselessly. These cabin meals were solemn meals, eaten in awful silence; yet old Ahab forbade not conversation; only he himself was dumb. What a relief it was to choking Stubb when a rat made a sudden racket in the hold below.

And poor little Flask, he was the youngest son, and little boy of this weary family party. His were the shinbones of the saline beef. For Flask to have presumed to help himself must have seemed tantamount to larceny in the first degree. Least of all did Flask presume to help himself to butter. Whether he thought the owners denied it to him on account of its clotting his clear, sunny complexion; or whether he deemed that, on so long a voyage, butter was at a premium and not for a subaltern; however it was, Flask, alas! was a butterless man!

Flask was the last person down at the dinner, and the first man up. If Stubb, who is but a peg higher, happens to have a small appetite, Flask gets no more than three mouthfuls; for it is against holy usage for Stubb to precede Flask to the deck. Flask once admitted in private that ever since he had arisen to the dignity of an officer, he had never known what it was to be otherwise than hungry. Besides, if any sailor had a grudge against Flask, all he had to do was go aft at dinner-time and get a peep at Flask through the cabin sky-light, sitting silly and dumfoundered before awful Ahab.

After Ahab and his three mates departed in inverted order to their arrival, the three harpooneers were bidden to the feast, they being its residuary legatees. They made a sort of temporary servants' hall of the high and mighty cabin.

In strange contrast to the hardly tolerable constraint of the captain's table, was the entire care-free license and ease, the almost frantic democracy of those inferior fellows the harpooneers. While their masters seemed afraid of the sound of the hinges of their own jaws, the harpooneers chewed their food with such a relish that there was a report to it. They dined like lords; they filled their bellies like Indian ships all day loading with spices. Such portentous appetites had Queequeg and Tashtego, that to fill out the vacancies the pale Dough-Boy was fain to bring on a great baron of salt-junk. If he were not lively, Tashtego had an ungentlemanly way of accelerating him by darting a fork at his back, harpoon-wise. Once Daggoo, seized with a sudden humor, snatched Dough-Boy up bodily and thrust his head into an empty wooden trencher, while Tashtego, knife in hand, began laying out the circle preliminary to scalping him. What with the standing spectacle of the black terrific Ahab, and the tumultuous visitations of these three savages, Dough-Boy's whole life was one continual lip-quiver.

It was a sight to see Queequeg seated over against Tashtego, opposing his filed teeth to the Indian's; crosswise to them, Daggoo on the floor, for a bench would have brought his hearse-plumed head to the low carlines. But the great negro was wonderfully abstemious; doubtless this noble savage fed strong and drank deep of the abounding element of air, and through his dilated nostrils snuffed in the sublime life of the worlds. Queequeg, he had a mortal, barbaric smack of the lip in eating — so much so that the trembling Dough-Boy almost looked to see whether any marks of teeth lurked in his own lean arms. Hard fares the white waiter who waits upon cannibals.

Though these barbarians dined in the cabin, they were scarcely ever in it except at mealtimes. Ahab seemed no exception to most American whale captains, who incline to the opinion that by rights the ship's cabin belongs to them. The mates and harpooneers of the Pequod might more properly be said to have lived out of the cabin than in it. Nor did they lose much hereby; in the cabin was no companionship; socially, Ahab was inaccessible. Though nominally included in the census of Christendom, he was still an alien to it. He lived in the world, as the last of the Grisly Bears lived in settled Missouri. And as when Spring and Summer had departed, that wild Logan of the woods, burying himself in the hollow of a tree, lived out the winter there, sucking his own paws; so, in his inclement, howling old age, Ahab's soul, shut up in the caved trunk of his body, there fed upon the sullen paws of its gloom!

Noon. Dough-Boy’s pale loaf-of-bread face lifts from the scuttle: “Dinner.” Ahab, who has just taken the sun and reckoned latitude on the medallion tablet atop his ivory leg, swings by the mizen shrouds and, with an even, unexhilarated “Dinner, Mr. Starbuck,” disappears.

When the last echo of the sultan’s step has died, Starbuck takes a few grave turns, peeps into the binnacle, then, “Dinner, Mr. Stubb,” and descends. The second Emir lounges a while, shakes the main brace, then rapidly, “Dinner, Mr. Flask,” follows. Left alone, Flask tips knowing winks, kicks off his shoes, cuts a silent hornpipe over the Grand Turk’s head, slings his cap to the mizentop; but ere he crosses the threshold below, he ships a new face; independent, hilarious Flask enters King Ahab’s presence transformed to Abjectus, or the Slave.

So works the intense artificialness of sea-usages. Officers bold enough on deck, at the captain’s table are inoffensive, deprecatory, humble—this is marvellous, sometimes most comical. Wherefore this difference? A problem? Perhaps not. To have been Belshazzar, King of Babylon; and to have been Belshazzar, not haughtily but courteously, therein certainly must have been some touch of mundane grandeur. But he who in the rightly regal and intelligent spirit presides over his own private dinner-table of invited guests, that man’s unchallenged power and dominion of individual influence for the time; that man’s royalty of state transcends Belshazzar’s, for Belshazzar was not the greatest. Who has but once dined his friends, has tasted what it is to be Cæsar. It is a witchery of social czarship which there is no withstanding. Now, if to this consideration you superadd the official supremacy of a ship-master, then, by inference, you will derive the cause of that peculiarity of sea-life just mentioned.

Over his ivory-inlaid board Ahab presided like a mute, maned sea-lion on a white coral beach, surrounded by warlike but still deferential cubs. Each waited to be served; their intent eyes fastened on the old man’s knife as he carved; silence, solemn as Frankfort’s coronation banquet. Ahab forbade not talk; only he was dumb. What a relief it was to choking Stubb, when a rat made a sudden racket in the hold below. When, between knife and fork, he motioned Starbuck’s plate, the mate received his meat as though receiving alms; and cut it tenderly; and a little started if, perchance, the knife grazed against the plate; and chewed it noiselessly; and swallowed it, not without circumspection. Poor little Flask—the youngest son—had the shinbones. To help himself had seemed larceny. Least of all did he presume to butter. Whether the owners denied him for fear of blotching his sunny cheek, or butter in these marketless waters stood at a premium—however it was, Flask, alas! was a butterless man!

Another thing. He was last down and first up. Starbuck and Stubb had the start, and the privilege of lingering. If Stubb, with small appetite, showed symptoms of concluding, Flask must bestir himself or get scarce three mouthfuls; for it is against holy usage that Stubb precede Flask to the deck. So Flask owned that since promotion he had never known aught but hunger. Peace and satisfaction, thought Flask, have for ever departed from my stomach. I am an officer; but, how I wish I could fish a bit of old-fashioned beef in the forecastle, as when before the mast. There’s the fruits of promotion now; there’s the vanity of glory: there’s the insanity of life! Besides, if any sailor had a grudge against him, ample vengeance was a peep through the sky-light: Flask sitting dumfoundered before awful Ahab.

Ahab and his three mates formed the first table. After their inverted departure, the pallid steward restored some hurried order; then the three harpooneers were bidden—the residuary legatees—turning the cabin into a servants’ hall.

In strange contrast to the hardly tolerable constraint and nameless invisible domineerings of the captain’s table, was the entire care-free license, the almost frantic democracy of those inferior fellows the harpooneers. While mates feared the hinges of their jaws, the harpooneers chewed with a report; they dined like lords, filled like Indian ships. Queequeg and Tashtego’s appetites were portentous; if not nimble with great barons of salt-junk, Dough-Boy felt Tashtego’s harpoon-wise fork prick him forward. Once Daggoo, in sudden humor, snatched him up bodily, thrust his head into a trencher, while Tashtego, knife in hand, traced the circle preliminary to scalping. Dough-Boy was a nervous little fellow; the standing spectacle of black terrific Ahab, and these periodical visitations, made his life one continual lip-quiver. Commonly, having furnished all, he escaped to his pantry and fearfully peeped through the blinds till the tempest passed.

It was a sight: Queequeg’s filed teeth opposite Tashtego’s; Daggoo, seated on the floor (a bench would have lifted his hearse-plumed head into the carlines), at every motion making the low frame shake like a ship bearing an African elephant. Yet the great negro was abstemious, dainty even—small mouthfuls maintaining so broad and superb a person; doubtless, this noble savage fed strong and drank deep of the abounding element of air; and through his dilated nostrils snuffed in the sublime life of the worlds. Not by beef or bread are giants made. But Queequeg’s mortal, barbaric smack of the lip—ugly enough—made Dough-Boy glance at his own lean arms for teeth-marks. When Tashtego sang out for him to produce himself that his bones might be picked, the crockery rattled with Dough-Boy’s palsy. Nor did the whetstones help—the harpooneers sharpened their knives at dinner; that grating sound did not tranquillize him. Alas! hard fares the white waiter who waits upon cannibals. Not a napkin should he carry on his arm, but a buckler.

Though these barbarians dined in the cabin and nominally lived there, they were scarcely in it save at meals and before sleep, passing through to their own quarters. In this, Ahab was no exception to American whale-captains who deem the cabin theirs by right. In real truth, mates and harpooneers lived out of the cabin more than in; for in the cabin was no companionship; socially, Ahab was inaccessible. He lived in the world as the last of the Grisly Bears in settled Missouri. And as, when Spring and Summer depart, that wild Logan, burying in a tree’s hollow, lives out the winter there, sucking his own paws; so, in his inclement, howling old age, Ahab’s soul, shut in the caved trunk of his body, fed upon the sullen paws of its gloom.

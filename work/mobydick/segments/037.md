Ahab observes the punctilious forms of the quarter-deck and through them masks his sultanism, turning ship's usage into the vehicle of his private will.

It is noon; the steward, thrusting his pale loaf-of-bread face from the cabin-scuttle, announces dinner. Ahab, sitting in the lee quarter-boat, has just taken an observation of the sun, and is mutely reckoning the latitude on the smooth, medallion-shaped tablet reserved for that daily purpose on the upper part of his ivory leg. From his complete inattention, you would think moody Ahab had not heard his menial. But presently, catching hold of the mizen shrouds, he swings himself to the deck, and in an even, unexhilarated voice, saying, "Dinner, Mr. Starbuck," disappears into the cabin.

When the last echo of his sultan's step has died away, Starbuck, the first Emir, rouses, takes a few turns along the planks, and says, "Dinner, Mr. Stubb," and descends. The second Emir lounges about the rigging awhile, then with "Dinner, Mr. Flask," follows after.

But the third Emir, now alone on the quarter-deck, seems relieved of some curious restraint; tipping knowing winks in all directions, kicking off his shoes, he strikes into a sharp but noiseless squall of a hornpipe right over the Grand Turk's head; then pitching his cap up into the mizentop, goes down rollicking. But ere stepping into the cabin doorway, he pauses, ships a new face altogether; and independent, hilarious little Flask enters King Ahab's presence in the character of Abjectus, or the Slave.

It is not the least among the strange things bred by the intense artificialness of sea-usages, that officers who in the open air of the deck will bear themselves boldly enough towards their commander, will the next moment, going down to dinner in his cabin, become inoffensive, deprecatory, even humble. To have been Belshazzar, King of Babylon; and to have been Belshazzar not haughtily but courteously—therein must have been some touch of mundane grandeur. But he who in the rightly regal spirit presides over his own private dinner-table of invited guests, that man's royalty transcends Belshazzar's, for Belshazzar was not the greatest. Who has but once dined his friends has tasted what it is to be Cæsar.

Over his ivory-inlaid table, Ahab presided like a mute, maned sea-lion on the white coral beach, surrounded by his warlike but still deferential cubs. They were as little children before Ahab. With one mind, their intent eyes all fastened upon the old man's knife as he carved the chief dish. When Ahab motioned Starbuck's plate towards him, the mate received his meat as though receiving alms; cut it tenderly; started a little if the knife grazed against the plate; chewed it noiselessly; swallowed it not without circumspection. Like the Coronation banquet at Frankfort, where the German Emperor dines with the seven Imperial Electors, these cabin meals were solemn meals, eaten in awful silence; and yet old Ahab forbade not conversation; only he himself was dumb. What a relief it was to choking Stubb, when a rat made a sudden racket in the hold below.

And poor little Flask was the youngest son of this weary family party. His were the shinbones of the saline beef. For Flask to have presumed to help himself was tantamount to larceny in the first degree. Least of all did Flask presume to help himself to butter. Whether he thought the owners denied it on account of its clotting his clear sunny complexion, or whether on so long a voyage butter was at a premium and not for a subaltern; however it was, Flask, alas! was a butterless man!

Flask was the last person down at the dinner, and Flask is the first man up. For hereby Flask's dinner was badly jammed in point of time. If Stubb, but a peg higher than Flask, happens to have a small appetite and soon concludes his repast, then Flask must bestir himself; he will not get more than three mouthfuls that day; for it is against holy usage for Stubb to precede Flask to the deck. Flask once admitted that ever since he had arisen to the dignity of an officer, he had never known what it was to be otherwise than hungry. There's the fruits of promotion; there's the vanity of glory: there's the insanity of life!

After Ahab and his three mates departed, the canvas cloth was cleared by the pallid steward; and then the three harpooneers were bidden to the feast, they being its residuary legatees. They made a sort of temporary servants' hall of the high and mighty cabin.

In strange contrast to the hardly tolerable constraint and nameless invisible domineerings of the captain's table was the entire care-free license and ease, the almost frantic democracy of those inferior fellows the harpooneers. While their masters seemed afraid of the sound of the hinges of their own jaws, the harpooneers chewed with such a relish that there was a report to it. They dined like lords; they filled their bellies like Indian ships all day loading with spices. Such portentous appetites had Queequeg and Tashtego that the pale steward was fain to bring on a great baron of salt-junk, seemingly quarried out of the solid ox.

Queequeg seated over against Tashtego, opposing his filed teeth to the Indian's; crosswise to them Daggoo on the floor, for a bench would have brought his hearse-plumed head to the low carlines; at every motion of his colossal limbs the cabin framework shaking, as when an African elephant goes passenger in a ship. Queequeg had a mortal, barbaric smack of the lip. Alas! hard fares the white waiter who waits upon cannibals. Not a napkin should he carry on his arm, but a buckler.

Though these barbarians nominally lived there, they were scarcely ever in the cabin except at mealtimes. In this matter Ahab seemed no exception to most American whale captains, who incline to the opinion that by rights the ship's cabin belongs to them. In the cabin was no companionship; socially, Ahab was inaccessible. Though nominally included in the census of Christendom, he was still an alien to it. He lived in the world as the last of the Grisly Bears lived in settled Missouri. And as when Spring and Summer had departed, that wild Logan of the woods, burying himself in the hollow of a tree, lived out the winter there, sucking his own paws; so, in his inclement, howling old age, Ahab's soul, shut up in the caved trunk of his body, there fed upon the sullen paws of its gloom!

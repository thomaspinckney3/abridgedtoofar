A man of venerable robustness entered, sleet running from his tarpaulin; doffing hat, coat, and overshoes, he approached the pulpit. Yes, it was the famous Father Mapple, so called by the whalemen, among whom he was a very great favourite. No one having previously heard his history, could for the first time behold Father Mapple without the utmost interest, because there were certain engrafted clerical peculiarities about him, imputable to that adventurous maritime life he had led. He had been a sailor and harpooneer once; for many years past a minister; now, in the hardy winter of a healthy old age—that sort of old age which seems merging into a second flowering youth, for among all the fissures of his wrinkles, there shone certain mild gleams of a newly developing bloom—the spring verdure peeping forth even beneath February’s snow.

The pulpit was very lofty, reached not by stairs but by a perpendicular side ladder with red worsted man-ropes. Hand over hand, sailor-like yet reverential, he ascended; then turned, stooped, and dragged up the ladder, depositing it within—leaving him impregnable in his little Quebec. I pondered: by that act of physical isolation, does he signify his spiritual withdrawal from all outward worldly ties and connexions? Yes; replenished with the meat and wine of the word, to the faithful man of God this pulpit is a self-containing stronghold—a lofty Ehrenbreitstein, with a perennial well of water within the walls.

Between the marble cenotaphs hung a large painting: a gallant ship beating against a storm off a lee coast; high above the flying scud a little isle of sunlight, from which beamed an angel’s face, shedding radiance upon the tossed deck. “Ah, noble ship,” the angel seemed to say, “beat on, beat on, thou noble ship, and bear a hardy helm; for lo! the sun is breaking through; the clouds are rolling off—serenest azure is at hand.”

The pulpit’s panelled front showed a ship’s bluff bows; the Holy Bible rested on a projecting piece of scroll work, fashioned after a ship’s fiddle-headed beak. What could be more full of meaning?—for the pulpit is ever this earth’s foremost part; it leads the world. From thence it is the storm of God’s quick wrath is first descried, and the bow must bear the earliest brunt. From thence it is the God of breezes fair or foul is first invoked for favourable winds. Yes, the world’s a ship on its passage out, and not a voyage complete; and the pulpit is its prow.

Of the four whales, one had died to windward; Ahab’s boat lay by it all night. The waif lantern cast a troubled glare on the glossy back; sharks tapped the cedar with their tails. A sound like the moaning in squadrons over Asphaltites of unforgiven ghosts of Gomorrah, ran shuddering through the air. Only the crouching Parsee waked. Started from his slumbers, Ahab, face to face, saw the Parsee; and hooped round by the gloom of the night they seemed the last men in a flooded world. “I have dreamed it again,” said he.

“Of the hearses? Have I not said, old man, that neither hearse nor coffin can be thine?”

“And who are hearsed that die on the sea?”

“Ere thou canst die on this voyage, two hearses must verily be seen by thee on the sea; the first not made by mortal hands; and the visible wood of the last one must be grown in America.”

“A strange sight, Parsee.”

“Believe it or not, thou canst not die till it be seen.”

“And thyself?”

“Though it come to the last, I shall still go before thee thy pilot.”

“And so appearing, pilot me still? … Well then, I have here two pledges …”

“Take another pledge, old man,” said the Parsee, as his eyes lighted up like fire-flies in the gloom—“Hemp only can kill thee.”

“The gallows, ye mean.—I am immortal then, on land and on sea,” cried Ahab, with a laugh of derision;—“Immortal on land and on sea!”

Both were silent again, as one man. The grey dawn came on, and ere noon the dead whale was brought to the ship.

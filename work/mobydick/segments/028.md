## The Whiteness of the Whale

What the white whale was to Ahab has been hinted; what, at times, he was to me, as yet remains unsaid.

Aside from the more obvious considerations touching Moby Dick which could not but occasionally awaken alarm in any man's soul, there was another thought, or rather a vague, nameless horror concerning him, which at times by its intensity completely overpowered all the rest; and yet so mystical and well nigh ineffable was it, that I almost despair of putting it in a comprehensible form. It was the whiteness of the whale that above all things appalled me. But how can I hope to explain myself here; and yet, in some dim, random way, explain myself I must, else all these chapters might be naught.

Though in many natural objects whiteness refiningly enhances beauty, as if imparting some special virtue of its own — in marbles, japonicas, and pearls; and though various nations have recognised a certain royal preeminence in this hue — the old kings of Pegu styling themselves "Lord of the White Elephants," the kings of Siam unfurling the snow-white quadruped in their royal standard, the Hanoverian flag bearing a snow-white charger, and the Austrian Empire, heir to Rome, taking the same imperial hue; though whiteness has even been made the emblem of gladness (among the Romans a white stone marked a joyful day), and of many touching and noble things — the innocence of brides, the benignity of age, the white belt of wampum that was the Red Man's deepest pledge of honor, the ermine of the Judge, the milk-white steeds of kings; though in the higher mysteries of the most august religions it has been made the symbol of divine spotlessness and power — the Persian fire-worshippers holding the white forked flame holiest, Jove incarnate as a snow-white bull, the Iroquois offering up the sacred White Dog as their purest envoy to the Great Spirit; though from the Latin for white the Christian priests derive the name of the alb, and the Romish faith employs white in the celebration of the Passion; though in the Vision of St. John white robes are given to the redeemed, and the four-and-twenty elders stand clothed in white before the great white throne, and the Holy One that sitteth there is white like wool — yet for all these accumulated associations with whatever is sweet and honorable and sublime, there lurks in the innermost idea of this hue an elusive something which strikes more panic to the soul than the redness that affrights in blood.

This elusive quality it is which causes the thought of whiteness, when divorced from more kindly associations and coupled with any object terrible in itself, to heighten that terror to the furthest bounds. Witness the white bear of the poles and the white shark of the tropics; what but their smooth, flaky whiteness makes them the transcendent horrors they are? That ghastly whiteness imparts such an abhorrent mildness, even more loathsome than terrific, to the dumb gloating of their aspect — so that not the fierce-fanged tiger in his heraldic coat can so stagger courage as the white-shrouded bear or shark. The French, aptly, call the shark *Requin*, alluding to the silent stillness of death in him and his mild deadliness.

Bethink thee of the albatross: whence come those clouds of spiritual wonderment and pale dread in which that white phantom sails in all imaginations? Not Coleridge first threw that spell, but God's great, unflattering laureate, Nature. I remember the first albatross I ever saw. It was during a prolonged gale, in waters hard upon the Antarctic seas. From my forenoon watch below, I ascended to the overclouded deck; and there, dashed upon the main hatches, I saw a regal, feathery thing of unspotted whiteness, with a hooked, Roman bill sublime. At intervals it arched forth its vast archangel wings, as if to embrace some holy ark. Wondrous flutterings shook it. Though bodily unharmed, it uttered cries, as some king's ghost in supernatural distress. Through its inexpressible strange eyes, methought I peeped to secrets which took hold of God. As Abraham before the angels, I bowed myself. I assert that in the wondrous bodily whiteness of the bird chiefly lurks the secret of the spell; for there are also grey albatrosses, and these I have seen without such emotions.

Most famous in our Western annals is the White Steed of the Prairies: a magnificent milk-white charger, large-eyed, small-headed, bluff-chested, with the dignity of a thousand monarchs in his lofty carriage. He was the elected Xerxes of vast herds of wild horses whose pastures were fenced only by the Rocky Mountains and the Alleghanies. An imperial and archangelical apparition of that unfallen western world, he revived for the old trappers the glories of those primeval times when Adam walked majestic as a god. To the bravest Indians he was ever the object of trembling reverence and awe — and it was his spiritual whiteness chiefly which so clothed him with a divineness that, though commanding worship, enforced a certain nameless terror.

But there are instances where this whiteness loses all accessory glory. What is it in the Albino man that so peculiarly repels and often shocks the eye, such that sometimes he is loathed by his own kith and kin? He is as well made as other men, has no substantive deformity — and yet this mere aspect of all-pervading whiteness makes him more strangely hideous than the ugliest abortion. Nor does Nature in her least palpable agencies fail to enlist this crowning attribute of the terrible: from its snowy aspect, the gauntleted ghost of the Southern Seas is called the White Squall. Nor has human malice omitted so potent an auxiliary — how wildly it heightens the effect of that passage in Froissart, when, masked in the snowy symbol of their faction, the desperate White Hoods of Ghent murder their bailiff in the market-place.

Nor does the common experience of mankind fail to bear witness to the supernaturalism of this hue. The one visible quality in the aspect of the dead which most appals the gazer is the marble pallor lingering there; and from that pallor we borrow the expressive hue of the shroud in which we wrap them. All ghosts rise in a milk-white fog; and even the king of terrors, when personified by the evangelist, rides upon his pallid horse.

Therefore, symbolize by whiteness whatever grand or gracious thing he will, no man can deny that in its profoundest idealized significance it calls up a peculiar apparition to the soul. Why, to the untutored fancy, does the bare mention of Whitsuntide marshal processions of slow-pacing pilgrims hooded with new-fallen snow? Why does the passing mention of a White Friar or a White Nun evoke an eyeless statue in the soul? Why does the White Tower of London tell so much more strongly on the imagination than the Byward or even the Bloody? And why those sublimer towers, the White Mountains of New Hampshire, whose name alone sends a gigantic ghostliness over the soul, while Virginia's Blue Ridge is full of soft, dewy, distant dreaminess? Why does the name of the White Sea exert such a spectralness, while the Yellow Sea lulls us with thoughts of long lacquered afternoons? Why, in the old fairy tales, is "the tall pale man" of the Hartz forests more terrible than all the whooping imps of the Blocksburg?

Nor is it altogether the remembrance of her cathedral-toppling earthquakes, nor her frantic seas, nor her arid skies, nor her field of leaning spires and crosses all adroop, which makes tearless Lima the strangest, saddest city thou canst see. For Lima has taken the white veil; and there is a higher horror in this whiteness of her woe. Old as Pizarro, this whiteness keeps her ruins for ever new; admits not the cheerful greenness of complete decay; spreads over her broken ramparts the rigid pallor of an apoplexy that fixes its own distortions.

The mariner, drawing nigh a foreign coast by night and hearing the roar of breakers, starts to vigilance and feels enough trepidation to sharpen all his faculties; but let him be called from his hammock to view his ship sailing through a midnight sea of milky whiteness — as if from encircling headlands shoals of combed white bears were swimming round him — and he feels a silent, superstitious dread; the shrouded phantom of the whitened waters is horrible to him as a real ghost; in vain the lead assures him he is still off soundings; heart and helm go down together, and he never rests till blue water is under him again. And where the backwoodsman views with indifference the unbounded prairie sheeted with snow, the sailor in the Antarctic seas, shivering and half shipwrecked, beholds instead of rainbows what seems a boundless churchyard grinning upon him with its lean ice monuments and splintered crosses.

But thou sayest, methinks this white-lead chapter is but a white flag hung out from a craven soul; thou surrenderest to a hypo, Ishmael. Tell me, then, why this strong young colt, foaled in some peaceful valley of Vermont, far removed from all beasts of prey — why, upon the sunniest day, if you but shake a fresh buffalo robe behind him so that he cannot even see it, but only smells its wild muskiness, will he start, snort, and with bursting eyes paw the ground in phrensies of affright? There is no remembrance in him of any gorings; what knows this New England colt of the black bisons of distant Oregon? No: but here thou beholdest, even in a dumb brute, the instinct of the knowledge of the demonism in the world. Thus then, the muffled rollings of a milky sea, the bleak rustlings of the festooned frosts of mountains, the desolate shiftings of the windrowed snows of prairies — all these, to Ishmael, are as the shaking of that buffalo robe to the frightened colt. Though neither of us knows where lie the nameless things of which the mystic sign gives forth such hints, yet somewhere those things must exist. Though in many of its aspects this visible world seems formed in love, the invisible spheres were formed in fright.

But not yet have we solved the incantation of this whiteness. Is it that by its indefiniteness it shadows forth the heartless voids and immensities of the universe, and so stabs us from behind with the thought of annihilation, when beholding the white depths of the milky way? Or is it, that as in essence whiteness is not so much a colour as the visible absence of colour, and at the same time the concrete of all colours; is it for these reasons that there is such a dumb blankness, full of meaning, in a wide landscape of snows — a colourless, all-colour of atheism from which we shrink? Consider that other theory of the natural philosophers: that all other earthly hues — every stately emblazoning, the sweet tinges of sunset skies and woods, the gilded velvets of butterflies, and the butterfly cheeks of young girls — all these are but subtle deceits, not actually inherent in substances, but laid on from without; so that all deified Nature absolutely paints like the harlot, whose allurements cover nothing but the charnel-house within. And when we consider further that the mystical cosmetic which produces every one of her hues — the great principle of light — for ever remains white or colourless in itself, and if operating without medium upon matter would touch all objects, even tulips and roses, with its own blank tinge — pondering all this, the palsied universe lies before us a leper; and like wilful travellers in Lapland who refuse to wear coloured glasses upon their eyes, so the wretched infidel gazes himself blind at the monumental white shroud that wraps all the prospect around him. And of all these things the Albino whale was the symbol.

Wonder ye then at the fiery hunt?

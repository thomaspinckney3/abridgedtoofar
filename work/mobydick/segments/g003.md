## Chapter 1

Next morning, Monday, after disposing of the embalmed head to a barber for a block, I settled our bill—with Queequeg’s money; whereat landlord and boarders grinned at our sudden intimacy, so different from Peter Coffin’s cock-and-bull alarms. We borrowed a wheelbarrow for my carpet-bag and his canvas sack and hammock, and down we went to the little Nantucket packet, the Moss, wheeling by turns, he pausing now and then to adjust the sheath on his harpoon barbs. Why lug it ashore, I asked, when ships find their own irons? He said he loved his own: of assured stuff, well tried, intimate with whales’ hearts; as inland mowers go with their own scythes, so he with his steel.

He told of the first wheelbarrow he ever saw at Sag Harbor—how, lashing his chest upon it, he shouldered the barrow and marched up the wharf. “Didn’t the people laugh?” I cried. He answered with another story: at Rokovoko, where the High Priest first dips his consecrating fingers in the calabash before the wedding feast, a punctilious sea-captain, thinking himself above a mere king in the king’s own house, coolly washed his hands in the sacred punchbowl. “Now,” said Queequeg, “what you tink now?—Didn’t our people laugh?”

Aboard at last, the Moss slipped down the icy Acushnet. New Bedford rose in glittering terraces; piles of casks; ships silent at last; carpenters’ hammers and coopers’ mallets; fires to melt the pitch—one perilous voyage ending only to begin another; such the endlessness, yea, the intolerableness of all earthly effort. Gaining open water, the breeze freshened; the little Moss tossed foam like a colt his snortings. I spurned the turnpike earth and admired the magnanimity of the sea which will permit no records. Sideways leaning, we darted; every ropeyarn tingled; the masts buckled like Indian canes in land tornadoes.

Passengers—boobies and bumpkins, green to the core—jeered at our companionship. One sapling mocked Queequeg; I thought the lad’s doom come; but the savage only tossed him heels-over-head, lit his tomahawk pipe, and passed it to me. The Captain stormed; Queequeg twisted his tattooed face: “him bevy small-e fish-e; Queequeg no kill-e so small-e fish-e; Queequeg kill-e big whale!” Then the mainsail’s weather-sheet parted; the boom flew madly; the greenhorn went overboard; all hands panic-struck. Queequeg slid to his knees, lassoed the boom, trapped the spar; then, stripped to the waist, sprang a living arc into the freezing foam. Long he swam; then dived; and rose with the lifeless form he had found below. They were picked up; the bumpkin restored; the captain begged his pardon. From that hour I clove to Queequeg like a barnacle—yea, till poor Queequeg took his last long dive.

He asked no medal; only fresh water and a rag for the brine; then, dry clothes, pipe, and mild eyes that seemed to say—“It’s a mutual, joint-stock world, in all meridians. We cannibals must help these Christians.”

## Chapter 2

After a fine run we arrived in Nantucket.

Nantucket! a mere elbow of sand, all beach without a background; more lonely than the Eddystone. Gamesters tell you they plant weeds there; carry wood like relics; wear quicksand shoes; that to chairs and tables small clams will sometimes be found adhering. Extravaganzas, to show only that Nantucket is no Illinois.

The old legend says an eagle carried off a New England infant; the parents followed in canoes and found an empty ivory casket—the poor little Indian’s skeleton—and so the island was discovered. Born on a beach, these sea hermits took naturally to water: first quohogs and crabs; then mackerel with nets; then cod by boat; last, a navy of great ships, a belt of circumnavigations, peeping in at Behring’s Straits, declaring war with the Himmalehan, salt-sea Mastodon.

Let America pile Cuba on Canada; let England blaze over India; two thirds of this terraqueous globe are the Nantucketer’s. For the sea is his; he owns it, as Emperors own empires; other seamen but have a right of way through it. He alone, in Bible language, goes down to it in ships; to and fro ploughing it as his own plantation. There is his home; there lies his business; a Noah’s flood would not interrupt it. For years he knows not the land; when he comes to it at last, it smells like another world. With the landless gull that at sunset folds her wings and sleeps between billows; so at nightfall, out of sight of land, he furls his sails and lays him to his rest, while under his very pillow rush herds of walruses and whales.

## Chapter 3

It was late when the Moss anchored, so our business was only supper and a bed. Peter Coffin had sent us to his cousin Hosea Hussey’s Try Pots, famed for chowders; but his helter-skelter sea-directions—yellow warehouse starboard till a white church larboard, then three points to starboard, then ask—set us beating about the dark till we came to something unmistakable.

Before an old doorway stood an old top-mast like a gallows, its cross-trees sawed so that two black wooden pots—enormous, suspended by asses’ ears—swung like ominous kettles; two horns left—one for Queequeg, one for me, thought I. A Coffin for my Innkeeper; tombstones at the chapel; and now a gallows and prodigious pots—oblique hints touching Tophet?

A freckled woman in yellow under a dull red lamp scolded a man in a purple shirt. “There’s Mrs. Hussey,” said I. Mr. Hosea being away, she was competent for all. “Clam or Cod?” she cried, seating us among the relics of a late repast. “A clam for supper, ma’am?” I ventured. Hearing only “clam,” she bawled “clam for two,” and vanished. Presently a warm, savory steam belied my fears; then came a chowder of small juicy clams and pounded biscuit, salted pork in little flakes, enriched with butter, pepper and salt—so excellent that, appetites sharpened by frost and Queequeg beholding favorite fishing-food, we despatched it swiftly. I tried an experiment—stepped to the kitchen door and uttered “cod”; soon a fine cod-chowder smoked before us.

Fishiest of all fishy places was the Try Pots: chowder for breakfast, dinner, supper; the area paved with clam-shells; Mrs. Hussey’s necklace of cod vertebra; Hosea’s accounts bound in shark-skin; even the milk fishy, till I spied his brindled cow feeding on fish remnants, marching the beach with each foot in a cod’s decapitated head.

Lamp given and bedward pointed, Mrs. Hussey, learning his name, stayed Queequeg’s ascent—she allowed no harpoon in her chambers. “Dangerous,” said she. “Ever since young Stiggs, back from an unfort’nate v’y’ge with only three barrels of ile, was found dead in my first floor back with his harpoon in his side—no such weepons at night.” “Why not?” I pleaded—“every true whaleman sleeps with his harpoon.” She was firm. “But the chowder; clam or cod to-morrow for breakfast, men?” “Both,” said I; “and a couple of smoked herring by way of variety.”

## Chapter 4

In bed we concocted plans; but Queequeg, consulting Yojo—his black little god—declared that Yojo strongly enjoined the choice of ship should rest wholly with me; Yojo had pitched upon a vessel which, left to myself, I should infallibly light upon; in that vessel I must immediately ship, even for the present irrespective of Queequeg. This I misliked; I had relied on the savage’s sagacity; but he fasted with Yojo that day—some Ramadan or Lent to which his XXXIX Articles were dark to me—and I sallied out alone among the shipping.

Three ships were up for three-years’ voyages: the Devil-dam, the Tit-bit, and the Pequod—named after the extinct tribe. I peered about the first two; then went aboard the Pequod and decided this was the very ship.

You may have seen quaint craft—square-toed luggers, Japanese junks—but never such a rare old Pequod: smallish, old-school, claw-footed look; long-seasoned, weather-stained; venerable bows bearded; her Japanese masts stiff as the three kings of Cologne; decks worn like Becket’s flagstone. To these antiquities were added marvellous features of her wild business. Old Captain Peleg, once chief mate and now a principal owner, had inlaid her grotesqueness with trophies: apparelled like a barbaric Ethiopian emperor, pendants of polished ivory; a cannibal of a craft, tricking herself in the chased bones of her enemies. All round, her open bulwarks were garnished like one continuous jaw, the long sharp teeth of sperm whales for pins; her running-gear travelled over sheaves of sea-ivory; scornful of a wheel, she sported a tiller carved in one mass from the long narrow lower jaw of her hereditary foe. The helmsman in a tempest felt like a Tartar clutching his steed’s jaw. A noble craft, but somehow a most melancholy! All noble things are touched with that.

Aft of the mainmast stood a tent—wigwam-like—conical slabs of right-whale bone laced and tufted at the apex. Half concealed within sat an elderly, brown, brawny man, blue pilot-cloth in Quaker cut, his eyes pursed by windward scowls—Captain Peleg. “Supposing it be the captain of the Pequod, what dost thou want?” he demanded. I said I would ship. He damned the “marchant service,” flourished his leg—“I’ll take that leg away from thy stern if ever thou talkest the marchant service to me again”—and plied me with suspicions and jests about pirates and murderers. At last, “What takes thee a-whaling?” “To see what it is; to see the world,” I said. “Clap eye on Captain Ahab, young man, and thou wilt find he has but one leg,” he cried. “Was the other lost by a whale?” “Devoured—chewed up—crunched by the monstrousest parmacetty that ever chipped a boat!”

He sent me forward to peep over the weather-bow. The ship pointed seaward; an unlimited, monotonous prospect; “nothing but water—and a squall coming.” “Well, what dost thou think then of seeing the world? Can’t ye see the world where you stand?” But go a-whaling I must, and he would ship me.

Below, in the small cabin, sat Captain Bildad—tall, gaunt, drab to the chin, spectacles on nose—reading a ponderous Scripture. Like many Nantucketers, both were Quakers; but some of that sect are the most sanguinary of all whale-hunters—fighting Quakers—with a vengeance. Bildad, from drab cabin-boy to harpooneer, boat-header, chief-mate, captain, and owner, had retired rich, and, to say truth, had the name of an incorrigible old hunks; hard-hearted in discipline though he never swore—extracting unmitigated hard work from men with the drab eye alone. His religion refused arms ashore, yet had spilled tuns upon tuns of leviathan gore afloat. Very probably he had come to the sensible conclusion that a man’s religion is one thing, this practical world another. This world pays dividends.

Peleg spread the ship’s articles. In whaling they pay no wages; all hands receive shares called lays, proportioned to duty. A green hand like me might hope for the 275th lay; a long lay, but something. While Peleg hacked at his pen, Bildad mumbled over his book—“Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth—” “Well, Captain Bildad,” cried Peleg, “what lay shall we give this young man?” “The seven hundred and seventy-seventh wouldn’t be too much,” said Bildad, never lifting his eyes—“where moth and rust do corrupt, but lay—” “Lay, indeed!” thought I; the 777th part of a farthing is leaner than seven hundred and seventy-seven doubloons. “Blast your eyes, Bildad,” roared Peleg, “thou dost not want to swindle this young man! He must have more than that. I put him down for the three hundredth.” Bildad lifted his drab face: widows and orphans must be considered; too abundant rewards take bread from them—the 777th lay, Captain Peleg. Then flame met drab. “Fiery pit! fiery pit! ye insult me, man; tell me I’m bound to hell, and I’ll—yes, I’ll swallow a live goat with all his hair and horns on! Out of the cabin, ye canting, drab-coloured son of a wooden gun!” With a marvellous sliding celerity, Bildad eluded the rush and sat down again; used to impenitent Peleg. The squall blew by; “Whew!” whistled Peleg—“mend that pen, will ye, Bildad.” And so I was set down for the three hundredth lay.

I asked leave to bring a friend next day. “Fetch him,” said Peleg. Signed, I climbed on deck, but bethought me—the captain remained unseen. Often a whale-ship is fitted and crewed ere the captain arrives; yet see him one should. “Where is Captain Ahab?” I asked. Peleg frowned: Ahab kept close; not sick, not well; a queer man, so some think, but a good one. “Oh, thou’lt like him well enough. He’s a grand, ungodly, god-like man; doesn’t speak much; but when he does—listen. Ahab’s above the common; colleges and cannibals both have schooled him; his lance the keenest out of all our isle. He ain’t Captain Bildad; he ain’t Captain Peleg; he’s Ahab, boy; and Ahab of old, thou knowest, was a crowned king!” “And a very vile one,” said I; “did not the dogs lick his blood?” Peleg’s eye fixed me. “Never say that on board the Pequod. His mother, crazy and widowed, named him; fools have called it prophetic. ’Tis a lie. I know him; I sailed with him; a good man—not pious-good like Bildad, but a swearing good man—something like me, only more of him. He was never very jolly; and on the passage home he was a little out of his mind—a spell from the sharp shooting pains in his bleeding stump. Ever since he lost his leg last voyage by that accursed whale, he’s been moody—desperate moody and savage sometimes; but that will pass. Better sail with a moody good captain than a laughing bad one. And remember: he has a wife—not three-voyages wed—a sweet, resigned girl; a child, by that sweet girl. Stricken, blasted, if he be, Ahab has his humanities!”

I walked away full of thoughtfulness: a vague, painful sympathy and a strange awe—not exactly awe—possessed me; yet it did not disincline me toward him. So, for the present, dark Ahab slipped my mind.

## Chapter 5

Queequeg’s Ramadan—his day of Fasting and Humiliation—was to last till nightfall. I respect all men’s obligations, never mind how comical, and could not undervalue even ants worshipping a toad-stool. We Presbyterians should be charitable: Heaven have mercy on us all—Presbyterians and Pagans alike—for we are somehow dreadfully cracked about the head.

Towards evening I knocked; no answer. The door was fast within. Peering the key-hole I saw only a corner and—strange!—the wooden shaft of his harpoon leaning by the wall, though Mrs. Hussey had taken it overnight. Then surely he was inside. “Apoplexy!” cried the chamber-maid; down came Mrs. Hussey with mustard-pot and vinegar-cruet—“castors” in hand, scolding her little black boy. “The axe! the axe!” I cried. She thrust out her countenance entire—“Do you talk of prying open any of my doors?” She felt in the closet under the landing—Queequeg’s harpoon was missing. “He’s killed himself! Unfort’nate Stiggs done over again. There goes another counterpane!” She would paint a sign: “no suicides permitted here, and no smoking in the parlor.” Her key turned; the supplemental bolt held. I ran for a start; she caught at me; I dashed—crash went the door; knob smote plaster; and there sat Queequeg, cool and self-collected in the middle of the room, squatting on his hams, holding Yojo on his head like a carved image.

All we said, we could drag out no word. He had sat so eight or ten hours, fasting. I left him; went to supper; listened to plum-puddingers till near eleven; returned—there he squatted still. Vexed, I besought him to shake himself; no reply. I threw my bearskin over him against the cold night and lay down; the thought of a wide-awake pagan on his hams in the dark made me wretched; but at last I slept. With dawn’s first glimpse he rose—stiff joints, cheerful look—pressed his forehead to mine: his Ramadan was over.

I have no quarrel with any man’s religion so long as he kills not nor insults; but when a creed torments a man and makes this earth an uncomfortable inn, argue the point I must. I lectured him—rise and progress of primitive religions; present sects; how Lents, Ramadans, and ham-squattings are stark nonsense; bad for health; useless for soul; opposed to Hygiene and common sense. Fasting caves the body, then the spirit; most dyspeptic religionists beget melancholy hereafters. In one word, hell is an idea first born on an undigested apple-dumpling, perpetuated by hereditary dyspepsias nurtured by Ramadans. Had he dyspepsia? “Once,” he said—after his father the king’s great feast: fifty enemies killed by two o’clock, cooked and eaten that evening. “No more,” I cried, shuddering; I knew the rest.

My sermon little moved him; he thought me pitiably lost to evangelical pagan piety. At last we dressed; and Queequeg, taking a prodigiously hearty breakfast of chowders of all sorts, that the landlady might not profit by his fast, we sallied toward the Pequod, picking our teeth with halibut bones.

## Chapter 6

As we walked down the wharf, Queequeg carrying his harpoon, Captain Peleg hailed from his whale-bone wigwam. He let no cannibals aboard unless they showed papers. “He must show that he’s converted,” added Captain Bildad’s hollow voice. “Son of darkness, art thou at present in communion with any Christian church?”

“He’s a member of the First Congregational Church,” said I. “First Congregational—Deacon Deuteronomy Coleman’s?” cried Bildad, rubbing spectacles and peering stiffly at Queequeg. “Not very long a member, I guess.” “Nor baptized right,” said Peleg, “or it would have washed some of that devil’s blue off his face.” Pressed, I explained: “I mean the same ancient Catholic Church to which you and I and Captain Peleg and Queequeg and all of us belong; the great and everlasting First Congregation of this whole worshipping world; only some of us cherish queer crotchets that touch not the grand belief. In that we all join hands.” “Splice, thou mean’st splice hands,” cried Peleg. “Never mind papers. Tell Quohog there—what’s his name?—to step along. By the great anchor, what a harpoon! Quohog, ever stand in a whale-boat’s head? ever strike a fish?”

Without a word, Queequeg leaped to a boat in the davits; bracing his left knee, he poised his iron: “Cap’ain, you see him small drop tar on water dere? you see him?—well, spose him one whale eye—well, den!” He darted—the iron hissed over Bildad’s broad brim; the glistening spot vanished. “Spos-ee him whale-e eye; why, dad whale dead.”

“Quick, Bildad,” said Peleg, retreating aghast toward the gangway, “get the ship’s papers. We must have Hedgehog—Quohog—in one of our boats. The ninetieth lay, and that’s more than ever was given a harpooneer yet out of Nantucket.”

Down we went; soon Queequeg was enrolled with me. “Quohog there don’t write, does he?” said Peleg. “Dost thou sign thy name or make thy mark?” Unabashed, Queequeg took the pen and copied in the proper place the queer round figure tattooed on his arm—so that, through Peleg’s obstinate mistake, it stood: Quohog. his X mark.

Bildad, steadfastly eyeing him, rose and drew from his drab pockets a tract—“The Latter Day Coming; or No Time to Lose”—and, grasping book and hands together, said, “Son of darkness, remain not for aye a Belial bondsman. Spurn the idol Bell; turn from the wrath to come; steer clear of the fiery pit!” “Avast there, Bildad,” cried Peleg, “avast spoiling our harpooneer. Pious harpooneers never make good voyagers; it takes the shark out of ’em. Young Nat Swaine joined the meeting and never came to good; he sheered from whales for fear of after-claps.” “Peleg!” said Bildad, lifting eyes and hands, “thou knowest the fear of death. When this same Pequod had her three masts overboard in that typhoon on Japan, did’st thou not think of Death and the Judgment then?” “Think of Death and the Judgment then?” cried Peleg, marching the cabin, hands deep in pockets. “No! Life was what Captain Ahab and I thought of—how to save all hands; rig jury-masts; make port—that was what I was thinking of.”

Bildad buttoned up and stalked on deck; we followed. He stood quietly overlooking the sailmakers in the waist—now and then stooping to save a patch, or an end of tarred twine, which otherwise might have been wasted.

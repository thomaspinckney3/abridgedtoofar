“Who would have thought it, Flask!” cried Stubb. “If I had but one leg ye’d not catch me in a boat—unless to stop the plug-hole with my timber toe.” Flask made nothing of it: Ahab had a knee and good part of the other; if off at the hip, that were disabling.

Among whale-wise people it has often been argued whether, considering the paramount importance of his life to the success of the voyage, it is right for a whaling captain to jeopardize that life in the active perils of the chase. So Tamerlane’s soldiers often argued with tears in their eyes, whether that invaluable life of his ought to be carried into the thickest of the fight. But with Ahab, the question assumed a modified aspect: in a calling where every individual moment comprises a peril, was it wise for any maimed man to enter a whale-boat? As a general thing, the joint-owners of the Pequod must have plainly thought not. Ahab well knew his friends at home might think little of his lowering in certain comparatively harmless vicissitudes of the chase, for nearness and command; but to have a boat apportioned to him as regular headsman, and—above all—to be supplied with five extra men for that same boat—no such conceits had the owners. He therefore solicited no crew, hinted nothing. Nevertheless he took private measures. After the whaleboats were fitted, men had spied him bestirring himself—making thole-pins with his own hands for what all thought a spare boat; shaping with solicitous care the small wooden skewers to pin the bow-groove over the running line; ordering an extra sheathing to the bottom—as if to better bear the pointed pressure of his ivory limb; and, most of all, exactly shaping the thigh-board—the clumsy cleat to brace the knee when darting. Oft he stood in that boat, his lone knee fixed in the semicircle, gouging and straightening with the carpenter’s chisel. Folks deemed all this but heedfulness for the ultimate chase of Moby Dick, whom he had vowed to hunt in person; no one dreamed a separate boat’s crew was assigned to that boat.

With the subordinate phantoms—those yellow men—what wonder remained soon waned; for in a whaler wonders soon wane. Outlandish odds and ends of nations ever come up to man these floating outlaws; ships pick queer castaways from planks, wrecks, canoes, blown-off Japanese junks; Beelzebub himself might climb the side and step into the cabin to chat with the captain, and it would not create any unsubduable excitement in the forecastle.

But the hair-turbaned Fedallah remained a muffled mystery to the last. Whence he came; by what unaccountable tie he was linked to Ahab’s fortunes; nay, what half-hinted influence—Heaven knows, it might have been authority—he held over him; none knew. But one cannot sustain an indifferent air concerning Fedallah. He was such a creature as civilized, domestic people in the temperate zone only see in their dreams, and that but dimly; but the like of whom now and then glide among the unchanging Asiatic communities, especially the Oriental isles to the east of the continent—those insulated, immemorial, unalterable countries, which even in these modern days still preserve much of the ghostly aboriginalness of earth’s primal generations, when the memory of the first man was a distinct recollection, and all men his descendants, unknowing whence he came, eyed each other as real phantoms, and asked of the sun and the moon why they were created and to what end; when though, according to Genesis, the angels indeed consorted with the daughters of men, the devils also, add the uncanonical Rabbins, indulged in mundane amours.

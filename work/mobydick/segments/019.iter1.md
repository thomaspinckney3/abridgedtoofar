In bed we concocted plans; but Queequeg, consulting Yojo—his black little god—declared that Yojo strongly enjoined the choice of ship should rest wholly with me; Yojo had pitched upon a vessel which, left to myself, I should infallibly light upon; in that vessel I must immediately ship, even for the present irrespective of Queequeg. This I misliked; I had relied on the savage’s sagacity; but he fasted with Yojo that day, and I sallied out alone among the shipping.

Three ships were up for three-years’ voyages: the Devil-dam, the Tit-bit, and the Pequod—named after the extinct tribe. I peered about the first two; then went aboard the Pequod and decided this was the very ship.

You may have seen quaint craft—square-toed luggers, Japanese junks—but never such a rare old Pequod: smallish, old-school, claw-footed look; long-seasoned, weather-stained; her venerable bows bearded; her masts stood stiff; decks worn like a pilgrim-worshipped flagstone. To these antiquities were added marvellous features of her wild business. Old Captain Peleg, once chief mate and now a principal owner, had inlaid her grotesqueness with trophies: apparelled like a barbaric Ethiopian emperor, pendants of polished ivory; a cannibal of a craft, tricking herself in the chased bones of her enemies. All round, her open bulwarks were garnished like one continuous jaw, the long sharp teeth of sperm whales for pins; her running-gear travelled over sheaves of sea-ivory; scornful of a wheel, she sported a tiller carved in one mass from the long narrow lower jaw of her hereditary foe. The helmsman in a tempest felt like a Tartar clutching his steed’s jaw. A noble craft, but somehow a most melancholy! All noble things are touched with that.

Aft of the mainmast stood a tent—wigwam-like—conical slabs of right-whale bone laced and tufted at the apex. Half concealed within sat an elderly, brown, brawny man, blue pilot-cloth in Quaker cut, his eyes pursed by windward scowls—Captain Peleg. “Supposing it be the captain of the Pequod, what dost thou want?” he demanded. I said I would ship. He damned the “marchant service,” flourished his leg—“I’ll take that leg away from thy stern if ever thou talkest the marchant service to me again”—and plied me with suspicions and jests about pirates and murderers. At last, “What takes thee a-whaling?” “To see what it is; to see the world,” I said. “Clap eye on Captain Ahab, young man, and thou wilt find he has but one leg,” he cried. “Was the other lost by a whale?” “Devoured—chewed up—crunched by the monstrousest parmacetty that ever chipped a boat!”

He sent me forward to peep over the weather-bow. The ship pointed seaward; an unlimited, monotonous prospect; “nothing but water—and a squall coming.” “Well, what dost thou think then of seeing the world? Can’t ye see the world where you stand?” But go a-whaling I must, and he would ship me.

Below, in the small cabin, sat Captain Bildad—tall, gaunt, drab to the chin, spectacles on nose—reading a ponderous Scripture. Like many Nantucketers, both were Quakers; but some of that sect are the most sanguinary of all whale-hunters—fighting Quakers—with a vengeance. And when these things unite in a man of greatly superior natural force, with a globular brain and a ponderous heart; who has also by the stillness and seclusion of many long night-watches in the remotest waters, and beneath constellations never seen here at the north, been led to think untraditionally and independently; receiving all nature’s sweet or savage impressions fresh from her own virgin voluntary and confiding breast, and thereby chiefly, but with some help from accidental advantages, to learn a bold and nervous lofty language—that man makes one in a whole nation’s census—a mighty pageant creature, formed for noble tragedies. Nor will it at all detract from him, dramatically regarded, if either by birth or other circumstances, he have what seems a half wilful overruling morbidness at the bottom of his nature. For all men tragically great are made so through a certain morbidness. Be sure of this, O young ambition, all mortal greatness is but disease. But, as yet we have not to do with such an one, but with quite another; and still a man, who, if indeed peculiar, it only results again from another phase of the Quaker, modified by individual circumstances. Bildad, retired from the sea and a principal owner, had the name of an incorrigible old hunks; hard-hearted in discipline though he never swore—getting unmitigated hard work out of men with the drab eye alone. His religion refused arms ashore, yet he had spilled tuns upon tuns of leviathan gore afloat. Very probably he had come to the sensible conclusion that a man’s religion is one thing, this practical world another. This world pays dividends.

“Dost thee?” said Bildad, in a hollow tone. “I _dost_,” said I unconsciously, he was so intense a Quaker. Peleg spread the ship’s articles. In whaling they pay no wages; all hands receive shares called lays, proportioned to duty. A green hand like me might hope for the 275th lay; a long lay, but something. It might be thought that this was a poor way to accumulate a princely fortune—and so it was, a very poor way indeed. But I am one of those that never take on about princely fortunes, and am quite content if the world is ready to board and lodge me, while I am putting up at this grim sign of the Thunder Cloud. While Peleg hacked at his pen, Bildad mumbled over his book—“Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth—” “Well, Captain Bildad,” cried Peleg, “what lay shall we give this young man?” “The seven hundred and seventy-seventh wouldn’t be too much,” said Bildad, never lifting his eyes—“where moth and rust do corrupt, but lay—” “Blast your eyes, Bildad,” roared Peleg, “thou dost not want to swindle this young man! He must have more than that. I put him down for the three hundredth.” Bildad lifted his drab face: widows and orphans must be considered; too abundant rewards take bread from them—the 777th lay, Captain Peleg. Then flame met drab. “Fiery pit! fiery pit! ye insult me, man; tell me I’m bound to hell, and I’ll—yes, I’ll swallow a live goat with all his hair and horns on! Out of the cabin, ye canting, drab-coloured son of a wooden gun!” The squall blew by; “Whew!” whistled Peleg—“mend that pen, will ye, Bildad.” And so I was set down for the three hundredth lay.

I asked leave to bring a friend next day. “Fetch him,” said Peleg. Signed, I climbed on deck, but bethought me—the captain remained unseen. Often a whale-ship is fitted and crewed ere the captain arrives; yet see him one should. “Where is Captain Ahab?” I asked. Peleg frowned: Ahab kept close; not sick, not well; a queer man, so some think, but a good one. “Oh, thou’lt like him well enough. He’s a grand, ungodly, god-like man; doesn’t speak much; but when he does—listen. Ahab’s above the common; colleges and cannibals both have schooled him; his lance the keenest out of all our isle. He ain’t Captain Bildad; he ain’t Captain Peleg; he’s Ahab, boy; and Ahab of old, thou knowest, was a crowned king!” “And a very vile one,” said I; “did not the dogs lick his blood?” Peleg’s eye fixed me. “Never say that on board the Pequod. His mother, crazy and widowed, named him; fools have called it prophetic. ’Tis a lie. I know him; I sailed with him; a good man—not pious-good like Bildad, but a swearing good man—something like me, only more of him. He was never very jolly; and on the passage home he was a little out of his mind—a spell from the sharp shooting pains in his bleeding stump. Ever since he lost his leg last voyage by that accursed whale, he’s been moody—desperate moody and savage sometimes; but that will pass. Better sail with a moody good captain than a laughing bad one. And remember: he has a wife—not three-voyages wed—a sweet, resigned girl; a child, by that sweet girl. Stricken, blasted, if he be, Ahab has his humanities!”

As I walked away, I was full of thoughtfulness; what had been incidentally revealed to me of Captain Ahab, filled me with a certain wild vagueness of painfulness concerning him. And somehow, at the time, I felt a sympathy and a sorrow for him, but for I don’t know what, unless it was the cruel loss of his leg. And yet I also felt a strange awe of him; but that sort of awe, which I cannot at all describe, was not exactly awe; I do not know what it was. But I felt it; and it did not disincline me towards him. However, my thoughts were at length carried in other directions, so that for the present dark Ahab slipped my mind.

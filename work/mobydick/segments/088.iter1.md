That for six thousand years—and no one knows how many ages before—the great whales should have been spouting all over the sea, and sprinkling and mistifying the gardens of the deep, as with so many sprinkling or mistifying pots; and that for centuries thousands of hunters have watched at the very fountain; and yet, down to this blessed minute (fifteen and a quarter minutes past one o’clock P.M., December 16th, 1851), it should remain a problem whether these spoutings are water or vapor—this is noteworthy.

Whales have regular lungs; the Sperm’s windpipe has no connexion with the mouth; he breathes through his spiracle atop the head. Anomalous as it seems, the whale does thus live by intervals, his full hour and more at the bottom, without drawing a breath. Between ribs and spine he carries a Cretan labyrinth of vermicelli-like vessels, distended with oxygenated blood when he quits the surface—his surplus stock of vitality, as a camel’s supplementary stomachs carry drink.

Hence that obstinacy in having his spoutings out. Unmolested, if he stays his set time and jets his set number, he will repeat them to a minute on rising. Alarm him after a few breaths, he will be dodging up to make good his allowance; not till they are told will he go down for his term. How obvious, too, that this necessity for rising exposes him to all the fatal hazards of the chase; for not by hook or by net could this vast leviathan be caught a thousand fathoms beneath the sunlight. Not so much thy skill, then, O hunter, as the great necessities that strike the victory to thee! In man breathing is incessant; but the Sperm Whale only breathes about one seventh or Sunday of his time.

It has been said that the whale only breathes through his spout-hole; if it could truthfully be added that his spouts are mixed with water, then I opine we should be furnished with the reason why his sense of smell seems obliterated in him; for the only thing about him that at all answers to his nose is that identical spout-hole. But what does he want of them? No roses, no violets, no Cologne-water in the sea.

As the spouting canal is locked against water, and opens to the windpipe alone, the whale has no voice—unless you say that when he strangely rumbles, he talks through his nose. But then again, what has the whale to say? Seldom have I known any profound being that had anything to say to this world, unless forced to stammer for a living. Oh! happy that the world is such an excellent listener!

But why pester one with all this reasoning on the subject? Speak out! You have seen him spout; then declare what the spout is; can you not tell water from air? My dear sir, in this world it is not so easy to settle these plain things. I have ever found your plain things the knottiest of all. And as for this whale spout, you might almost stand in it, and yet be undecided as to what it is precisely.

The central body is hidden in snowy mist; close by, the sea is all commotion, cascading; the drops you think you see may be mere condensation, or those few always lodged in the countersunk fissure of the blow-hole, for even in calm the whale carries a small basin of water on his head, as a rock-cavity holds rain. Nor is it prudent to be over-curious. Among whalemen, the spout is deemed poisonous; they try to evade it. I know one who, coming closer, had the skin peel; and if spouted fairly into your eyes, it will blind you. Best let this deadly fountain alone.

Still we may hypothesize. Mine is this: the spout is nothing but mist. And I am impelled to it by considerations touching the whale’s inherent dignity and sublimity—never found on soundings, nor near shores; both ponderous and profound. From the heads of all ponderous profound beings—Plato, Pyrrho, the Devil, Jupiter, Dante—there goes up a certain semi-visible steam while thinking deep thoughts. While composing a treatise on Eternity, I placed a mirror before me and saw a curious worming and undulation in the atmosphere over my head; the invariable moisture of my hair, after six cups of hot tea in my thin-shingled attic, of an August noon, seems further argument.

How nobly it raises our conceit of the mighty, misty monster to behold him solemnly sailing a calm tropical sea, his vast mild head overhung by a canopy of vapor—sometimes glorified by a rainbow, as if Heaven had put its seal upon his thoughts. For rainbows do not visit clear air; they irradiate vapor. And through all the thick mists of my doubts, divine intuitions now and then shoot, enkindling my fog with a heavenly ray. For this I thank God; for all have doubts; many deny; but doubts or denials, few along with them have intuitions. Doubts of all things earthly, and intuitions of some things heavenly; this combination makes neither believer nor infidel, but makes a man who regards them both with equal eye.

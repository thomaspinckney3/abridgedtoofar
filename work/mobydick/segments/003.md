## The Carpet-Bag

I stuffed a shirt or two into my old carpet-bag, tucked it under my arm, and started for Cape Horn and the Pacific. Quitting the good city of old Manhatto, I duly arrived in New Bedford on a Saturday night in December, only to learn that the little packet for Nantucket had already sailed, and no way of reaching that place would offer till the following Monday.

I was set on sailing in no other than a Nantucket craft. Though New Bedford has of late monopolized the whaling business, Nantucket was her great original—the Tyre of this Carthage—the place where the first dead American whale was stranded, and whence that first adventurous little sloop put forth, partly laden (so goes the story) with imported cobblestones to throw at the whales.

Having a night, a day, and another night before me, it became a matter of concern where to eat and sleep. The night was dark and dismal, bitingly cold. I knew no one in the place, and sounding my pocket brought up only a few pieces of silver. *Wherever you go, Ishmael,* said I to myself, *be sure to inquire the price, and don't be too particular.*

I paced past "The Crossed Harpoons"—too expensive and jolly. Further on, the red windows of the "Sword-Fish Inn" blazed so fervently that they seemed to have melted the packed snow before the house. Too jolly again. I followed the streets waterward, where doubtless were the cheapest inns.

Such dreary streets! Blocks of blackness, not houses; here and there a candle like a candle moving about in a tomb. Presently I stumbled into a low, wide building and nearly choked on flying ash from a box in the porch. A hundred black faces turned to peer at me; beyond, a preacher beat a book in the pulpit, his text upon the blackness of darkness, and the weeping and wailing and teeth-gnashing there. *Wretched entertainment at the sign of 'The Trap!'* I muttered, backing out.

At last, near the docks, a forlorn creaking drew my eye upward to a swinging sign: a tall jet of misty spray painted white, and underneath—"The Spouter Inn:—Peter Coffin."

Coffin?—Spouter?—Rather ominous in that particular connexion, thought I. But it is a common name in Nantucket. The dim light, the dilapidated wooden house leaning sadly on a bleak corner where the tempestuous wind Euroclydon howled worse than ever it did about poor Paul's tossed craft—all promised cheap lodgings.

Euroclydon, nevertheless, is a mighty pleasant zephyr to any one in-doors, with his feet on the hob. "In judging of that tempestuous wind," says an old writer, "it maketh a marvellous difference, whether thou lookest out at it from a glass window where the frost is all on the outside, or whether thou observest it from that sashless window, where the frost is on both sides, and of which the wight Death is the only glazier." True enough. These eyes are windows, and this body of mine is the house—a pity they didn't stop up the chinks. But it is too late; the universe is finished, the copestone is on, the chips were carted off a million years ago. Poor Lazarus, chattering his teeth against the curbstone, could plug his ears with rags and still not keep out Euroclydon. *Pooh, pooh!* says old Dives in his red silken wrapper; *what a fine frosty night; how Orion glitters.* But can Lazarus warm his blue hands by the northern lights? Would he not rather lie down along the equator, yea, go down to the fiery pit, to keep out this frost? Dives himself lives like a Czar in an ice palace of frozen sighs, drinking only the tepid tears of orphans.

But no more of this blubbering now; we are going a-whaling. Let us scrape the ice from our frosted feet, and see what sort of a place this "Spouter" may be.

Steering by Ahab’s levelled steel and his own levelled log and line, the Pequod held towards the Equator; making a long passage through unfrequented waters, over waves monotonously mild—strange calms preluding some riotous and desperate scene.

Near the outskirts of the Equatorial fishing-ground, in the deep darkness before the dawn, as she sailed by a cluster of rocky islets, the watch—then headed by Flask—was startled by a cry so plaintively wild and unearthly—like half-articulated wailings of the ghosts of all Herod’s murdered Innocents—that they stood, or sat, or leaned all transfixedly listening. The Christian or civilized part of the crew said it was mermaids, and shuddered; but the pagan harpooneers remained unappalled. Yet the grey Manxman—oldest mariner of all—declared those wild thrilling sounds were the voices of newly drowned men.

At sunrise a sailor went from his hammock to his mast-head at the fore; and, ere long, a cry and a rushing—and looking up, they saw a falling phantom in the air; and looking down, a little tossed heap of white bubbles in the blue of the sea. The life-buoy dropped from the stern, where it always hung obedient to a cunning spring; but the sun had so shrunken and parched the cask that it slowly filled, and the studded iron-bound cask followed the sailor to the bottom, as if to yield him his pillow, though but a hard one.

Thus the first man of the Pequod who mounted the mast to look out for the White Whale, on the White Whale’s own peculiar ground, was swallowed up in the deep. But few, perhaps, thought of that then. Indeed, in some sort, they were not grieved at this event, at least as a portent; for they regarded it, not as a foreshadowing of evil in the future, but as the fulfilment of an evil already presaged. They declared that now they knew the reason of those wild shrieks heard the night before. But again the old Manxman said nay.

The lost buoy must be replaced; Starbuck was directed to see to it; but no cask of sufficient lightness could be found, and all hands were impatient of any toil not directly bent to the voyage’s end. They were going to leave the stern unprovided, when by certain strange signs and innuendoes Queequeg hinted a hint concerning his coffin.

“A life-buoy of a coffin!” cried Starbuck, starting.

“Rather queer, that, I should say,” said Stubb.

“It will make a good enough one,” said Flask.

“Bring it up; there’s nothing else for it,” said Starbuck. “Rig it, carpenter.”

“And shall I nail down the lid, sir?”

“Aye.”

“And shall I caulk the seams, sir?”

“Aye.”

“And shall I then pay over the same with pitch, sir?”

“Away! Make a life-buoy of the coffin, and no more.”

“He goes off in a huff. The whole he can endure; at the parts he baulks. Now I don’t like this. I make a leg for Captain Ahab, and he wears it like a gentleman; but I make a bandbox for Queequeg, and he won’t put his head into it. I don’t like this cobbling sort of business—I don’t like it at all; it’s undignified; it’s not my place. Let tinkers’ brats do tinkerings; we are their betters. I like to take in hand none but clean, virgin, fair-and-square mathematical jobs, something that regularly begins at the beginning, and is at the middle when midway, and comes to an end at the conclusion; not a cobbler’s job, that’s at an end in the middle, and at the beginning at the end. Nail down the lid; caulk the seams; pay over the same with pitch; batten it tight, and hang it with the snap-spring over the stern. Were ever such things done before with a coffin? Cruppered with a coffin! Sailing about with a grave-yard tray! But never mind. We workers in woods make bridal-bedsteads and card-tables, as well as coffins and hearses. Any way, I’ll have me thirty separate, Turk’s-headed life-lines, each three feet long, hanging all round to the coffin. Then, if the hull go down, there’ll be thirty lively fellows all fighting for one coffin. Come, hammer, caulking-iron, pitch-pot, and marling-spike! Let’s to it.”

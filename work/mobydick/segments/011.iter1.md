Presently there entered a man of venerable robustness, sleet running from his tarpaulin; hat, coat, and overshoes shed, he came decently to the pulpit. It was Father Mapple—sailor and harpooneer once; for many years past a minister; now, in the hardy winter of a healthy old age—that sort of old age which seems merging into a second flowering youth, for among all the fissures of his wrinkles, there shone certain mild gleams of a newly developing bloom—the spring verdure peeping forth even beneath February’s snow.

The pulpit was very lofty, reached not by stairs but by a perpendicular side ladder with red worsted man-ropes, like mounting a ship from a boat. Hand over hand, sailor-like yet reverential, he ascended; then, turning, stooped and deliberately dragged up the ladder, depositing it within—leaving him impregnable in his little Quebec. I pondered: Can it be, then, that by that act of physical isolation, he signifies his spiritual withdrawal for the time, from all outward worldly ties and connexions? Yes, for replenished with the meat and wine of the word, to the faithful man of God, this pulpit, I see, is a self-containing stronghold—a lofty Ehrenbreitstein, with a perennial well of water within the walls.

Between the marble cenotaphs behind him hung a large painting: a gallant ship beating against a terrible storm off a lee coast; high above the flying scud a little isle of sunlight, from which beamed forth an angel’s face, shedding a distinct radiance upon the tossed deck. “Ah, noble ship,” the angel seemed to say, “beat on, beat on, thou noble ship, and bear a hardy helm; for lo! the sun is breaking through; the clouds are rolling off—serenest azure is at hand.”

The pulpit’s panelled front showed a ship’s bluff bows; the Holy Bible rested on a projecting piece of scroll work, fashioned after a ship’s fiddle-headed beak. What could be more full of meaning?—for the pulpit is ever this earth’s foremost part; all the rest comes in its rear; the pulpit leads the world. From thence it is the storm of God’s quick wrath is first descried, and the bow must bear the earliest brunt. From thence it is the God of breezes fair or foul is first invoked for favourable winds. Yes, the world’s a ship on its passage out, and not a voyage complete; and the pulpit is its prow.

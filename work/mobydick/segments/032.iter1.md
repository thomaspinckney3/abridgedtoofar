Ice and icebergs astern, the Pequod rolled into the bright Quito spring—the warmly cool, clear, ringing, perfumed, overflowing, redundant days, were as crystal goblets of Persian sherbet, heaped up—flaked up, with rose-water snow. The starred and stately nights seemed haughty dames in jewelled velvets, nursing at home in lonely pride, the memory of their absent conquering Earls, the golden helmeted suns! But all that witchery turned inward. Inward they turned upon the soul, especially when the still mild hours of eve came on; then, memory shot her crystals as the clear ice most forms of noiseless twilights. And more and more it wrought on Ahab’s texture. Old age is always wakeful; and Ahab, long linked with life, haunted the open air, muttering that to descend this narrow scuttle to his grave-dug berth “feels like going down into one’s tomb.”

Nightly, when quietude fell, he would emerge, gripping the iron banister, and usually abstained from patrolling the quarter-deck—the crack of the ivory heel would have set dreams on the crunching teeth of sharks. But once the mood was on him too deep; with heavy, lumber-like pace he measured the ship from taffrail to mainmast, till Stubb came up from below with deprecating humorousness, hinting at muffling the noise with a globe of tow for the ivory heel. “Am I a cannon-ball, Stubb,” said Ahab, “that thou wouldst wad me that fashion? But go thy ways; I had forgot. Below to thy nightly grave… Down, dog, and kennel!” Starting at the unforeseen concluding exclamation of the so suddenly scornful old man, Stubb was speechless a moment; then said excitedly, “I am not used to be spoken to that way, sir; I do but less than half like it, sir.” “Avast!” gritted Ahab between his set teeth, and violently moving away, as if to avoid some passionate temptation. “No, sir; not yet,” said Stubb, emboldened, “I will not tamely be called a dog, sir.” “Then be called ten times a donkey, and a mule, and an ass, and begone, or I’ll clear the world of thee!” Ahab advanced upon him with such overbearing terrors that Stubb involuntarily retreated down the scuttle.

There he muttered, “It’s very queer. Stop, Stubb; somehow, now, I don’t well know whether to go back and strike him, or—what’s that?—down here on my knees and pray for him? Yes, that was the thought coming up in me; but it would be the first time I ever did pray.” How he flashed at me—his eyes like powder-pans! is he mad? A hot old man, with a conscience—“a kind of Tic-Dolly-row—worse nor a toothache.” Didn’t that Dough-Boy tell me, of a morning he always finds the old man’s hammock clothes rumpled and tumbled, the sheets down at the foot, the coverlid almost tied into knots, and the pillow a sort of frightful hot, as though a baked brick had been on it? “Here goes for a snooze. Think not, is my eleventh commandment; and sleep when you can, is my twelfth.” Yet even as he slung to hammock, he started: “didn’t he call me a dog?—ten times a donkey—and pile jackasses on top of that!” His brow flashed like a bleached bone. “By the Lord, I must have been dreaming—How? how? how?—the only way’s to stash it; so here goes to hammock again; and in the morning, I’ll see how this plaguey juggling thinks over by daylight.”

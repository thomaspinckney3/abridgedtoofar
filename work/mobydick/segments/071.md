I have given no small attention to that not unvexed subject, the skin of the whale. I have had controversies about it with experienced whalemen afloat, and learned naturalists ashore.

What and where is the skin of the whale? From the unmarred body you can scrape an infinitely thin, transparent substance—isinglass-like, soft as satin until dried; I have used such shreds for markers in my whale-books; it is pleasant to read about whales through their own spectacles, as you may say. But that is rather the skin of the skin, so to speak. The only dense enveloping layer you can raise is the blubber: firm, elastic, compact, eight to fifteen inches thick. Assuming the blubber for skin, then, when a great Sperm yields a hundred barrels of oil, which is but three-fourths of its expressed substance, conceive the enormity of the mass whose mere integument pours such a lake.

In life, the Sperm’s visible surface is marvellously graven—crossed with numberless straight marks like the finest Italian line-engraving. Those marks are not upon the isinglass above mentioned, but seem seen through it, as if engraved upon the body itself; and, to the quick eye, they afford the ground for far other delineations. These are hieroglyphical; that is, if you call those mysterious cyphers on the walls of pyramids hieroglyphics, then that is the proper word to use in the present connexion. By my retentive memory of the hieroglyphics upon one Sperm Whale in particular, I was much struck with a plate representing the old Indian characters chiselled on the famous hieroglyphic palisades on the banks of the Upper Mississippi. Like those mystic rocks, too, the mystic-marked whale remains undecipherable. Besides, in some parts rude random scratches efface the lines—hostile contacts, I deem, among the large, full-grown bulls; New England rocks scored by ice may resemble him there.

Blanket-pieces they call the long strips—aptly; for he is wrapt in his blubber as in a blanket or Indian poncho. By this cosy surtout he keeps comfortable in all weathers, in all seas. Other fish—cold-blooded, lungless refrigerators—may bask under the lee of an iceberg; but the whale, like man, has warm blood and lungs: freeze his blood, and he dies. How wonderful is it then—except after explanation—that this great monster, to whom corporeal warmth is as indispensable as it is to man, should be found at home, immersed to his lips for life in those Arctic waters! where seamen fallen overboard have been found, months afterwards, frozen into the fields of ice, as a fly is found glued in amber. More surprising still, the blood of a Polar whale is warmer than that of a Borneo negro.

It does seem to me, that herein we see the rare virtue of a strong individual vitality, of thick walls, of interior spaciousness. Oh, man! admire and model thyself after the whale! Do thou, too, remain warm among ice. Do thou, too, live in this world without being of it. Be cool at the equator; keep thy blood fluid at the Pole. Like the great dome of St. Peter’s, and like the great whale, retain, O man! in all seasons a temperature of thine own.

But how easy and how hopeless to teach these fine things! Of erections, how few are domed like St. Peter’s! of creatures, how few vast as the whale!

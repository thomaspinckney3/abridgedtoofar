It was when curiosity about Gatsby was at its highest that the lights in his house failed to go on one Saturday night—and, as obscurely as it had begun, his career as Trimalchio was over. An unfamiliar butler slammed the door in my face; my Finn told me Gatsby had dismissed all his servants and replaced them with people who wouldn’t gossip. Next day he called.

“I wanted somebody who wouldn’t gossip. Daisy comes over quite often—in the afternoons.”

So the whole caravansary had fallen in like a card house at the disapproval in her eyes. He asked me to lunch at her house; Daisy, calling separately, sounded relieved that I was coming.

The next day was broiling, almost the last, certainly the warmest, of the summer. Through the hall of the Buchanans’ house blew a faint wind, carrying the sound of the telephone bell out to Gatsby and me. In this heat every extra gesture was an affront to the common store of life. Daisy and Jordan lay upon an enormous couch, like silver idols; Tom’s gruff voice carried from the hall telephone. Gatsby stood in the centre of the crimson carpet, gazing around with fascinated eyes. Daisy laughed—her sweet, exciting laugh.

The voice in the hall rose high with annoyance—car deals and lunch-time affronts—then Tom came in, shook Gatsby’s hand with well-concealed dislike, and went to make drinks. As he left, Daisy pulled Gatsby’s face down and kissed him. Jordan said, “You forget there’s a lady present,” and Daisy only laughed again.

We drank nervous gaiety with the cold ale. “What’ll we do with ourselves this afternoon,” cried Daisy, “and the day after that, and the next thirty years?” The heat pressed down; “Let’s all go to town!”

At table, her eyes went to Gatsby. “Ah,” she cried, “you look so cool.” Their eyes met and held. “You always look so cool,” she repeated. She had told him she loved him, and Tom Buchanan saw.

Tom’s temper cracked. “All right—we’re all going to town.”

On the drive, as he wrapped a towel round a bottle and Daisy and Jordan came down in tight little metallic hats, Gatsby said with sudden illumination, “Her voice is full of money.” That was it—the inexhaustible charm that rose and fell in it, the jingle of it, the cymbals’ song of it … High in a white palace the king’s daughter, the golden girl …

Tom insisted on driving Gatsby’s big yellow car; Daisy told him to take me and Jordan and said she and Gatsby would follow in the coupé. We shot off into the oppressive heat.

Down the road, under the giant, fading eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg, Tom stopped impatiently for gas at Wilson’s garage. Wilson, green in the sunlight, said he and his wife were going West. “I’m going to get her away.” In the upper window a curtain stirred; Myrtle Wilson peered down, her eyes wide with jealous terror, fixed not on Tom but on Jordan, whom she took for his wife. Tom paid, tight-shouldered, and we went on.

We all took the less explicable step of engaging the parlour of a suite in the Plaza Hotel. The room was large and stifling; opening the windows admitted only a gust of hot shrubbery from the Park. A telephone book slipped and splashed to the floor. Someone below played Mendelssohn’s Wedding March; Daisy remembered a June ceremony and stray names, and, for a moment, there was the flimsy comedy with which people shield themselves from what approaches.

Tom picked at Gatsby’s “old sport,” at his Oxford claim; Gatsby, steady, said he had been there five months in 1919—an officers’ chance after the armistice. “Wait a minute,” said Tom. “I want to ask Mr. Gatsby one more question.”

“What kind of a row are you trying to cause in my house anyhow?”

“They were out in the open at last and Gatsby was content.” Daisy begged for self-control; Tom swelled with a last barrier of civilization—“Nowadays people begin by sneering at family life and family institutions”—and stood flushed in his impassioned gibberish. He sneered at West Egg and pigsties; the transition from libertine to prig was complete.

“I’ve got something to tell you, old sport—” began Gatsby.

“Please don’t!” cried Daisy helplessly.

Tom: “I want to know what Mr. Gatsby has to tell me.”

“Your wife doesn’t love you,” said Gatsby. “She’s never loved you. She loves me.”

Gatsby sprang to his feet, vivid with excitement. “She only married you because I was poor and she was tired of waiting for me. It was a terrible mistake, but in her heart she never loved anyone except me!”

Tom tapped his fingers like a clergyman. “You’re crazy! Daisy loved me when she married me and she loves me now. Once in a while I go off on a spree and make a fool of myself, but I always come back, and in my heart I love her all the time.”

“You’re revolting,” said Daisy. Gatsby stood beside her. “Daisy, that’s all over now,” he said earnestly. “Just tell him the truth—that you never loved him—and it’s all wiped out forever.”

“I never loved him,” she said—then, to Tom’s soft recall of places and days, the steadiness went out of her. Her hand shook at a match; suddenly she flung it away.

“Oh, you want too much!” she cried to Gatsby. “I love you now—isn’t that enough? I can’t help what’s past.” She began to sob helplessly. “I did love him once—but I loved you too.”

Gatsby’s eyes opened and closed. “You loved me too?” he repeated.

Tom pressed the advantage. He had “made a little investigation”—Gatsby’s “drugstores” selling grain alcohol in side streets. “I picked him for a bootlegger the first time I saw him.” The unfamiliar yet recognizable look shadowed Gatsby’s face. He began to talk excitedly to Daisy, denying and defending; with every word she drew further into herself. Only the dead dream fought on as the afternoon slipped away, struggling unhappily toward that lost voice across the room.

“Please, Tom! I can’t stand this any more,” Daisy’s voice begged. Tom, with magnanimous scorn, said, “You two start on home, Daisy—in Mr. Gatsby’s car.” They went, snapped out like ghosts even from our pity.

Tom wrapped the unopened bottle again. “Want any of this stuff? … Nick?” “No … I just remembered that today’s my birthday.” I was thirty.

It was seven o’clock when we started for Long Island. Tom talked incessantly, exulting and laughing, his voice remote from us as foreign clamour. Human sympathy has its limits. Thirty—the promise of a decade of loneliness, thinning enthusiasms. Jordan’s wan face fell lazily against my shoulder and the formidable stroke of thirty died with the reassuring pressure of her hand.

So we drove on toward death through the cooling twilight.

The young Greek, Michaelis, who ran the coffee joint beside the ash-heaps, later told how, at dusk, Myrtle Wilson rushed out; the “death car,” light-coloured in the failing light, wavered and did not stop. When they tore open her shirtwaist there was no need to listen for the heart beneath.

We saw the crowd before we reached the garage. A hollow, wailing sound issued from within—“Oh, my Ga-od! Oh, my Ga-od!”—Wilson swaying in the office doorway, gripping the jambs. Tom thrust through; Myrtle’s body lay on a worktable, wrapped and wrapped again as if she were cold in the hot night. A well-dressed negro said it was a big yellow car. Wilson cried he knew what kind of car it was. Tom seized his arms: “You’ve got to pull yourself together.” He told the policeman the yellow car wasn’t his—he had just brought Wilson another car from New York. He carried Wilson into the office and shut the door. When we were beyond the bend Tom’s face broke; tears overflowed. “The God damned coward! He didn’t even stop his car.”

The Buchanans’ house floated toward us through rustling trees. Tom looked up at the two lighted windows. “Daisy’s home.” He ordered a taxi for me; I wanted to be alone and turned down Jordan’s soft invitation to come in.

I had not gone twenty yards down the drive when Gatsby stepped from the bushes, the moon whitening his pink suit. “Just standing here, old sport.” He asked, after a pause, “Was she killed?” When I said yes, he said he had told Daisy he thought so and that she had borne it pretty well. He spoke as if Daisy’s reaction were the only thing that mattered.

I guessed at the truth. “Was Daisy driving?”

“Yes,” he said after a moment, “but of course I’ll say I was. When we left New York she was very nervous and she thought it would steady her to drive—and this woman rushed out as we were passing another car. Daisy turned away, then lost her nerve and turned back. The second my hand reached the wheel I felt the shock.” He winced at what I told him of the violence. “Anyhow—Daisy stepped on it. I tried to make her stop; she fell over into my lap and I drove on.”

He meant to wait there all night if necessary. “She’s locked herself into her room, and if he tries any brutality she’s going to turn the light out and on again.”

“He won’t touch her,” I said. “He’s not thinking about her.”

Still he didn’t trust Tom.

“Wait here,” I told him, and crossed the gravel softly. Through a rift in the pantry blind I saw Daisy and Tom at the kitchen table, a plate of cold fried chicken between them and two bottles of ale. He was talking intently; in his earnestness his hand had fallen upon and covered hers. They weren’t happy; and yet they weren’t unhappy either. There was an unmistakable air of natural intimacy; anybody would have said they were conspiring together.

“It’s all quiet,” I told Gatsby, when the taxi nosed up the dark road. “You’d better come home and get some sleep.”

He shook his head. “I want to wait here till Daisy goes to bed. Good night, old sport.” He put his hands in his coat pockets and turned back eagerly to his scrutiny of the house, as though my presence marred the sacredness of the vigil. I walked away and left him standing there in the moonlight—watching over nothing.

Nick Carraway, freshly returned from the Great War with a resolve to suspend judgment, leaves the Midwest in 1922 to learn the bond business and rents a shabby cottage in West Egg, Long Island, beside a colossal mansion owned by a man named Jay Gatsby. Across the bay, in old-money East Egg, live his cousin Daisy and her domineering, athletic husband Tom Buchanan, whose drawing-room grace thinly veils brutish arrogance and the clang of a telephone that announces his infidelity. Nick meets the coolly self-possessed golfer Jordan Baker at their dinner, and together they drift through the parties and porches of a summer gilded by wealth. Between the Eggs and New York lies a grey industrial wasteland—“the valley of ashes”—over which the billboard eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg brood, and where Tom keeps his mistress, Myrtle Wilson, the restless wife of a spiritless garage owner. Dragged to a tawdry Manhattan gathering with Myrtle, Nick sees how appetite and pretension mingle beneath the city’s sheen. That night he glimpses his mysterious neighbor alone on the lawn, arms outstretched toward a single green light across the water.

All summer music floats from Gatsby’s castle as uninvited crowds swarm his dazzling parties, where gossip makes a legend of the invisible host. Personally invited at last, Nick finds Gatsby an elegant, earnest young man with a rehearsed charm, while a gambler named Meyer Wolfshiem hints that Gatsby’s money may not be clean. Jordan confides that Gatsby and Daisy had fallen in love in Louisville before the war, and that he bought his mansion solely to be across the bay from her green light. Gatsby begs Nick to arrange a tea; the reunion, awkward at first in Nick’s cottage, blooms into a rapture that Gatsby carries into a tour of silks, rooms, and a storm of beautiful shirts. For a moment the dream seems realized, even as the green light’s power fades now that the object is within reach. Nick senses both Gatsby’s incorruptible capacity for hope and the shaky foundations of the dream he has built. Beneath the polish, he learns that Gatsby began life as James Gatz, a poor North Dakotan who remade himself under the tutelage of the wealthy drifter Dan Cody, grafting boundless ambition to an almost religious ideal of self-creation.

Gatsby convinces himself the past can be restored if Daisy will say she never loved Tom, while Nick—entangled in a brittle romance with the charmingly dishonest Jordan—warns that the past cannot be repeated. A chance visit by Tom and a later party at Gatsby’s house expose the gulf between West Egg spectacle and East Egg disdain; Daisy is unsettled rather than transported. On the summer’s hottest day Tom, sensing danger, forces a showdown after an uneasy luncheon at his own house. They all decamp to a suite at the Plaza Hotel, where Gatsby demands Daisy renounce her marriage as Tom retaliates by unmasking Gatsby’s bootlegging fortune and shadowy partners. Daisy falters, admitting she once loved Tom as well, and the incandescent dream that had sustained Gatsby begins to flicker.

The party breaks in bitterness, and on the drive back through the valley of ashes Daisy—at the wheel of Gatsby’s yellow car—strikes and kills Myrtle Wilson, who had rushed into the road, desperate and mistaken. Gatsby vows to take the blame and keeps a vigil outside Daisy’s window, while Tom gathers her back into the protection of money and marriage. By morning Tom has pointed the grief-crazed George Wilson to the yellow car’s owner; Wilson finds Gatsby at his mansion, shoots him as he floats on a mattress in his pool, and then turns the gun on himself. The newspapers spin lurid fantasies, but the truth is simpler and sadder: a dreamer destroyed by another’s grief and by a world where wealth insulates the guilty and disposes of the rest. The ash-gray eyes and the green light survive only as symbols in Nick’s mind of seeing without understanding and of desire forever postponed.

Nick sets about finding mourners and discovers how shallow Gatsby’s vast acquaintance was; Daisy and Tom have vanished without a forwarding address, and Wolfsheim refuses to “get mixed up” in death. Only an “owl-eyed” partygoer and Gatsby’s bewildered, proud father—clutching a boyhood schedule of self-improvement from Hopalong Cassidy—attend the rain-soaked funeral. Sickened, Nick breaks with Jordan, then encounters Tom and sees him plainly as one of the careless people who smash things and creatures and retreat into their money and indifference. He resolves to leave the East, now haunted and distorted, and return to the steadier horizons of the Midwest. Sitting one last time on the shore, he thinks of the Dutch sailors who first saw the fresh green breast of the New World, and of Gatsby reaching across the dark for a light that always receded. The story becomes an elegy for an American Dream of self-invention and boundless promise, corrupted by class, money, and the fatal faith that the past can be recovered, even by the purest hope. So we beat on, he concludes, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past.

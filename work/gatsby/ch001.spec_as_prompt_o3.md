“In my younger and more vulnerable years my father gave me some advice that I’ve been turning over in my mind ever since.

‘Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone,’ he told me, ‘just remember that all the people in this world haven’t had the advantages that you’ve had.’”

From this I formed the habit of reserving judgments, a habit that opened curious natures to me—none more curious than Gatsby, whose name is on this book. If personality is an unbroken series of successful gestures, then there was something gorgeous about him, some heightened sensitivity to the promises of life, an extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness such as I have never found in any other person. No—Gatsby turned out all right at the end; it was what preyed on him, what foul dust floated in the wake of his dreams, that closed my interest in the abortive sorrows of men.

My family, prominent Midwesterners, claim descent from the Dukes of Buccleuch. I graduated from New Haven in 1915, fought in the Great War, and, restless afterward, came East in 1922 to learn the bond business. A chance suggestion lodged me in a weather-beaten bungalow on West Egg, the less fashionable of two identical promontories that jut into Long Island Sound. Next door, on forty acres of lawn and marble swimming pool, stood Gatsby’s spectacular imitation of a Normandy Hôtel de Ville. My own house was a small overlooked eyesore, but it gave me a view of the water, of millionaires across the bay in East Egg, and of Gatsby’s lawns gleaming through the night.

The summer began the evening I drove to East Egg to dine with Tom and Daisy Buchanan. Daisy was my second cousin once removed; I had known Tom at Yale, where his enormous wealth and physical dominance were already notorious. Their red-and-white Georgian mansion opened to a warm wind; curtains whipped like pale flags until Tom shut them with a boom.

On a giant couch two women floated as though upon an anchored balloon—Daisy, laughing “I’m p-paralysed with happiness,” and a stranger she murmured to be Miss Baker. Daisy’s low, thrilling voice drifted like music; her face was sad and lovely with bright things in it. Miss Baker lay motionless, balancing her chin as though on some invisible object.

Tom prowled restlessly. “What you doing, Nick?”

“I’m a bond man.”

“Never heard of them,” he said decisively.

At Tom’s question Miss Baker spoke her first word: “Absolutely!” She rose, slender and erect, grey eyes polite and wan. Presently she remarked, “You live in West Egg. You must know Gatsby.”

“Gatsby?” demanded Daisy. “What Gatsby?”

Dinner was served on a rosy porch where candles flickered in the wind. Daisy snapped them out: “Do you always watch for the longest day in the year and then miss it?” Conversation slid from idle banter to Tom’s sudden outburst: “Civilization’s going to pieces. Have you read The Rise of the Coloured Empires by this man Goddard? It’s been proved the white race will be submerged.” Daisy winked at me: “We’ve got to beat them down,” she whispered.

A telephone rang; the butler whispered to Tom, who left the table. Miss Baker leaned forward. “Tom’s got some woman in New York. She might have the decency not to telephone at dinner time.” Tom’s return dissolved the incident; Daisy, tense and laughing, spoke of a nightingale on the lawn.

Later, in veranda dusk, Daisy touched her cynicism. “When my daughter was born and Tom was God knows where, I said, ‘I hope she’ll be a fool—that’s the best thing a girl can be in this world, a beautiful little fool.’ ” She laughed with thrilling scorn. “Sophisticated—God, I’m sophisticated!”

Inside, lamplight bloomed. Miss Baker—Jordan, the golfer whose photograph I had seen—rose, saying, “Ten o’clock. Time for this good girl to go to bed.” Daisy promised to fling us together; Tom muttered that her family oughtn’t to let her run around.

At the door Daisy and Tom challenged a rumor that I was engaged out West. “We heard it from three people, so it must be true.” I denied it; their interest touched and faintly disgusted me.

Driving home through the bright summer night I felt the earth’s full bellows blow the frogs full of life. Reaching my yard I saw a figure emerge from Gatsby’s mansion. Hands in pockets, he regarded the silver pepper of the stars. I half-called to him, but he seemed content to be alone. Suddenly he stretched his arms toward the dark water, toward a single green light, minute and far away, at the end of a dock. When I looked back he had vanished, and I was alone again in the unquiet darkness.

## PROLOGUE

It was 2 p.m. on the afternoon of May 7, 1915. The *Lusitania*, struck by two torpedoes in succession, was sinking rapidly, while the boats were being launched with all possible speed. Women and children were being lined up awaiting their turn. One girl stood slightly apart from the rest. She was quite young, not more than eighteen. She did not seem afraid, and her grave, steadfast eyes looked straight ahead.

"I beg your pardon."

A man's voice beside her made her start. She had noticed him more than once amongst the first-class passengers—a hint of mystery about him, a nervous way of looking over his shoulder with a swift, suspicious glance. Now beads of perspiration stood on his brow. He was evidently in a state of overmastering fear, and yet he did not strike her as the kind of man who would be afraid to meet death.

"Yes?"

He stood looking at her with desperate irresolution. "It must be!" he muttered. "Yes—it is the only way." Then aloud: "You are an American?"

"Yes."

"A patriotic one?"

She flushed. "Of course I am!"

"Don't be offended. But I've got to trust some one—and it must be a woman. Because of 'women and children first.'" He lowered his voice. "I'm carrying papers—vitally important papers. They may make all the difference to the Allies in the war. They've more chance with you than with me. Will you take them?"

The girl held out her hand.

"There may be a risk—if I've been followed. Have you the nerve to go through with it?"

She smiled. "I'll go through with it all right. And I'm real proud to be chosen! What am I to do with them afterwards?"

"Watch the newspapers! I'll advertise in the personal column of the *Times*, beginning 'Shipmate.' At the end of three days if there's nothing—you'll know I'm down and out. Then take the packet to the American Embassy, and deliver it into the Ambassador's own hands."

"Quite clear."

"Then good-bye." He took her hand. "Good-bye. Good luck to you," he said in a louder tone.

Her hand closed on the oilskin packet that had lain in his palm. The *Lusitania* settled with a more decided list to starboard. In answer to a quick command, the girl went forward to take her place in the boat.

## CHAPTER I. THE YOUNG ADVENTURERS, LTD.

"Tommy, old thing!"

"Tuppence, old bean!"

The two young people greeted each other affectionately, momentarily blocking the Dover Street Tube exit. The adjective "old" was misleading: their united ages would certainly not have totalled forty-five.

"Not seen you for simply centuries," said the young man. "Come and chew a bun with me."

The girl assented, and they walked down Dover Street towards Piccadilly. The very faint anxiety underlying his tone did not escape the astute ears of Miss Prudence Cowley, known to her intimate friends for some mysterious reason as "Tuppence."

"Tommy, you're stony!"

"Not a bit of it. Rolling in cash."

"You always were a shocking liar," said Tuppence severely, "though you did once persuade Sister Greenbank that the doctor had ordered you beer as a tonic. Do you remember?"

Tommy chuckled. "Good old hospital—demobbed like everything else, I suppose?"

Tuppence sighed. "Yes. You too?" Tommy nodded. "Two months ago."

"Gratuity?" hinted Tuppence.

"Spent. The cost of living—"

"My dear child," interrupted Tuppence, "there is nothing I do *not* know about the cost of living. Here we are at Lyons', and we will each pay for our own."

They found a table. "Funny scraps one does overhear," murmured Tommy. "I passed two Johnnies in the street to-day talking about some one called Jane Finn. Did you ever hear such a name?"

Tommy was pleasantly ugly, with a shock of slicked-back red hair—nondescript, yet unmistakably the face of a gentleman and a sportsman. Tuppence had no claim to beauty, but there was character and charm in the elfin lines of her little face, with its determined chin and large, wide-apart grey eyes. She wore a small bright green toque over her bobbed black hair; her appearance was a valiant attempt at smartness.

"Now then," said Tommy, taking a large bite of bun. "Let's get up-to-date. I haven't seen you since that time in hospital in 1916."

"Very well." Tuppence helped herself liberally to buttered toast and gave the abridged biography of Miss Prudence Cowley, fifth daughter of Archdeacon Cowley of Little Missendell, Suffolk: she had entered an officers' hospital, where her duties ran from plate-washing and peeling potatoes to sweeping out wards (where she found a friend of her childhood in Lieutenant Thomas Beresford—bow, Tommy!), then a trade van, a motor-lorry, and a young general. "After that, a Government office, and several enjoyable tea parties—until I was combed out at last. Since then I've been looking for a job. Now—your turn."

"There's not so much promotion in mine," said Tommy regretfully, "and a great deal less variety. France, Mesopotamia, wounded again, hospital out there, Egypt till the Armistice, finally demobbed. For ten long, weary months I've been job hunting. There aren't any jobs. And, if there were, they wouldn't give 'em to me. What good am I?"

"Rich relations?" asked Tuppence.

"I've got an old uncle who's more or less rolling, but he's no good."

"Why not?" she pressed.

"Wanted to adopt me once. I refused—because of my mother. You see, he hated her—wanted to get me away from her."

"Your mother's dead, isn't she?" said Tuppence gently.

Tommy nodded.

"You're a good sort, Tommy," said Tuppence. Her grey eyes looked misty. "I always knew it."

"Rot! Well, that's my position. I'm just about desperate."

"So am I. I shall have to go home. Father's a dear, but you've no idea how I worry him—short skirts and smoking are immoral, in his early Victorian view. He just heaved a sigh of relief when the war took me off. Seven of us at home. All housework and mothers' meetings!"

She burst out: "Money, money, money! I think about money morning, noon and night. There are only three ways to get it: to be left it, to marry it, or to make it. The first is ruled out—my relatives are in homes for decayed gentlewomen. Marriage is my best chance. I made up my mind to marry money when I was quite young. Any thinking girl would. I'm not sentimental, you know."

"No one would ever think of sentiment in connection with you," said Tommy hastily.

"That's not very polite. Well, there it is—I'm ready and willing, but I never meet any rich men. All the boys I know are about as hard up as I am."

"Same here."

"We've tried the orthodox ways. Suppose we try the unorthodox. Tommy, let's be adventurers!"

"Certainly. How do we begin?"

"If we could make ourselves known, people might hire us to commit crimes for them."

"Delightful—coming from a clergyman's daughter!"

"The moral guilt would be theirs—not mine. Let's form a—joint venture! It struck me as such a romantic phrase. It's got an Elizabethan flavour about it—galleons and doubloons."

"Trading under the name of the Young Adventurers, Ltd.? Is that your idea?"

"Don't laugh. Advertisement! Have you got paper and pencil?"

Tommy handed over a rather shabby green notebook, and Tuppence wrote busily.

"How's this? 'Two young adventurers for hire. Willing to do anything, go anywhere. Pay must be good. No unreasonable offer refused.'"

"It would strike me as either a hoax or written by a lunatic."

"It's not half so insane as a thing I read this morning beginning 'Petunia' and signed 'Best Boy.'" She tore out the leaf and handed it to him. "There you are. *Times*, I think."

Tommy was holding the paper thoughtfully. His face burned a deeper red. "Shall we really try it, Tuppence? Just for the fun of the thing?"

"Tommy, you're a sport! Let's drink to success." She poured cold dregs of tea into the two cups. "Here's to our joint venture, and may it prosper!"

"The Young Adventurers, Ltd.!" responded Tommy.

They put down the cups and laughed rather uncertainly. Tuppence rose. "I must return to my palatial suite at the hostel."

"Perhaps it is time I strolled round to the *Ritz*. Where shall we meet?"

"Twelve o'clock to-morrow. Piccadilly Tube station."

"My time is my own," replied Mr. Beresford magnificently.

The two young people went off in opposite directions. Tuppence's hostel was situated in what was charitably called Southern Belgravia. For reasons of economy she did not take a bus.

She was half-way across St. James's Park when a man's voice behind her made her start.

"Excuse me. But may I speak to you for a moment?"

## CHAPTER II. MR. WHITTINGTON’S OFFER

Tuppence turned sharply, but the words hovering on the tip of her tongue remained unspoken; the man's appearance and manner did not bear out her first natural assumption. He was a big man, clean-shaven, with a heavy jowl. His eyes were small and cunning, and shifted their glance under her direct gaze.

"I can assure you I mean no disrespect," he said. "I happened to overhear part of your conversation with the young gentleman in Lyons'. I think I may be of some use to you."

"You followed me here?"

"I took that liberty."

He took a card from his pocket and handed it to her with a bow. It bore the inscription, "Mr. Edward Whittington," and below it, "Esthonia Glassware Co."

"If you will call upon me to-morrow morning at eleven o'clock, I will lay the details of my proposition before you."

"Very well. I'll be there."

He raised his hat with a flourish and walked away. Tuppence gazed after him, then gave a curious movement of her shoulders, rather as a terrier shakes himself.

"The adventures have begun," she murmured. "There's something about you, Mr. Whittington, that I don't like at all. But little Tuppence can look after herself, thank you!"

She turned aside into a post office. The thought of a possible five shillings spent unnecessarily on the advertisement spurred her to risk the waste of ninepence. She wrote rapidly: "Don't put in advertisement. Will explain to-morrow." She addressed it to Tommy at his club, from which in one short month he would have to resign unless a kindly fortune intervened.

That night, in her tiny cubicle at the top of the house, she dreamed that Mr. Whittington had set her to washing up a pile of Esthonia Glassware, which bore an unaccountable resemblance to hospital plates.

On the stroke of eleven the next morning she plunged into the recesses of the building. The Esthonia Glassware Co. was on the top floor. A middle-aged clerk admitted her at the partition door marked "Private."

Mr. Whittington was seated behind a large desk covered with papers. The combination of his sleek prosperity and his shifty eye was not attractive.

"So you've turned up. Sit down."

Tuppence sat with downcast eyes whilst he sorted his papers. Finally he leaned over the desk.

"Now, my dear young lady, let us come to business. You want work? What should you say to £100 down, and all expenses paid?"

"And the nature of the work?"

"Nominal—purely nominal. A pleasant trip."

"Where to?"

"Paris."

"Oh!" said Tuppence thoughtfully.

"What could be more delightful? To re-enter one of those charming *pensionnats de jeunes filles*—Madame Colombier's, in the Avenue de Neuilly."

Tuppence knew the name well. Nothing could have been more select. "For how long?"

"Possibly three months."

"And no other conditions?"

"None whatever. You would, of course, go in the character of my ward, and hold no communication with your friends. Absolute secrecy. By the way, you are English, are you not?"

"Yes."

"Yet you speak with a slight American accent?"

"My great pal in hospital was a little American girl."

"It might be simpler for you to pass as an American. Yes, decidedly better."

"One moment, Mr. Whittington! You seem to be taking my consent for granted."

"Surely you are not thinking of refusing? The terms are most liberal."

"Almost too liberal. I cannot see how I can be worth that amount."

"I will tell you. I could obtain some one else for very much less. What I am willing to pay for is a young lady with sufficient intelligence and presence of mind to sustain her part well, and sufficient discretion not to ask too many questions."

"There's another thing. So far there has been no mention of Mr. Beresford. Where does he come in?"

"I'm afraid we shan't require his services."

"Then it's off!" Tuppence rose. "It's both or neither. Good morning, Mr. Whittington."

"Wait. Sit down again, Miss—"

Tuppence's conscience gave her a passing twinge as she remembered the archdeacon. She seized hurriedly on the first name that came into her head.

"Jane Finn," she said hastily—and then paused open-mouthed at the effect of those two simple words.

All the geniality faded out of Whittington's face. It was purple with rage; the veins stood out on his forehead. Behind it there lurked a sort of incredulous dismay. He leaned forward and hissed savagely:

"So that's your little game, is it? Been playing with me, all the time, like a cat and mouse? Knew what I wanted you for, but kept up the comedy. Who's been blabbing? Rita?"

Tuppence shook her head. Doubtful how long she could sustain the illusion, she realized the importance of not dragging an unknown Rita into it. "Rita knows nothing about me."

"How much do you know?" he shot out.

"Very little indeed," answered Tuppence, pleased to note that his uneasiness was augmented rather than allayed.

"Anyway, you knew enough to come in here and plump out that name."

"It might be my own name."

"Likely, with two girls answering a name like that?"

"Or I might have hit upon it by chance."

He brought his fist down upon the desk with a bang. "Quit fooling! How much do you know? And how much do you want?"

The last five words took Tuppence's fancy mightily, especially after a meagre breakfast and a supper of buns the night before. She smiled with the air of one who has the situation thoroughly well in hand.

"My dear Mr. Whittington, let us by all means lay our cards upon the table. You heard me say yesterday that I proposed to live by my wits. I admit knowledge of a certain name—but perhaps my knowledge ends there."

"Yes—and perhaps it doesn't."

"Suppose we say a little something down, and a fuller discussion of the matter later?"

"Blackmail, eh?"

"Oh no! Payment of services in advance. You see, I'm so very fond of money."

"You're about the limit," he growled, with a sort of unwilling admiration. "Took me in all right. Thought you were quite a meek little kid with just enough brains for my purpose."

A discreet knock; the clerk laid a paper at his master's elbow. "Telephone message just come for you, sir."

Whittington snatched it up and read it. A frown gathered on his brow. "That'll do, Brown."

He turned to Tuppence. "Come to-morrow at the same time. I'm busy now. Here's fifty to go on with." He pushed some notes across the table.

The girl counted them in a businesslike manner, secured them in her handbag, and rose.

"Good morning, Mr. Whittington. At least, au revoir, I should say."

"Au revoir, my clever and charming young lady."

A wild elation possessed her. The clock showed five minutes to twelve. "Let's give Tommy a surprise!" she murmured, and hailed a taxi.

The cab drew up outside the tube station. Tommy hurried forward to assist her to alight. She smiled at him affectionately and remarked in a slightly affected voice:

"Pay the thing, will you, old bean? I've got nothing smaller than a five-pound note!"

## CHAPTER III. A SET BACK

The moment was not quite so triumphant as it ought to have been. Tommy's pockets were limited, and only after the lady recollected a plebeian twopence was the fare managed; the driver moved off with one last hoarse demand as to what the gentleman thought he was giving him.

"I think you've given him too much, Tommy," said Tuppence innocently. "I fancy he wants to give some of it back."

"What the—dickens, did you want to take a taxi for?"

"I was afraid I might be late. And really and truly, I haven't got anything smaller than a five-pound note."

"You did that part of it very well, old bean, but the fellow wasn't taken in—not for a moment!"

"That's the curious part about speaking the truth. No one believes it. Now let's go to lunch. How about the *Savoy*?"

"Is this a new brand of humour? Or is your brain really unhinged?"

"I have come into money, and the shock has been too much for me!" Tuppence wrenched open her bag. "Look here, and here, and here!"

"Great Jehosaphat! My dear girl, don't wave Fishers aloft like that!"

"They're not Fishers. They're five times better than Fishers, and this one's ten times better!"

Over lunch at the *Piccadilly*, surrounded by the many *hors d'œuvre* of Tuppence's dreams, Miss Cowley told him the whole story.

"And the curious part of it is," she ended, "I really did invent the name of Jane Finn!"

"You didn't invent it. *I* told it to you. I said yesterday I'd overheard two people talking about a female called Jane Finn. That's what brought the name into your mind so pat."

"How extraordinary—Tommy, what were they like, the two men you passed?"

"One was a big fat sort of chap. Clean shaven, and dark."

"That's him!" cried Tuppence, in an ungrammatical squeal. "That's Whittington!"

But Tommy had become serious. "Look here, old girl, what is this going to lead to? You can't bluff him forever. He'll want to know how much *you* know, and where you got your information from. What are you going to do?"

Tuppence frowned severely. "I've got a plan. Obviously what we've got to do is find out more about it all. We can only find out through Whittington—we must sleuth him. I can't, because he knows me. But he only saw you for a minute or two in Lyons'. After all, one young man is much like another."

"I repudiate that remark utterly."

"I'll go alone to-morrow and put him off again. You'll hang about outside. When he comes out of the building I'll drop a handkerchief, and off you go."

"Anyway, it will be rather a lark."

The day passed pleasantly; the evening even more so. Two of the five-pound notes were now irretrievably dead.

They met by arrangement the following morning. Tommy remained on the opposite side of the road while Tuppence plunged into the building. Just as he came abreast of it again, she darted across.

"The place is shut. I can't make anyone hear."

They tried again. On the third floor landing a young clerk emerged.

"Were you wanting the Esthonia Glassware? It's closed down. Since yesterday afternoon. Company being wound up. The office is to let."

"I suppose you don't know Mr. Whittington's address?"

"Afraid I don't. They left rather suddenly."

In the street they gazed at one another blankly.

"That's torn it," said Tommy at length.

"And I never suspected it!" wailed Tuppence.

"Cheer up. It can't be helped."

"Can't it, though!" Tuppence's little chin shot out defiantly. "If they are scared enough to run away like this, it shows there must be a lot in this Jane Finn business! We'll get to the bottom of it. We'll be sleuths in earnest!"

"But there's no one left to sleuth."

"That's why we'll have to start all over again. Lend me that bit of pencil."

She handed him back a slip of paper. Tommy read the words on it aloud:

"WANTED, any information respecting Jane Finn. Apply Y. A."

## CHAPTER IV. WHO IS JANE FINN?

The day of disillusionment had been a Wednesday. On Friday, Tommy met Tuppence at the National Gallery with two answers to the advertisement.

"Tommy, you devil! Give them to me."

The first was from "A. Carter," 27 Carshalton Gardens, requesting a call at eleven the next morning; the second, from the *Ritz*, signed "Julius P. Hersheimmer," asked them round at lunch-time.

"A hundred pounds instead of fifty!" said Tommy. "Do I smell a Boche? Or only an American millionaire of unfortunate ancestry? At all events we'll call at lunch-time."

"Now for Carter."

Carshalton Terrace proved an unimpeachable row of ladylike-looking houses. A neat maid showed them into a small study, where presently a tall man with a lean hawklike face and a tired manner entered.

"Mr. Y. A.?" he said, and smiled. His smile was distinctly attractive. "Do sit down, both of you."

He took a chair opposite Tuppence. "What do *you* know about Jane Finn?"

"I don't see that that's got anything to do with it."

"No? But it has, you know." He leaned forward, his weary voice held a hint of persuasiveness. "Suppose you tell me…."

To Tuppence's surprise, Tommy spoke first, with an unusual note of deference. "I dare say the little we know won't be any good to you, sir. But you're welcome to it."

"Tommy!"

Mr. Carter's eyes asked a question. Tommy nodded. "I recognized you at once, sir. Saw you in France when I was with the Intelligence."

"No names, please. I'm known as Mr. Carter here. It's my cousin's house, by the way—she's willing to lend it when it's a case of working on strictly unofficial lines. Well now—who's going to tell me the story?"

"Fire ahead, Tuppence. It's your yarn."

Obediently Tuppence told the whole tale, from the forming of the Young Adventurers, Ltd. downwards. Mr. Carter listened in silence. When she had finished he nodded gravely.

"Not much. But suggestive. Quite suggestive. I don't know—you might succeed where others have failed. I believe in luck. How would you like to work for me? All quite unofficial. Expenses paid, and a moderate screw."

"What should we have to do?" Tuppence breathed.

"Just go on with what you're doing now. *Find Jane Finn.*"

"Yes, but—who *is* Jane Finn?"

He leaned back, brought the tips of his fingers together, and began in a low monotone:

"In the early days of 1915 a draft secret treaty was drawn up in America—then a neutral country—and dispatched to England by a special messenger, a young fellow called Danvers. He sailed on the *Lusitania*, carrying the papers in an oilskin packet next his skin. The *Lusitania* was torpedoed. Danvers's body was washed ashore, identified beyond doubt. But the oilskin packet was missing.

"During the launching of the boats, Danvers was seen speaking to a young American girl. No one actually saw him pass anything to her, but he might have done so. It seems likely he entrusted the papers to her, believing that she, as a woman, had a greater chance of bringing them safely to shore. Her name was Jane Finn, and it duly appeared among the list of survivors—but the girl herself seemed to have vanished completely. She was an orphan, a pupil teacher in a small school out West, going to join the staff of a Paris hospital. We tracked her across Ireland, but nothing could be heard of her after she set foot in England. No use was made of the draft treaty, and we concluded that Danvers had destroyed it. The whole affair was lost in oblivion."

"But why has it all cropped up again? The war's over."

"Because it seems the papers were *not* destroyed, and might be resurrected to-day with a new and deadly significance. Five years ago, that draft treaty was a weapon in our hands; to-day it is a weapon against us. If its terms were made public, it would mean disaster. It might possibly bring about another war—not with Germany this time. That document undoubtedly implicates a number of our statesmen whom we cannot afford to have discredited.

"You may have heard that there is Bolshevist influence at work behind the present Labour unrest? That is the truth. Bolshevist gold is pouring into this country for the specific purpose of procuring a Revolution. The Bolshevists are behind the Labour unrest—but a certain man is *behind the Bolshevists*. He is always spoken of by the unassuming title of 'Mr. Brown.' One thing is certain: he is the master criminal of this age."

"A naturalized German?" asked Tommy.

"On the contrary, I have every reason to believe he is an Englishman. We have no clue as to his real personality. Where we have come across his tracks, he has always played a secondary part—some nonentity, a servant or a clerk, in the background unnoticed. Afterwards we always find that the elusive Mr. Brown has escaped us once more."

"Oh!" Tuppence jumped. "I remember—in Mr. Whittington's office, the clerk—he called him Brown. You don't think—"

Carter nodded thoughtfully. "Very likely. A curious point is that the name is usually mentioned. An idiosyncrasy of genius. Can you describe him at all?"

"I really didn't notice. He was quite ordinary—just like anyone else."

"That is the invariable description of Mr. Brown. He brought a telephone message to Whittington, did he? Notice a telephone in the outer office?"

"No, I don't think I did."

"Exactly. That 'message' was Mr. Brown's way of giving an order to his subordinate. He overheard the whole conversation. Was it after that that Whittington handed you the money, and told you to come the following day?"

Tuppence nodded.

"Undoubtedly the hand of Mr. Brown! Well, you see what you are pitting yourselves against? Possibly the finest criminal brain of the age. I don't quite like it. You're such young things, both of you."

"It won't," Tuppence assured him positively.

"I'll look after her, sir," said Tommy.

"And *I'll* look after *you*," retorted Tuppence.

"Well, then, look after each other. Now—there's something mysterious about this draft treaty we haven't fathomed. The Revolutionary element as good as declare it's in their hands, yet they are clearly at fault about many of its provisions. The position is much as though they had got hold of an incriminating document, but couldn't read it because it was in cipher—but we know the draft treaty wasn't in cipher. Of course, Jane Finn may be dead for all we know—but I don't think so. The curious thing is that *they're trying to get information about the girl from us*."

"What?"

"They know we're looking for Jane Finn. They'll produce a Jane Finn of their own—say, at a *pensionnat* in Paris. No one knows in the least what she looks like, so that's all right. She's primed with a trumped-up tale, and her real business is to get information out of us."

"Then you think—it *was* as Jane Finn that they wanted me to go to Paris?"

Mr. Carter smiled more wearily than ever. "I believe in coincidences, you know."

## CHAPTER V. MR. JULIUS P. HERSHEIMMER

"Well," said Tuppence, recovering herself, "it really seems as though it were meant to be."

"Have you any tips before we clear out, sir?" Tommy asked.

"I think not. My experts, working in stereotyped ways, have failed. You will bring imagination and an open mind. I'm just warning you that you haven't much time. I have information that the big *coup* was planned for early in the new year. The less time they have to mature their plans the better."

Tuppence rose. "What exactly can we count upon you for, Mr. Carter?"

"Funds within reason, detailed information on any point, and *no official recognition*. If you get yourselves into trouble with the police, I can't officially help you out. You're on your own."

"I quite understand. Now—about money—"

"Three hundred a year. And an equal sum for Mr. Beresford, of course."

Tuppence beamed. "How lovely. You are kind. I do love money! I'll keep beautiful accounts of our expenses—debit and credit, and the balance on the right side."

He shook hands with them, and in another minute they were descending the steps with their heads in a whirl.

"Tommy! Tell me at once, who is 'Mr. Carter'?"

Tommy murmured a name in her ear.

"Oh!"

"And I can tell you, old bean, he's IT!"

"I like him. He looks so awfully tired and bored, and yet you feel that underneath he's just like steel, all keen and flashing. Pinch me, Tommy, do pinch me. I can't believe it's real!"

Mr. Beresford obliged.

"Ow! That's enough!"

"Julius P. Hersheimmer!" they exclaimed together.

"We never told Mr. Carter about hearing from him."

"Come on, we'd better take a taxi."

On inquiring for Mr. Hersheimmer at the *Ritz*, they were taken up to his suite. He was younger than they had pictured—perhaps thirty-five, of middle height, squarely built to match his jaw, his face pugnacious but pleasant. No one could have mistaken him for anything but an American.

"Get my note? Sit down and tell me right away all you know about my cousin."

"Your cousin?"

"Sure thing. Jane Finn. My father and her mother were brother and sister."

"Then you know where she is?"

"No!" Hersheimmer brought down his fist on the table. "I'm darned if I do. Don't you?"

"We advertised to receive information, not to give it," said Tuppence severely.

But Mr. Hersheimmer seemed to grow suddenly suspicious. "See here. This isn't Sicily! No demanding ransom or threatening to crop her ears if I refuse. So quit the funny business, or I'll just sing out for that beautiful big British policeman I see out there in Piccadilly."

Tommy hastened to explain. "We haven't kidnapped your cousin. On the contrary, we're trying to find her. We're employed to do so." He gave a guarded version of the disappearance, alluding to Tuppence and himself as "private inquiry agents" commissioned in connection with "some political show."

"I guess that's all right. I was just a mite hasty. Just trot out your questions."

"When did you last see your cousin?"

"Never seen her. My father and her mother were brother and sister, but they didn't always get on together. When my aunt made up her mind to marry Amos Finn, a poor school teacher out West, my father was just mad! Said if he made his pile, she'd never see a cent of it. Aunt Jane went out West and we never heard from her again.

"The old man *did* pile it up. He went into oil, and steel, and railroads—made Wall Street sit up. Then he died last fall, and I got the dollars. My conscience got busy. I hired a man to hunt her down. Result, she was dead, and Amos Finn was dead, but they'd left a daughter—Jane—who'd been torpedoed in the *Lusitania* on her way to Paris. She was saved all right, but they didn't seem able to hear of her over this side. So I came along over to speed things up. I phoned Scotland Yard and the Admiralty first thing. Scotland Yard were very civil—even sent a man round this morning to get her photograph. I'm off to Paris to-morrow."

Oysters had just given place to Sole Colbert when a card was brought to Hersheimmer.

"Scotland Yard again. Another man. I hope they haven't lost that photograph. The Western photographer's place was burned down and his negatives destroyed—this is the only copy in existence."

An unformulated dread swept over Tuppence.

"You—you don't know the name of the man who came this morning?"

"Yes, I do. It was on his card. Inspector Brown. Quiet, unassuming sort of chap."

## CHAPTER VI. A PLAN OF CAMPAIGN

A veil might with profit be drawn over the next half-hour. No such person as "Inspector Brown" was known to Scotland Yard. The photograph of Jane Finn was lost beyond recovery. Once again Mr. Brown had triumphed.

The immediate result was a *rapprochement* between Julius Hersheimmer and the Young Adventurers. All barriers crashed down; Tommy and Tuppence revealed the whole history of the joint venture, whereat the young man declared himself "tickled to death."

"I've always had a kind of idea that English girls were just a mite moss-grown," he told Tuppence. "Old-fashioned and sweet, but scared to move round without a footman or a maiden aunt. I guess I'm a bit behind the times!"

The upshot was that Tommy and Tuppence took up their abode at the *Ritz*, in order, as Tuppence put it, to keep in touch with Jane Finn's only living relation. "And put like that," she added confidentially to Tommy, "nobody could boggle at the expense!"

Nobody did, which was the great thing.

"And now," said Tuppence the morning after their installation, "to work!"

Tommy lowered the *Daily Mail* and applauded with somewhat unnecessary vigour. He was politely requested by his colleague not to be an ass.

"Dash it all, Tommy, we've got to *do* something for our money."

"Yes, I fear even the dear old Government will not support us at the *Ritz* in idleness for ever."

"Therefore we must do something. We have two distinct clues. First, we know one of the gang."

"Whittington?"

"Yes. I'd recognize him anywhere."

"Hum. You don't know where to look for him, and it's about a thousand to one against running into him by accident."

"Once coincidences start happening, they go on happening in the most extraordinary way. Still—clue No. 2 is more promising. Only a Christian name—Rita."

She set out her reasoning: the man Danvers had been shadowed on the way over, very likely by a woman, a good-looking one. That woman must have been saved—else how would they have known Jane Finn had the papers? With luck, she might be Rita.

"Then we've got to hunt through the survivors of the *Lusitania* till we find her. The first thing is to get a list."

"I've got it." Tuppence had written a long list of things she wanted to know and sent it to Mr. Carter; his reply, received that morning, enclosed the official statement of those saved from the *Lusitania*. But the list gave few Christian names; mostly Mrs. or Miss.

"That complicates matters."

Tuppence gave her characteristic terrier shake. "We've just got to get down to it. We'll start with the London area."

Five minutes later they emerged into Piccadilly, bearing a list of seven. The first address proved a dilapidated house with a few grimy bushes for a front garden. As Tuppence reached for the bell, Tommy arrested her hand.

"What are you going to say?"

"Why—I shall say—oh dear, I don't know."

"I thought as much. How like a woman! Stand aside, and see how easily the mere male deals with the situation." He pressed the bell. A slatternly servant answered.

"Good morning," said Tommy briskly. "From the Hampstead Borough Council. The new Voting Register. Mrs. Edgar Keith?"

"Yaas."

"Christian name?"

"Eleanor Jane."

"Thank you." He closed the notebook with a snap.

"I thought perhaps as you'd come about the gas," the servant observed cryptically, and shut the door.

"Child's play to the masculine mind."

"For once you've scored handsomely. I should never have thought of it."

By lunch they had collected a Gladys Mary and a Marjorie, been baffled by one change of address, and endured a long lecture on universal suffrage from a vivacious American named Sadie.

"Where's the next draw?"

"Mrs. Vandemeyer, 20 South Audley Mansions."

"The Mayfair lady is clearly indicated."

"Tommy, I'm getting discouraged."

"Buck up, old bean. We always knew it was an outside chance."

South Audley Mansions was an imposing block just off Park Lane. To the elderly woman who opened the door of No. 20—looking more like a housekeeper than a servant—Tommy rattled off his formula.

"Christian name?"

"Margaret." She watched him begin to spell it, then corrected: "No—g-u-e."

"Oh, Marguerite; French way, I see." He plunged boldly. "We had her down as Rita Vandemeyer, but I suppose that's incorrect?"

"She's mostly called that, sir, but Marguerite's her name."

Hardly able to contain his excitement, Tommy hurried down. Tuppence was waiting at the angle of the turn.

"You heard?"

"Yes. Oh, *Tommy!*"

He squeezed her arm sympathetically. "I know, old thing. I feel the same."

"It's so lovely to think of things—and then for them really to happen!"

There were footsteps on the stairs above, and voices. Suddenly Tuppence dragged him into the shadow beside the lift.

"Hush!"

Two men passed out through the entrance. Tuppence's hand closed tighter on his arm.

"Quick—follow them. I daren't. He might recognize me. The bigger of the two was Whittington."

## CHAPTER VII. THE HOUSE IN SOHO

Whittington and his companion walked at a good pace through the small Mayfair streets. Tommy started in pursuit. Following anyone, he discovered, was fraught with difficulties unmentioned in the novels he had read. But the men did not hail a taxi, and presently they zigzagged into Oxford Street.

Just before Bond Street Tube they crossed and entered the big Lyons'. Tommy took a seat directly behind Whittington at the next table. The other man was fair, with a weak unpleasant face—Russian or Pole, Tommy judged—about fifty, his shoulders cringing a little, his eyes small and crafty. Whittington addressed him as Boris.

He talked earnestly in a low voice; Tommy caught only fragments—"Ireland," "propaganda," but no mention of Jane Finn. Then in a lull he heard Whittington whole: "Ah, but you don't know Flossie. She's a marvel. An archbishop would swear she was his own mother. She gets the voice right every time, and that's really the principal thing."

Then Tommy lost the thread, until two words uttered by Boris had a most stimulating effect upon the listener: "Mr. Brown."

Whittington seemed to remonstrate; Boris merely laughed.

"Why not, my friend? It is a name most respectable—most common. Did he not choose it for that reason? Ah, I should like to meet him—Mr. Brown."

There was a steely ring in Whittington's voice. "Who knows? You may have met him already."

"Bah! That is children's talk—a fable for the police. Or is it indeed true that he is with us and amongst us, unknown to all but a chosen few? If so, he keeps his secret well. We never know. We look at each other—*one of us is Mr. Brown*—which? He commands—but also he serves. Among us—in the midst of us. And no one knows which he is...."

With an effort the Russian shook off the fancy, and looked at his watch.

Outside, Whittington hailed a taxi and directed it to Waterloo. Tommy followed in another. At the booking-office Whittington took a first-class single to Bournemouth; Tommy did the same. He overheard Boris glance up at the clock: "You are early. You have nearly half an hour."

So Whittington was making the journey alone. Tommy could not follow both men—unless. He hurried into a telephone box. He dared not waste time tracing Tuppence; he rang the *Ritz* and asked for Hersheimmer.

"That you, Hersheimmer? Beresford speaking. I'm at Waterloo. I've followed Whittington and another man here. Whittington's off to Bournemouth by the 3.30. Can you get there by then?"

"Sure. I'll hustle."

Tommy put back the receiver with a sigh of relief. The taking of his ticket had left him with only a few shillings; it was to be hoped Julius would arrive better provided.

3.15. 3.20. 3.27. Doors banged. 3.29.... Then a hand fell on his shoulder.

"Here I am, son. Your British traffic beats description!"

"That's Whittington—the big dark man, getting in now. The other is the foreign chap he's talking to. Got any money?"

Julius shook his head. "I guess I haven't more than three or four hundred dollars on me."

Tommy whooped. "Oh, Lord, you millionaires! Climb aboard the lugger. Here's your ticket. Whittington's your man."

The train slid out as Julius swung himself aboard. Tommy drew a deep breath. Boris was coming along the platform; Tommy let him pass and took up the chase again.

From Waterloo Boris took the tube to Piccadilly Circus, walked up Shaftesbury Avenue, and turned into the maze of mean streets round Soho. Tommy followed at a judicious distance. They reached at length a small dilapidated square—a cul-de-sac, sinister in dirt and decay. Boris glanced round; Tommy drew back into a friendly porch.

From the doorway he watched Boris go up the steps of a particularly evil-looking house and rap, with a peculiar rhythm, on the door. It opened promptly; he passed inside.

It was at this juncture that Tommy lost his head. What any sane man would have done was wait. What he did do was entirely foreign to the sober common sense which was, as a rule, his leading characteristic. Something seemed to snap in his brain. He went up the steps and reproduced the peculiar knock as best he could.

The door swung open. A villainous-faced man with close-cropped hair stood in the doorway.

"Well?" he grunted.

The full realization of his folly began to come home to Tommy. But he dared not hesitate. He seized at the first words that came into his mind.

"Mr. Brown?"

To his surprise the man stood aside.

"Upstairs," he said, jerking his thumb. "Second door on your left."

## CHAPTER VIII. THE ADVENTURES OF TOMMY

Tommy passed quietly into the house and mounted the ramshackle staircase. Everything was filthy beyond words. The man below had disappeared into a back room; clearly no suspicion attached. To come and ask for "Mr. Brown" appeared a reasonable proceeding.

At the top a narrow passage ran in front of him, doors on either side. From the one nearest on the left came a low murmur of voices. What fascinated him was a small recess immediately on his right, half concealed by a torn velvet curtain—directly opposite the left-hand door, commanding a view of the staircase. As a hiding-place it was ideal.

He thought it over in his slow, steady way. "Mr. Brown" was probably a password used by the gang; his lucky use of it had gained him admission. To enter the room itself would be a colossal risk—a further password might be required, or proof of identity. He could not hope to sustain his part indefinitely.

A repetition of the signal knock sounded below. Tommy slipped into the recess and drew the curtain across. There were rents enough in the ancient material to give him a good view.

The man who came up was unknown—obviously of the dregs of society, his low beetling brows and criminal jaw a type Scotland Yard would have recognized at a glance. He gave the signal knock at the door opposite. As he was admitted, Tommy caught a momentary glimpse of the room: four or five people round a long table, and at its head a tall man with close-cropped hair and a short, pointed, naval-looking beard, papers before him. He spoke with a curiously precise enunciation.

"Your number, comrade?"

"Fourteen, gov'nor," the other replied hoarsely.

"Correct."

"If that isn't a Hun, I'm a Dutchman," Tommy thought. "Lucky I didn't roll in. I'd have given the wrong number."

The next visitor proved an Irish Sinn Feiner; the next, a quiet, intelligent man like a city clerk; then a working-man whose face was vaguely familiar; then a man of commanding appearance, exquisitely dressed, evidently well born—his face also not unknown to the watcher, though he could not place it. A strange and sinister gathering. Who held in his finger these curiously variegated links of an unknown chain?

After his arrival there was a long wait. Tommy was cautiously creeping out when another knock sent him scuttling back. This last-comer came up so quietly he was almost abreast of Tommy before the young man realized his presence—small, very pale, with a gentle, almost womanish air. The angle of the cheek-bones hinted at his Slavonic ancestry. As he passed, he turned his head slowly. The strange light eyes seemed to burn through the curtain; Tommy could hardly believe that the man did not know he was there, and in spite of himself he shivered. The creature reminded him of a venomous snake.

His reception was different. The bearded man rose, and all the others followed suit. The German came forward and shook hands, his heels clicking together.

"We are honoured. I much feared that it would be impossible."

The other answered in a low voice with a kind of hiss in it: "There were difficulties. But one meeting is essential—to define my policy. I can do nothing without—Mr. Brown. He is here?"

The German's voice changed audibly. "We have received a message. It is impossible for him to be present in person." He gave a curious impression of having left the sentence unfinished.

A very slow smile overspread the other's face. "Ah! I understand. He works in the dark and trusts no one. But, all the same, it is possible that he is among us now...." He looked round, and an expression of fear swept the group. Each man seemed eyeing his neighbour doubtfully.

"So be it. Let us proceed."

Number Fourteen shut the door, and the voices sank once more to an undistinguishable murmur. Tommy must hear more. He removed his shoes and crept to the door, turned the handle by infinitesimal degrees, then pressed. The door did not budge. He pressed harder, then in desperation pushed with all his might. Locked or bolted on the inside.

"Well, I'm damned!" he muttered. "What a dirty trick!"

There was a bulldog tenacity about Tommy that made him slow to admit defeat. A little farther along the passage was a second door. He slipped through into an untenanted bedroom—and there was the thing he had hoped to find: a communicating door. The bolt was rusty; with patient wriggling he drew it back, then turned the handle. The door swung open a crack. A velvet *portière* inside prevented him seeing, but he could recognize the voices.

The Sinn Feiner was speaking. "More money is essential. No money—no results!"

"Will you guarantee that there *are* results?" That was Boris.

"In a month from now I will guarantee you such a reign of terror in Ireland as shall shake the British Empire to its foundations."

A pause; then the soft, sibilant accents of Number One: "Good! You shall have the money. Boris, you will see to that."

"Via the Irish Americans, and Mr. Potter as usual?"

"I guess that'll be all right," said a new voice with a transatlantic intonation, "though there's a growing disposition to let the Irish settle their own affairs without interference from America."

"Does that matter, since the money only nominally comes from the States?"

"Think of the feelings of Belfast if they could hear you!" said the well-bred voice.

"That is settled," said the sibilant tones. "Now, the loan to an English newspaper—Boris?"

"I think so."

"An official denial from Moscow will be forthcoming if necessary."

The German resumed: "I am directed by—Mr. Brown, to place the summaries of the reports from the different unions before you. The miners are most satisfactory. We must hold back the railways. There may be trouble with the A.S.E."

For a long time there was only the rustle of papers. Then fingers drumming on the table.

"And—the date, my friend?"

"The 29th."

"That is rather soon."

"It was settled by the principal Labour leaders. They must believe it to be entirely their own show."

The Russian laughed softly, as though amused. "Yes, yes. They must have no inkling that we are using them for our own ends. They are honest men—and that is their value to us. It is curious—but you cannot make a revolution without honest men. The instinct of the populace is infallible." He paused, and then repeated, as though the phrase pleased him: "Every revolution has had its honest men. They are soon disposed of afterwards."

There was a sinister note in his voice.

"Clymes must go," resumed the German. "He is too far-seeing. Number Fourteen will see to that."

"That's all right, gov'nor. Suppose I'm nabbed."

"You will have the best legal talent. But in any case you will wear gloves fitted with the finger-prints of a notorious housebreaker."

"Oh, I ain't afraid, gov'nor. All for the good of the cause. The streets is going to run with blood, so they say."

"Then all is arranged. We are assured of success?"

"I—think so." But the German spoke with less than his usual confidence.

Number One's voice held suddenly a dangerous quality: "What has gone wrong?"

"The Labour leaders. If they do not declare a general strike on the 29th—"

"Why should they not?"

"They're honest. Public opinion swings to the side of the Government. They will not go against it."

The Russian's fingers drummed. "I was given to understand there was a certain document in existence which assured success."

"That is so. If that document were placed before the leaders, they would publish it broadcast and declare for the revolution without a moment's hesitation. The Government would be broken finally and completely."

"Then what more do you want?"

"The document itself. It is not in our possession."

"But you know where it is?"

"No."

"Does anyone know?"

"One person—perhaps. And we are not sure of that even."

"Who?"

"A girl."

Tommy held his breath.

"A girl?" The Russian's voice rose contemptuously. "And you have not made her speak? In Russia we have ways."

"This case is different."

"How—different? Where is the girl now?"

"She is—"

But Tommy heard no more. A crashing blow descended on his head, and all was darkness.

## CHAPTER IX. TUPPENCE ENTERS DOMESTIC SERVICE

Earlier that same morning, while Tommy had set off on the trail of two men seen leaving the second-floor flat—one slender thread, the name "Rita," having set the Young Adventurers once more upon the track of the abductors of Jane Finn—Tuppence had retraced her steps to the entrance hall of the mansions, now tenanted by a small lift-boy polishing brass and whistling the latest air. She had always got on well with small boys; an ally in the enemy's camp was not to be despised.

Glancing mysteriously round, she leaned in. "I want a word with you." She flung back her coat and exposed a small enamelled badge—in reality the device of a local training corps, pinned in for some flowers a day or two before—but she had noted the corner of a threepenny detective novel protruding from his pocket, and the immediate enlargement of his eyes told her that her tactics were good.

"American Detective Force!" she hissed.

He fell for it.

"One of the flats?" he demanded breathlessly.

"No. 20. Calls herself Vandemeyer. Ready Rita they call her in the States." She produced a long-drawn-out wink and elaborated: old man Rysdale's emeralds, finest collection in the world, worth a million dollars. The boy was ecstatic—it was just like the pictures. So was Tuppence: she was a great frequenter of the cinema.

"Mind you, sonny, not a word of this. In the States we know a smart lad when we see one." She tapped him on the shoulder. "Now—how would it be if a friend of yours had a cousin that might suit the place? The maid here is leaving."

"You leave it to me, miss. I'll fix it in two ticks."

She walked briskly back to the Ritz, well pleased, and dispatched a few brief words to Mr. Carter. Tommy had not returned, which did not surprise her. She set off on a shopping expedition: a cheap clothing store, two second-hand establishments, and a well-known hairdresser's. In the seclusion of her bedroom that evening she smiled at her reflection. An actress's pencil had altered the line of her eyebrows; that, taken with the new luxuriant growth of fair hair above, so changed her appearance that even Whittington would not recognize her. With elevators in her shoes and the cap and apron of service, the disguise would be complete; from hospital experience she knew that a nurse out of uniform was frequently unrecognized by her patients.

"You'll do," she told the pert reflection.

The morning post brought a note from Mr. Carter:

"DEAR MISS TUPPENCE,—You have made a splendid start, and I congratulate you. I should like to point out once more the risks you are running. Those people are absolutely desperate and incapable of either mercy or pity. If you choose to withdraw now no one could blame you.

"If you decide to go through with it, everything is arranged. You have lived for two years with Miss Dufferin, The Parsonage, Llanelly, and Mrs. Vandemeyer can apply to her for a reference. A word of advice—stick as near to the truth as possible. I suggest you represent yourself as what you are, a former V.A.D. who has chosen domestic service. There are many such at present. That explains away any incongruities of voice or manner.

"Good luck.—Your sincere friend, MR. CARTER."

Tuppence's spirits rose mercurially; the warnings passed unheeded. The post also brought a somewhat dirty postcard with "It's O.K." scrawled on it—Tommy.

At ten-thirty she surveyed with pride a battered, artistically corded tin trunk, drove to Paddington, deposited it in the cloak room, and repaired with a handbag to the ladies' waiting-room. Ten minutes later a metamorphosed Tuppence walked demurely out and boarded a bus.

A few minutes past eleven she rang the bell of No. 20. A smart young woman opened the door.

"It's a rotten place," she said without hesitation. "Regular old cat. Accused me of tampering with her letters. There's never anything in the waste-paper basket—she burns everything. Swell clothes, but no class. Cook knows something about her, but won't tell—scared to death of her—"

"Annie!" called a clear voice with a peculiarly steely ring.

The young woman jumped as if shot. "Yes, ma'am."

"Show her in. At once."

Tuppence was ushered into a room on the right of the long passage. A woman stood by the fireplace—no longer in her first youth, the beauty she undeniably possessed hardened and coarsened. Her pale gold hair, owing slight assistance to art, was coiled low on her neck; her eyes, of a piercing electric blue, seemed to possess a faculty of boring into the very soul of the person she was looking at. Her exquisite figure was enhanced by a wonderful gown of indigo charmeuse. And yet, despite her swaying grace and the almost ethereal beauty of her face, one felt instinctively the presence of something hard and menacing, a kind of metallic strength that found expression in the tones of her voice and in that gimlet-like quality of her eyes.

For the first time Tuppence felt afraid. She had not feared Whittington; this woman was different. Mr. Carter's warning recurred to her. Here, indeed, she might expect no mercy.

Fighting down the instinct to turn tail, she returned the lady's gaze firmly and respectfully. As though that first scrutiny had been satisfactory, Mrs. Vandemeyer motioned to a chair.

"You can sit down. How did you hear I wanted a house-parlourmaid?"

"Through a friend who knows the lift boy here. He thought the place might suit me."

Again that basilisk glance. "You speak like an educated girl."

Glibly enough Tuppence ran through her imaginary career on the lines suggested by Mr. Carter. The tension in Mrs. Vandemeyer's attitude relaxed.

"I see. Is there anyone I can write to for a reference?"

"Miss Dufferin, The Parsonage, Llanelly. I was with her two years."

"And then you thought you would get more money in London. Well, it doesn't matter to me. £50—£60—whatever you want. You can come in at once?"

"Yes, ma'am. To-day, if you like. My box is at Paddington."

"Fetch it in a taxi, then. It's an easy place; I am out a good deal. What's your name?"

"Prudence Cooper, ma'am."

"Very well, Prudence. I shall be out to lunch. The cook will show you where everything is."

"Thank you, ma'am."

Tuppence withdrew. The adventure had begun, but she felt less elated than earlier in the morning. It crossed her mind that if the unknown Jane Finn had fallen into the hands of Mrs. Vandemeyer, it was likely to have gone hard with her.

## CHAPTER X. ENTER SIR JAMES PEEL EDGERTON

Tuppence betrayed no awkwardness in her new duties—the archdeacon's daughters were well grounded in household tasks. The cook went in deadly terror of her mistress; for the rest, she cooked like a *chef*. Mrs. Vandemeyer was expecting a guest to dinner, and Tuppence laid the table for two with some inward trepidation, fearing it might prove to be Whittington. The bell rang at a few minutes past eight; she was relieved to find the visitor was the second of the two men Tommy had set out to follow.

He gave his name as Count Stepanov. Mrs. Vandemeyer rose with a quick murmur of pleasure: "It is delightful to see you, Boris Ivanovitch."

Whilst waiting at table, Tuppence listened closely, but the talk ran only on plays and society gossip. After dinner the pair repaired to the small boudoir for coffee and liqueurs. As she retired she heard the Russian say, "New, isn't she?"

"She came in to-day. The other was a fiend. This girl seems all right."

She lingered a moment by the door, which she had carefully neglected to close.

"Quite safe, I suppose?"

"Really, Boris, you are absurdly suspicious. She's the cousin of the hall porter, or something. Nobody even dreams that I have any connection with our—mutual friend, Mr. Brown."

"For heaven's sake, be careful, Rita. That door isn't shut."

Tuppence removed herself speedily, cleared away at hospital speed, then slipped along the passage to Mrs. Vandemeyer's bedroom. Long French windows gave on a balcony running the length of the flat; she crept along it noiselessly until she reached the boudoir window, which stood a little ajar.

"With your persistent recklessness you will end by ruining us!" the Russian was saying bitterly.

"Notoriety of the right kind is the best way of disarming suspicion."

"In the meantime, you are going about everywhere with Peel Edgerton. Not only is he perhaps the most celebrated K.C. in England, but his special hobby is criminology! It is madness!"

"His eloquence has saved untold men from the gallows," said Mrs. Vandemeyer calmly. "I may need his assistance in that line myself one day."

"Give him up, Rita."

"I think not."

"Then, by Heaven—"

"You forget, Boris. I am accountable to no one. I take my orders only from—Mr. Brown."

"They say Peel Edgerton can *smell* a criminal! How do we know what is at the bottom of his sudden interest in you?"

"Reassure yourself. I am commonly accounted a beautiful woman. I assure you that is all that interests him. Besides," she added, "he is extremely rich. The 'sinews of war,' you know, Boris."

"Money—money! Sometimes I believe you would sell—*us*."

"The price would have to be enormous. Beyond the power of anyone but a millionaire to pay."

"Ah! You see, I was right!"

"My dear Boris, can you not take a joke? Touch the bell. We will have some drinks."

Tuppence beat a hasty retreat. The conversation proved beyond doubt the complicity of both Rita and Boris, but threw little light on present preoccupations; Jane Finn's name had not been mentioned.

The next morning a few brief words with Albert—who had been calling in at a small stationer's near the mansions, the message drop she had arranged before leaving the Ritz—confirmed that nothing was waiting for her there. It seemed incredible that Tommy should not send word. A cold hand seemed to close round her heart. But she leapt at a chance offered by Mrs. Vandemeyer.

"What day do you usually go out, Prudence?"

"Friday's my usual day, ma'am."

"And to-day is Friday. But I suppose you hardly wish—"

"I was thinking of asking you if I might."

Mrs. Vandemeyer looked at her a minute longer, then smiled. "I wish Count Stepanov could hear you. He made a suggestion about you last night." Her smile broadened, catlike. "Your request is very—typical. You can go out to-day. I shall not be dining at home."

Once out of the other's presence, Tuppence admitted to herself that she was afraid, horribly afraid, of the beautiful woman with the cruel eyes.

In the midst of a desultory polishing of her silver, the front door bell disturbed her. The visitor this time was a man of striking appearance—just a shade over average height, yet conveying the impression of a big man; his face, clean-shaven and exquisitely mobile, was stamped with an expression of power and force far beyond the ordinary. Magnetism seemed to radiate from him. Her doubts whether to put him down as actor or lawyer were soon solved when he gave his name: Sir James Peel Edgerton.

This, then, was the famous K.C. whose name was familiar all over England. She had heard it said he might one day be Prime Minister. She returned to her pantry thoughtfully; she understood Boris's agitation. Peel Edgerton would not be an easy man to deceive.

In about a quarter of an hour the bell rang and Tuppence repaired to the hall to show the visitor out. As she handed him his hat and stick, she was conscious of his eyes raking her through. He stopped in the doorway.

"Not been doing this long, eh?"

She raised her eyes, astonished. She read in his glance kindliness, and something else more difficult to fathom.

"V.A.D. and hard up, I suppose?"

"Did Mrs. Vandemeyer tell you that?" she asked suspiciously.

"No, child. The look of you told me. Good place here?"

"Very good, thank you, sir."

"Ah, but there are plenty of good places nowadays. And a change does no harm sometimes."

"Do you mean—?"

But Sir James was already on the topmost stair. He looked back with his kindly, shrewd glance. "Just a hint. That's all."

Tuppence went back to the pantry more thoughtful than ever.

## CHAPTER XI. JULIUS TELLS A STORY

Dressed appropriately, Tuppence sallied forth for her "afternoon out." She went first to the stationer's to make sure nothing had come for her, then to the Ritz. Tommy had not yet returned. It was the answer she had expected, but another nail in the coffin of her hopes. She resolved to appeal to Mr. Carter, telling him when and where Tommy had started on his quest, and asking him to trace him. The prospect revived her mercurial spirits, and she inquired for Julius Hersheimmer. He had returned half an hour ago, but had gone out immediately.

She wrote her note to Mr. Carter in Julius's sitting-room, and was just addressing the envelope when the door burst open.

"What the hell——" began Julius, then checked himself. "I beg your pardon, Miss Tuppence. Those fools down at the office would have it that Beresford wasn't here any longer—hadn't been here since Wednesday. Is that so?"

Tuppence nodded. "You don't know where he is?"

"I? How should I know? I haven't had one darned word from him, though I wired him yesterday morning."

"I expect your wire's at the office unopened. Where is he?"

"I don't know. I hoped you might."

"I haven't had one darned word from him since we parted at the depot on Wednesday."

"What depot?"

"Waterloo. Your London and South Western road. He gave me a call. Said he was trailing two crooks. I hurried along right away. Beresford pointed out the crooks. The big one was mine, the guy you bluffed. Tommy shoved a ticket into my hand and told me to get aboard. He was going to sleuth the other crook." Julius paused. "I thought for sure you'd know all this."

"Julius," said Tuppence firmly, "stop walking up and down. It makes me giddy. Sit down in that armchair, and tell me the whole story with as few fancy turns of speech as possible."

Mr. Hersheimmer obeyed.

"Well, I got into one of your dear old-fashioned first-class British compartments. Did a bit of prospecting along the corridor. Whittington was there right enough. When I saw the skunk, with his big sleek fat face, and thought of poor little Jane in his clutches, I felt real mad that I hadn't got a gun with me.

"We got to Bournemouth. Whittington took a cab to an hotel. I did likewise. He hired a room, and I hired one too. He sat around in the lounge, reading the papers till dinner. I began to think there was nothing doing—but he hadn't changed for dinner, though it was a slap-up hotel, so it seemed likely he'd be going out on his real business afterwards.

"Sure enough, about nine o'clock, so he did. Took a car across town, paid it off, and struck out along those pine-woods on the top of the cliff. I was there too, you understand. We walked maybe half an hour. The villas got more and more thinned out, and in the end we got to one that seemed the last of the bunch—big house, piny grounds.

"It was a pretty black night, the carriage drive dark as pitch. I could hear him ahead but couldn't see him. I turned a curve just in time to see him ring the bell and get admitted. I stopped where I was. It was beginning to rain. Whittington didn't come out, and by and by I got restive, and began to mouch around. All the ground floor windows were shuttered tight, but upstairs I noticed a window with a light burning and the curtains not drawn.

"Just opposite that window there was a tree growing—about thirty foot from the house. I got it into my head that if I climbed up I'd very likely see into that room. No reason why Whittington should be in that room rather than any other—less reason, in fact—but I'd got the hump from standing in the rain. So I started up.

"It wasn't easy. The boughs were mighty slippery. But bit by bit I managed it, till at last I was level with the window. Then I was disappointed. I was too far to the left—a bit of curtain and a yard of wallpaper was all I could command. Just as I was going to give it up, some one inside moved and threw his shadow on my little bit of wall—and, by gum, it was Whittington!

"After that, my blood was up. I noticed a long branch running out in the right direction. Mighty uncertain whether it would bear my weight. I decided I'd just got to risk it. Inch by inch, I crawled along. The bough creaked and swayed in a nasty fashion, but at last I got safely to where I wanted to be.

"The room was medium-sized, furnished bare and hygienic. At a table with a lamp, facing me, was Whittington. He was talking to a woman dressed as a hospital nurse. Her back was to me. The window was shut, so I couldn't catch a word. Whittington seemed to be doing all the talking. He was very emphatic—once or twice he beat with his fist on the table.

"Presently he got up, and so did she. He looked towards the window and asked something. She came right across and looked out. Just then the moon came out from behind the clouds. I was full in the moonlight. I tried to move back. The jerk was too much for that rotten old branch. Down it came, and Julius P. Hersheimmer with it!"

"Oh, Julius," breathed Tuppence, "how exciting! Go on."

"Luckily I pitched into a good soft bed of earth—but it put me out of action. Next thing I knew, I was lying in bed with a hospital nurse (not Whittington's one) on one side, and a little black-bearded man with gold glasses, medical man written all over him, on the other. 'Ah!' he said. 'So our young friend is coming round again. Capital. Capital.'

"I did the usual stunt. Said: 'What's happened? Where am I?' But I knew the answer to the last well enough. There's no moss growing on my brain. I tried to sit up, but my right foot gave a nasty twinge. 'A slight sprain,' said the doctor. 'You'll be about again in a couple of days.'"

"I noticed you walked lame," interpolated Tuppence.

Julius nodded. "'How did it happen?' I asked. He replied dryly: 'You fell, with a considerable portion of one of my trees, into one of my newly planted flower-beds.'

"I liked the man—seemed to have a sense of humour. Felt sure he was plumb straight. 'Sure, doc,' I said, 'I'm sorry about the tree. But perhaps you'd like to know what I was doing in your garden?' 'I think the facts do call for an explanation.' 'Well, to begin with, I wasn't after the spoons.' He smiled. 'My first theory. By the way, you are an American?' I told him my name. 'And you?' 'I am Dr. Hall, and this is my private nursing home.'

"I made up my mind in a flash. I mumbled out something about a girl—stern guardian, nervous breakdown, fancied I'd recognized her among the patients. 'Quite a romance,' he said genially. 'Now, doc,' I went on, 'will you be frank with me? Have you here now, or have you had here at any time, a young girl called Jane Finn?' He repeated the name thoughtfully. 'Jane Finn? No.'

"I was chagrined. 'You are sure?' 'Quite sure, Mr. Hersheimmer. It is an uncommon name.'

"That laid me out. 'Now, there's another matter. When I was hugging that branch I thought I recognized an old friend talking to one of your nurses.' I didn't mention any name—Whittington might be calling himself something different—but the doctor answered at once. 'Mr. Whittington, perhaps?' 'That's the fellow. What's he doing down here?'

"Dr. Hall laughed. 'He came down to see one of my nurses, Nurse Edith, who is a niece of his.' 'Is he still here?' 'No, he went back to town almost immediately.' 'Perhaps I could speak to his niece?' But the doctor shook his head. 'Nurse Edith left with a patient to-night also.' 'Have you his address in town?' 'I don't know it. I can write to Nurse Edith for it if you like.' I thanked him. 'Don't say who it is wants it. I'd like to give him a little surprise.'

"That was all I could do. I wired Beresford that I was laid up. I had to be guarded. Didn't hear from him, my foot soon got right—it was only ricked—and to-day I came back to town. Say, Miss Tuppence, you're looking mighty pale!"

"It's Tommy," said Tuppence. "What can have happened to him?"

"Buck up, I guess he's all right really. See here, it was a foreign-looking guy he went off after. Maybe they've gone abroad—to Poland, or something?"

"He couldn't without passports. Besides, I've seen that man, Boris Something, since. He dined with Mrs. Vandemeyer last night."

"Mrs. Who?"

"I forgot. You don't know all that."

"Put me wise."

Tuppence related the events of the last two days. Julius's astonishment was unbounded.

"Bully for you! Fancy you a menial. It just tickles me to death!" Then more seriously: "But say now, I don't like it, Miss Tuppence. These crooks would as soon croak a girl as a man."

"Do you think I'm afraid?" said Tuppence indignantly, repressing memories of the steely glitter in Mrs. Vandemeyer's eyes.

"I said you were darned plucky. But that doesn't alter facts. Let's think what can have happened to Tommy. I guess we'd better get on the track of Boris. You say he's been to your place. I'd better get hold of a car, dress as a chauffeur and hang about outside. If Boris comes, you could signal, and I'd trail him."

"Splendid, but he mightn't come for weeks."

"We'll have to chance that." He rose. "I'll be round in half an hour."

"You're awfully good, Julius. But I can't help feeling it's rather a forlorn hope. I'm really pinning my faith to Mr. Carter."

"Then I shouldn't."

"Why?"

"Just an idea of mine."

"Oh, but he must do something. There's no one else. By the way, I forgot to tell you of a queer thing that happened this morning." And she narrated her encounter with Sir James Peel Edgerton. Julius was interested.

"What did the guy mean, do you think?"

"I don't quite know. But I think that, in an ambiguous, legal, without-prejudishish lawyer's way, he was trying to warn me. He looked kind, and simply awfully clever. I wouldn't mind going to him and telling him everything."

Somewhat to her surprise, Julius negatived the idea sharply. "See here, we don't want any lawyers mixed up in this. That guy couldn't help us any."

"Well, I believe he could," reiterated Tuppence obstinately.

"Don't you think it. So long."

Thirty-five minutes later Julius returned, and walked Tuppence to the window. "There she is."

"Oh!" said Tuppence with a note of reverence, gazing down at the enormous Rolls-Royce.

"How did you get it?" she gasped.

"She was just being sent home to some bigwig. I went round to his house. Said I reckoned a car like that was worth every penny of twenty thousand dollars. Then I told him it was worth just about fifty thousand dollars to me if he'd get out."

"Well?" said Tuppence, intoxicated.

"Well," returned Julius, "he got out, that's all."

## CHAPTER XII. A FRIEND IN NEED

Friday and Saturday passed uneventfully. Mr. Carter's reply to Tuppence's appeal was cold comfort: the Young Adventurers had undertaken the work at their own risk and been fully warned; if anything had happened to Tommy, he regretted it, but could do nothing.

Without Tommy, all the savour went out of the adventure, and for the first time Tuppence felt doubtful of success. She had relied upon him more than she had realized—there was something eminently sober and clear-headed about him. Curious that Julius, undoubtedly cleverer, did not give her the same support. Tommy was slow, but very sure. The mission they had undertaken so lightheartedly, like a page of romance, was turning to grim reality. "Little fool," she apostrophized herself, blinking the tears away, "don't snivel."

Tuppence had not entirely relinquished the idea of appealing to Sir James Peel Edgerton. Sunday was her afternoon out. Julius required persuading, but at last they proceeded to Carlton House Terrace.

The butler ushered them into a library—one wall devoted to works on crime and criminology. Sir James rose from a roll-top desk.

"You have a message for me? Ah—it's you, is it? From Mrs. Vandemeyer, I suppose?"

"I'm afraid I only said that to be sure of getting in. This is Mr. Hersheimmer."

"Sir James," Tuppence plunged in when seated, "did you mean to warn me against Mrs. Vandemeyer the other day?"

"My dear young lady, as far as I recollect I only mentioned that there were equally good situations to be obtained elsewhere."

"But it was a hint. I want to know just _why_."

He smiled. "Suppose the lady brings a libel action against me?"

"Can't we say 'without prejudice'?"

"Without prejudice, then—if I had a young sister forced to earn her living, I should not like to see her in Mrs. Vandemeyer's service."

"I'm not _really_ inexperienced. I knew she was a bad lot when I went there—that's _why_ I went——" She broke off. "I'd better tell you the whole story."

The lawyer listened with close attention.

"Very interesting. A great deal of what you tell me is already known to me. I've had certain theories of my own about this Jane Finn. You've done extraordinarily well, but it's rather too bad of Mr. Carter to pitchfork you two young things into this. Where did Mr. Hersheimmer come in originally?"

"I'm Jane's first cousin," Julius explained.

"Ah!"

"Sir James," broke out Tuppence, "what do you think has become of Tommy?"

The lawyer paced slowly. "When you arrived, I was just packing up for Scotland—a few days' fishing. But there are different kinds of fishing. I've a good mind to stay, and see if we can't get on the track of that young chap."

Tuppence clasped her hands ecstatically.

"Things look bad for him. But don't give up hope. There's one person quite near at hand who in all probability knows where he is. Mrs. Vandemeyer."

"But she'd never tell us."

"Ah, that is where I come in. I have one or two powerful levers. Still, there is always the possibility of bribery."

"Sure. And that's where I come in!" cried Julius. "You can count on me for one million dollars!"

Sir James gave him a long scrutiny. "That is a very large sum."

"These aren't the kind of folk to offer sixpence to."

"There is no time to be lost," said Sir James, turning to Tuppence. "Is Mrs. Vandemeyer dining out tonight?"

"Yes, but she will not be out late."

"Good. I will call upon her about ten. Be back by nine-thirty as usual—it might arouse suspicion otherwise. Mr. Hersheimmer will wait below in a taxi."

"He's got a new Rolls-Royce," said Tuppence with vicarious pride.

"Even better. If I obtain the address, we can go at once, taking Mrs. Vandemeyer with us if necessary."

"Isn't he a duck?" inquired Tuppence ecstatically as they descended the steps.

"Well, I allow he seems to be the goods."

Tuppence asked to be dropped in the park. As she drew near Hyde Park corner, the temptation to look at South Audley Mansions was almost irresistible. Just to look.

The building seemed unchanged. She was turning away when a piercing whistle sounded, and Albert came running from the doorway.

"I say, miss, she's a-going! Mrs. Vandemeyer—she's a-packing up, and just sent down for a taxi."

"What?" Tuppence clutched his arm. "Albert—there's a telephone box round the corner. Ring the _Ritz_—ask for Mr. Hersheimmer, tell him to get Sir James and come at once, as Mrs. Vandemeyer is trying to hook it. If you can't get him, ring Sir James Peel Edgerton; his number's in the book."

"But aren't you afraid to trust yourself with her?"

"_Go and telephone._ Be quick."

Drawing a long breath, Tuppence ran up to No. 20. How she was to detain Mrs. Vandemeyer until the men arrived, she did not know.

After two presses of the bell, Mrs. Vandemeyer herself opened the door, eyebrows lifted.

"You?"

"I had a touch of toothache, ma'am. Thought it better to come home."

"How unfortunate. You had better go to bed."

"Oh, I shall be all right in the kitchen—"

"Cook is out. I sent her out. So you had better go to bed."

There was a ring in Mrs. Vandemeyer's voice that Tuppence did not like. The other woman was slowly edging her up the passage. Tuppence turned at bay.

"I don't want—"

In a flash, a rim of cold steel touched her temple, and Mrs. Vandemeyer's voice rose cold and menacing:

"You damned little fool! Do you think I don't know? If you struggle or cry out, I'll shoot you like a dog. March—into my room."

The room was in wild disorder, a half-packed suit-case in the middle of the floor.

"You can't shoot me," Tuppence said, voice shaking. "Every one in the building would hear."

"I'd risk that. But as long as you don't sing out, you're all right—and I don't think you will. You deceived _me_ all right. I hadn't a suspicion of you! Sit on the bed. Hands above your head."

Mrs. Vandemeyer laid the revolver on the edge of the washstand within reach, and, eyeing Tuppence like a lynx, took a stoppered bottle from the marble and poured some into a glass of water.

"What's that?"

"Something to make you sleep soundly."

"Are you going to poison me?"

"Perhaps," she said, smiling agreeably.

"Then I shan't drink it. I'd much rather be shot. At any rate that would make a row."

"Don't be a fool. Do you really think I want a hue and cry for murder after me? It's a sleeping draught. You'll wake tomorrow none the worse. The alternative is being tied up and gagged—and you won't like it."

In her heart Tuppence believed her. But once Mrs. Vandemeyer gave them the slip, the last hope of finding Tommy would be gone. She saw where a chance lay, and determined to risk all in one supreme effort.

She lurched off the bed and fell on her knees, clutching Mrs. Vandemeyer's skirts.

"I don't believe it!" she moaned. "It's poison—I know it's poison! Don't make me drink it!"

"Get up, you little idiot! How you ever had the nerve to play your part as you did, I can't think!"

But Tuppence continued to cling and sob, moving imperceptibly nearer her objective. Mrs. Vandemeyer jerked her up.

"Drink it at once!"

"You swear it won't hurt me?"

"Of course not."

"Will you swear it?"

"Yes, yes. I swear it."

Tuppence raised a trembling left hand to the glass. "Very well." Her mouth opened meekly.

Mrs. Vandemeyer gave a sigh of relief, off her guard. Then, quick as a flash, Tuppence jerked the glass upward. The fluid splashed into Mrs. Vandemeyer's face, and during her momentary gasp Tuppence's right hand shot out and grasped the revolver. The next moment she had sprung back, the revolver pointing straight at Mrs. Vandemeyer's heart.

"Now who's on top and who's underneath?" she crowed.

Mrs. Vandemeyer's face was convulsed with rage, but with an effort she controlled herself, and a slow evil smile crept over her face.

"Not a fool, then, after all! You did that well. But you shall pay for it. I have a long memory!"

"Supposing we sit down," said Tuppence pleasantly. "Our present attitude is melodramatic. Draw a chair up to the table. I'll sit opposite with the revolver in front of me—just in case of accidents. Now, let's talk."

"What about?"

Tuppence eyed her thoughtfully, remembering Boris's words—"I believe you would sell—_us!_"—and Whittington's: "Who's been blabbing? Rita?" Would Rita Vandemeyer prove the weak spot in the armour of Mr. Brown?

"Money," she replied quietly.

Mrs. Vandemeyer started. "What do you mean?"

"You said you had a long memory. A long memory isn't half as useful as a long purse. Revenge is unsatisfactory. But money—there's nothing unsatisfactory about money."

"Do you think I am the kind of woman to sell my friends?"

"Yes," said Tuppence promptly. "If the price was big enough."

"A paltry hundred pounds or so!"

"No. I should suggest—a hundred thousand."

Her economical spirit did not permit her to mention Julius's whole million dollars.

A flush crept over Mrs. Vandemeyer's face. "What did you say?" Her fingers played nervously with a brooch on her breast. In that moment Tuppence knew the fish was hooked, and for the first time felt a horror of her own money-loving spirit—a dreadful sense of kinship with the woman fronting her.

"A hundred thousand pounds."

"Bah! You haven't got it."

"No—but I know some one who has. An American. He'll pay it without a murmur."

"I'm inclined to believe you. What does he want to know?"

"Where Jane Finn is."

"I'm not sure where she is at the present moment, but I could find out. There would be no difficulty."

"Then—there's a boy, a friend of mine. I'm afraid something's happened to him, through your pal Boris. Tommy Beresford."

"Never heard of him. But I'll ask Boris."

Tuppence felt a terrific rise in her spirits. "There's one thing more." She leaned forward. "_Who is Mr. Brown?_"

She saw the sudden paling of the beautiful face. Mrs. Vandemeyer shrugged. "You can't have learnt much about us if you don't know that _nobody knows who Mr. Brown is_..."

"You do," said Tuppence quietly.

Again the colour deserted the other's face. "What makes you think that?"

"I don't know. But I'm sure."

Mrs. Vandemeyer stared in front of her for a long time. "Yes," she said hoarsely at last, "_I_ know. I was beautiful, you see—very beautiful——" A strange gleam in her electric-blue eyes. "Not beautiful enough! And sometimes, lately, I've been afraid.... It's dangerous to know too much!" She leaned forward. "Swear that my name shan't be brought into it."

"I swear it. And once he's caught, you'll be out of danger."

A terrified look swept across her face. "Shall I? Shall I ever be?" She clutched Tuppence's arm. "You're sure about the money? When shall I have it?"

"My friend will be here presently. There won't be any delay."

A resolute look settled on her face. "I'll do it. It is not—wise to throw over a woman like me!"

She tapped her fingers on the table. Suddenly she started, her face blanched.

"What was that?"

"I heard nothing."

"If there should be some one listening—even the walls might have ears. I tell you, I'm frightened. You don't know him!"

"Think of the hundred thousand pounds," said Tuppence soothingly.

"You don't know him," she reiterated hoarsely. "He's—ah!"

With a shriek of terror she sprang to her feet, her outstretched hand pointing over Tuppence's head. Then she swayed to the ground in a dead faint.

Tuppence looked round.

In the doorway stood Sir James Peel Edgerton and Julius Hersheimmer.

## CHAPTER XIII. THE VIGIL

Sir James brushed past Julius and bent over the fallen woman.

"Heart," he said sharply. "Brandy—quickly, or she'll slip through our fingers."

Tuppence directed him to the tantalus in the dining-room. Between them they lifted Mrs. Vandemeyer to the bed and dashed water on her face, but with no result. Julius returned with the spirit, and Sir James forced a little between her closed lips. Finally she opened her eyes feebly, drank, and the colour came back to her white cheeks.

"It's my heart," she whispered. "I mustn't talk." She lay back with closed eyes.

Sir James kept his finger on her wrist a moment longer, then withdrew it with a nod. "She'll do now."

The three drew aside, conscious of anticlimax. Tuppence related how Mrs. Vandemeyer had agreed to disclose Mr. Brown's identity and Jane Finn's whereabouts.

"That hundred thousand pounds will look just as good in the morning," said Julius. "She won't speak without the cash anyway, you bet."

"I confess I wish we had not interrupted at the minute we did," said Sir James. "Still, it cannot be helped."

"We mustn't leave the flat," Tuppence said. "She seemed terribly frightened of Mr. Brown. She looked round and said even walls had ears."

"She meant a dictaphone, maybe," said Julius.

"Miss Tuppence is right," Sir James said quietly. "We have a formidable adversary. We must not leave the flat—if only for Mrs. Vandemeyer's sake. I would suggest Miss Tuppence go to bed, and that Mr. Hersheimmer and I share the vigil."

Tuppence was about to protest when she glanced at the bed. Mrs. Vandemeyer's eyes were half-open, her face such a mingling of fear and malevolence that the words froze on Tuppence's lips. For a moment she wondered whether the faint had been a sham; then the expression vanished as by magic, and the woman lay inert and motionless as before.

As they prepared to leave, Mrs. Vandemeyer lifted her lids. She seemed to struggle to speak. Tuppence bent close.

"Don't—leave—" Then, almost inaudibly: "Mr.—Brown—"

Tuppence said quickly, "I shan't leave the flat. I shall sit up all night."

A flash of relief showed before the lids descended once more.

In the passage Sir James said, "Lock the door on the outside, Miss Tuppence, and take the key. There must be no chance of anyone entering that room."

His gravity impressed them. Tuppence pulled open the wardrobe—of course, no one—and stooped to look beneath the bed before she left, then chid herself for nerves.

She suggested Mrs. Vandemeyer's boudoir for their quarters. Sir James pressed her again to sleep; she shook her head. "I should dream of Mr. Brown all night."

After a hearty meal of cold pie that Julius produced from the larder, Tuppence narrated her adventures. Sir James's quiet "Well done, Miss Tuppence" made her flush with pleasure.

"What put her up to clearing out?" Julius asked.

"The room was in great disorder," Sir James said. "Almost as though she got a sudden warning to go from someone."

"Mr. Brown, I suppose," said Julius scoffingly.

The lawyer looked at him deliberately. "Why not? Remember, you yourself have once been worsted by him."

"In any case, it's the original I'm out after," Julius said. "Where do you think Jane is, Sir James?"

"I've a very good idea where she has been. The scene of your nocturnal adventures—the Bournemouth nursing home."

"Impossible. I asked—"

"You asked if anyone of the name of Jane Finn had been there. If she was placed there it would almost certainly be under an assumed name."

"Bully for you. I never thought of that."

"Perhaps the doctor's in it too," suggested Tuppence.

"I took to him at once. Dr. Hall's all right."

"Hall?" said Sir James. "Curious—I happened to meet him this morning. Staying at the Métropole. You did not mention his name this afternoon, or I would have suggested you go to him for further information."

A silence settled on the party. Little by little the magic of the night took hold—creaks of furniture, rustlings in curtains. Tuppence sprang up.

"I can't help it. I know Mr. Brown's somewhere in the flat. I can _feel_ him."

"This door's open into the hall," Julius said. "No one could have come in by the front door without our seeing him."

"With due deference to your feelings, Miss Tuppence," said Sir James gravely, "I do not see how it is humanly possible for anyone to be in the flat without our knowledge. We are in the condition of people holding a séance. Perhaps if a medium were present we might get some marvellous results."

The hours drew on. With the first faint glimmerings of dawn Sir James drew aside the curtains, and the dreads of the night seemed absurd.

"Hooray!" said Tuppence. "It's going to be a gorgeous day. We shall find Tommy. And Jane Finn. And everything will be lovely."

At seven she made tea—four cups, the fourth for "the prisoner."

"Supposing she's escaped?" she whispered at the door, the key in her hand.

"Plumb impossible," replied Julius.

But Sir James said nothing.

Tuppence entered, drew a long breath as she saw the figure on the bed. "Good morning. I've brought you some tea."

No answer. Tuppence put down the cup, drew the blinds. When she turned, Mrs. Vandemeyer still lay without movement. The hand she lifted was cold as ice.

Mrs. Vandemeyer was dead—must have been dead some hours. She had evidently died in her sleep.

"If that isn't the cruellest luck," cried Julius.

Sir James was calmer, but there was a curious gleam in his eyes. "If it is luck."

"You don't think—but, say, that's plumb impossible. No one could have got in."

"And yet—she is on the point of betraying Mr. Brown, and—she dies. Is it only chance?" He stood there, gently stroking his chin. "We must find out."

A charred mass in the grate showed Mrs. Vandemeyer had been burning papers on the eve of her flight. They searched—nothing of importance remained, though there was a small old-fashioned safe let into the wall.

"It's for jewellery, I believe," said Tuppence, "but there might be something else."

The key was in the lock. Julius swung open the door and searched inside. He was some time over the task.

"Well?" said Tuppence impatiently.

There was a pause before he withdrew his head and shut the door. "Nothing."

A young doctor arrived, deferential to Sir James. "Heart failure, or possibly an overdose of some sleeping-draught." He sniffed. "Rather an odour of chloral in the air."

A new thought drove Tuppence to the washstand. She found the little bottle from which Mrs. Vandemeyer had poured a few drops. It had been three parts full. Now— _it was empty_.

## CHAPTER XIV. A CONSULTATION

Nothing was more bewildering to Tuppence than the ease with which everything was arranged through Sir James's skilful handling. The doctor accepted that Mrs. Vandemeyer had taken an accidental overdose of chloral and doubted whether an inquest would be necessary. A nurse arrived to take charge, and they left the ill-omened building.

"And what now?" asked Julius, with a gesture of despair.

"There is still the chance that Dr. Hall may be able to tell us something," said Sir James. "I told you he is staying at the Métropole. I suggest we call upon him after a bath and breakfast."

A little after eleven they drew up before the Métropole. The little doctor came hurrying.

"Ah, my young friend of the tree episode! Ankle all right, eh?"

"Cured owing to your skilful treatment, doc."

"Can we have a word with you in private?" asked Sir James.

He led them to a quiet room, and Sir James came at once to the point.

"Dr. Hall, I am very anxious to find a young lady who has been at one time in your establishment at Bournemouth. She would almost certainly have been sent to you under an assumed name. Are you acquainted with a Mrs. Vandemeyer?"

"Mrs. Vandemeyer, of 20 South Audley Mansions? I know her slightly."

"You are not aware that she is dead? She took an overdose of chloral last night."

"Dear, dear, I had no idea. Very sad."

"Is it not a fact that she committed a young relative of hers to your charge?"

Julius leaned forward eagerly.

"That is the case," said the doctor. "She came to me in June or July of 1915, under the name of Janet Vandemeyer. I understood her to be a niece. Mrs. Vandemeyer told me the girl had been on the _Lusitania_ when that ill-fated ship was sunk, and had suffered a severe shock in consequence."

"We're on the right track, I think?" Sir James said.

"As I said before, I'm a mutt!" returned Julius.

"You spoke of wanting a statement from her," said the doctor. "Supposing she is not able to give one? She is perfectly sane—but if you want a statement concerning any events prior to May 7, 1915, she will not be able to give it to you. Janet Vandemeyer is suffering from a complete loss of memory."

_"What?"_

"Quite so. After May 7, 1915, her memory is as good as yours or mine. Before that, nothing. She did not know her own name, or where she had come from. She couldn't even speak her own tongue."

"Sooner or later," he went on, "the memory will return—as suddenly as it went. But in all probability the girl will have entirely forgotten the intervening period, and take up life where she left off—at the sinking of the _Lusitania_."

"And when do you expect this to happen?"

The doctor shrugged. "Sometimes a matter of months, sometimes as long as twenty years. Sometimes another shock does the trick. One restores what the other took away."

"Another shock, eh?" said Julius thoughtfully. He fell into a brown study, then suddenly hit the table such a resounding bang that everyone jumped.

"I've got it! Doc, what about your medical opinion on this. Say Jane was to cross the herring pond again, and the same thing was to happen—the submarine, the sinking ship, every one taking to the boats. Wouldn't that give a mighty big bump to her subconscious self, and start it functioning again?"

"In my own opinion, it would be successful. Unfortunately there is no chance of the conditions repeating themselves."

"Not by nature, doc. But I'm talking about art. Hire a liner, hire some passengers, hire a submarine—it's done every day in the States for the movies. By the time she's got a life-belt on her, with a well-drilled lot of artistes doing the hysterical stunt on deck—why, she ought to be right back where she was in May, 1915."

"But the expense, my dear sir!" Dr. Hall's voice rose. "It will be _colossal!_"

"Money doesn't worry me any," explained Julius simply.

Dr. Hall turned an appealing face to Sir James, who smiled slightly. "Mr. Hersheimmer is very well off—very well off indeed."

The doctor's glance came back to Julius with a new deference.

"Very remarkable plan. The movies—of course! And you really mean to carry it out?"

"You bet your bottom dollar I do. Can we get on the long distance to your place right away, and ask them to send her up?"

The doctor stared. "I beg your pardon. I thought you understood. Miss Vandemeyer is no longer under my care."

## CHAPTER XV. TUPPENCE RECEIVES A PROPOSAL

Julius sprang up. "What?"

"I thought you were aware. To-day is Monday, is it not? It must have been last Wednesday—the same evening that you—er—fell out of my tree. A very urgent message arrived from Mrs. Vandemeyer. The young lady and the nurse left by the night train."

Julius sank back. "Nurse Edith—left with a patient—I remember. My God, to have been so near."

Dr. Hall looked bewildered. "Is the young lady not with her aunt, after all?"

Tuppence was about to speak when a warning glance from Sir James made her hold her tongue. The lawyer rose.

"I'm much obliged to you, Hall. I'm afraid we're now in the position of having to track Miss Vandemeyer anew."

Outside they stood by the car.

"How maddening," cried Tuppence. "Julius must have been actually under the same roof with her for a few hours."

"I was a darned idiot."

"You might advertise for the nurse," said Sir James. "That is the only course I can suggest. Otherwise there is nothing to be done."

"Nothing?" said Tuppence blankly. "And—Tommy?"

"We must hope for the best." Over her downcast head his eyes met Julius's, and almost imperceptibly he shook his head. "You must let me know if anything further comes to light."

"You are going away?"

"To Scotland. My dear young lady, I can do nothing more, I fear. Our clues have all ended in thin air."

His words gave Tuppence an extraordinarily desolate feeling. As Julius bent over the car, Sir James's keen eyes rested on her face.

"Don't be too disconsolate, Miss Tuppence," he said in a low voice. "Remember, holiday-time isn't always all playtime. One sometimes manages to put in some work as well."

Something in his tone made her glance up sharply.

"No, I shan't say any more. Great mistake to say too much. Remember that. Never tell all you know—not even to the person you know best."

He strode away. Was this a hint? Did he mean that, secretly, he would be working on it still?

"You're looking kind of thoughtful," remarked Julius as they started off. "Did the old guy say anything more?"

Tuppence opened her mouth impulsively, then shut it again. Sir James's words sounded in her ears. And like a flash there came another memory: Julius before the safe in the flat, her question and the pause before his reply, "Nothing." Was there really nothing? Or had he found something he wished to keep to himself? If he could make a reservation, so could she.

"Nothing particular," she replied.

For a while they ran on under the trees in silence.

"Say, Miss Tuppence, do you think I'm ever going to find Jane?" Julius spoke in a discouraged voice. "Sir James to-day hadn't got any hope at all, I could see that. I've half a mind to go back to the States."

"Oh no! We've got to find Tommy."

"I sure forgot Beresford. We must find him. But after—Miss Tuppence, there's something I'd like to ask you. You and Beresford. What about it?"

"I don't understand you," replied Tuppence with dignity. "And anyway, you're wrong."

"Not got a sort of kindly feeling for one another?"

"Certainly not. Tommy and I are friends—nothing more."

"Supposing we never find Beresford. Supposing he's—dead. What are you going to do?"

"I don't know," said Tuppence forlornly.

"What about marriage? What about me?"

" _You?_ "

"Sure thing."

"Oh, I couldn't!"

"Why not?"

"It would seem so unfair."

"I admire you immensely, Miss Tuppence, more than any girl I've ever met. You're so darned plucky. Say the word, and we'll run round to some high-class jeweller and fix up the ring business."

"I can't," gasped Tuppence.

"Because of Beresford?"

"No, no, _no!_"

Tuppence shook her head violently. "Thanking you very much, but I think I'd better say no."

"I'd be obliged if you'd think it over until to-morrow."

Neither spoke again until they reached the Ritz.

Tuppence went upstairs, sat in front of the glass, and stared at her reflection.

"Fool," she murmured. "Little fool. Everything you want—everything you've ever hoped for, and you bleat out 'no' like an idiotic little sheep. It's your one chance. Why don't you take it? Grab it? Snatch at it? What more do you want?"

Her eyes fell on a small snapshot of Tommy in a shabby frame on her dressing-table. She held it to her lips and burst into a fit of sobbing.

"Oh, Tommy, Tommy, I do love you so—and I may never see you again."

After five minutes she sat up, blew her nose, pushed back her hair.

"I seem to have fallen in love with an idiot of a boy who probably doesn't care two straws about me. He'd never have dared to say so. I've always jumped on sentiment—and here I am being more sentimental than anybody. What idiots girls are. I wonder if he did find anything in that safe—"

Tuppence reviewed the events of last night carefully and persistently. Somehow they seemed bound up with Sir James's enigmatical words.

Suddenly she gave a great start—the colour faded out of her face. Her eyes, fascinated, gazed in front of her, the pupils dilated.

"Impossible," she murmured. "Impossible! I must be going mad even to think of such a thing—"

Monstrous—yet it explained everything.

After a moment's reflection she sat down and wrote a note, weighing each word as she did so. She slipped it into an envelope addressed to Julius and went down the passage to his sitting-room. The room was empty. She left the note on the table.

A small page-boy was waiting outside her own door when she returned.

"Telegram for you, miss."

Tuppence tore it open carelessly. Then she gave a cry. The telegram was from Tommy.

## CHAPTER XVI. FURTHER ADVENTURES OF TOMMY

From a darkness punctuated with throbbing stabs of fire, Tommy dragged his senses back to life. He blinked feebly. This was not his bedroom at the Ritz.

"Damn!" said Tommy, and tried to sit up. He had remembered. He was in that sinister house in Soho. They knew him now for a spy, and would in all probability give him short shrift. Nobody knew where he was; he must depend solely on his own wits.

The bearded German stepped forward and placed a glass to his lips. "Drink." The potency of the draught made him choke, but it cleared his brain in a marvellous manner. He was on a couch in the room where the meeting had been held; the others grouped at a little distance. But Tommy missed one face—the man known as Number One was no longer of the company.

"Have you anything to say before you are put to death as a spy?" asked the German.

"Simply lots of things," replied Tommy with urbanity.

Tommy Beresford was one of those young Englishmen not distinguished by any special intellectual ability, but emphatically at their best in what is known as a "tight place." Behind his casual manner he was racking his brains furiously.

"Why didn't you kill me off at once before I regained consciousness?" he asked. "Because you didn't know how much I knew—and where I obtained that knowledge. If you kill me now, you never will know."

A foreigner among them sprang forward, waving his arms. "You hell-hound of a spy! Kill him!"

"Pack of fools." Tommy shrugged. "How did I get into this place? With your own password, wasn't it? You don't suppose I came up those steps haphazard?"

"That is true," said one suddenly. "Comrades, we have been betrayed!"

"You will tell us who has betrayed us," said the German. "But that shall not save you. You shall tell us all that you know."

"You will neither torture me nor kill me," said Tommy scornfully, fighting down a singularly unpleasant feeling in the pit of his stomach.

"And why not?"

"Because you'd kill the goose that lays the golden eggs."

There was a pause. They were no longer completely sure of themselves.

"I know something that puts me in a position to propose a bargain," Tommy went on. "My life and liberty against—" He paused. "The papers that Danvers brought over from America in the *Lusitania*."

The effect was electrical. Every one was on his feet. The German leaned over him, his face purple with excitement.

"*Himmel!* You have got them, then?"

With magnificent calm Tommy shook his head. "I don't know where the papers are—but I believe that I can find them. If I produce the papers, you give me my life and liberty in exchange. Is it a bargain?"

"And if we refuse?"

"The 29th," said Tommy thoughtfully, "is less than a fortnight ahead—"

The German hesitated, then signed for Tommy to be taken next door. After agonizing minutes he was brought back.

"We accept, on terms. The papers must be delivered to us before you go free."

"Idiot! How can I look for them if you keep me tied by the leg here?"

"One of our number will carry out your instructions. Those are our terms. Otherwise, death!"

"So be it. But one thing is essential. I must see the girl. Jane Finn."

The German looked at him curiously. "Do you not know that she can tell you nothing?"

"I shall not ask her to tell me anything—not in so many words. To watch her face when I ask her one question."

Again there was a look in the German's eyes that Tommy did not quite understand. "I wonder whether, after all, you know as much as we think?"

Tommy felt his hold slip a little. On impulse he spoke. "Danvers was a damned clever fellow——" He broke off as if he had said too much.

But the German's face had lightened. "Danvers. I see." He waved a hand. "Take him away. Upstairs."

"Wait. What about the girl?"

"That may perhaps be arranged. Only one person can decide that."

"Mr. Brown——"

"Perhaps."

Upstairs Tommy was shut into a small, airless, windowless room. Four pictures hung crookedly on the wall representing scenes from Faust—the last, of Faust and Mephistopheles, brought his mind back to Mr. Brown again. In this sealed chamber the sinister power of the arch-criminal seemed more real. The place was a living tomb.

With an effort Tommy pulled himself together and sank on to the bed.

"Anyway," he said, trying to cheer himself, "I shall see the chief—the mysterious Mr. Brown—and with a bit of luck in bluffing I shall see the mysterious Jane Finn also. After that——"

After that the prospect looked dreary.

## CHAPTER XVII. ANNETTE

The present's troubles pressed harder than the future's — chiefly, hunger. Tommy prowled his prison, pounded the door, was answered by no one.

"Hang it all!" he said. "They can't mean to starve me to death." Conrad's spite, he decided. A bright idea flashed: why not wait in ambush behind the door, and bring one of the decrepit pictures down on Conrad's egg-shaped head? Intoxicated by the plan, Tommy unhooked the picture of the Devil and Faust and settled in position.

Conrad did not come. At ten he flung himself on the bed and slept.

The key woke him at eight. The door swung open, and too late he remembered his scheme. A moment later he was glad: it was not Conrad who entered, but a girl carrying a tray. In the feeble gaslight he decided at once that she was one of the most beautiful girls he had ever seen — rich brown hair shot with gold, hazel eyes wide-set, a wild-rose face.

"Are you Jane Finn?" he asked breathlessly.

The girl shook her head. "My name is Annette, monsieur." She spoke in soft, broken English.

*"Française?"*

"Oui, monsieur."

He inspected the tray — loaf, margarine, coffee. "The living is not equal to the *Ritz*. But for what we are about to receive the Lord has made me truly thankful. Amen. — What are you doing in this house?"

"I do the *service*, monsieur."

"Have you heard the name Jane Finn?"

"I have heard people speak of her, I think. — I must go."

The key turned. *That girl might help me*, mused Tommy.

That evening he tried. She was alone. "Annette, help me get out."

"Impossible. There are three of them on the floor below."

"But you would, if you could?"

"No, monsieur. They are my own people. You have spied on them."

"They're a bad lot. I'll take you away. A good whack of money."

"I dare not. I am afraid."

"Wouldn't you save another girl from their clutches? Jane Finn?"

She passed her hand across her forehead. "Jane Finn. Always I hear that name. It is familiar."

"You must know *something*?"

"I know nothing — only the name." She walked toward the door, then uttered a cry. She had caught sight of the picture against the wall. For a moment terror flashed in her eyes; as inexplicably it changed to relief. Then she was gone.

Three more days passed in dreary inaction. The girl had become dumb; a dark suspicion smouldered in her eyes. They were waiting, Tommy gathered, on orders from Mr. Brown.

The third evening brought a rude awakening. Conrad entered with the evil-looking Number 14 and a length of fine cord. In a minute Tommy was a helpless bundle.

"Thought you'd bluffed us, did you?" Conrad spat. "All the time it was bluff. Your number's up now."

"Why not cut my throat without delay?"

"And have the police nosing round? We've ordered the carriage for your lordship to-morrow morning."

An hour later the key turned softly. Annette — with Conrad's voice behind her: "He doesn't want supper to-night."

"But I must take the other tray."

She crossed to the table, raised a hand, turned out the light.

"Curse you — why did you do that?"

"I always turn it out."

"Le beau petit monsieur," she cried gaily, pausing by the bed in the darkness. "You have tied him up well, *hein?* He is like a trussed chicken!" — and as her hand ran lightly over his bonds, she pressed something small and cold into his palm.

The door shut. The object was a penknife, blade open. From her studied avoidance of his eyes and her trick with the light, he understood: there was a peep-hole. He had been observed all along.

He sawed at the cord. The blade cut his wrist badly, but at last it slackened. He bound the wound, unhooked the famous picture again, and waited.

At last, footsteps. The door opened; Conrad went straight to the gas. Number 14 followed — and as he stepped across the threshold, Tommy brought the picture down with terrific force on his head. Number 14 went down amidst a stupendous crash of broken glass. Tommy slipped out, pulled the door, turned the key as Conrad hurled himself against it from inside.

The German's voice came up the stairs. "Gott im Himmel! Conrad, what is it?"

Annette's hand was in his, pointing up a rickety ladder. "Quick — up here!"

In the dusty garret she put her finger to her lips. "They will think you are still inside. The door is too thick." She bent, fastening string to the handle of a cracked jug. "Give me the key. Swing behind the ladder. There's a cupboard in the shadow of the landing — when I've let the others out, *pull!*"

She flitted down with a loud cry: "Mon Dieu! Qu'est-ce qu'il y a?"

Tommy crouched behind the cupboard. "Mon Dieu, voilà la clef!" The German snatched it; Conrad stumbled out swearing.

"Where is he?"

"We have seen no one."

"He's got away."

"Impossible. He would have passed us."

Tommy pulled the string. A crash of crockery sounded above; in a trice the men were scrambling up the ladder.

He leapt out and dashed down, pulling the girl with him. He fumbled the bolts; the door swung open. He turned. Annette had disappeared.

He fumed, but would not go without her. Suddenly her voice floated down, clear and high:

"Ma foi, he has escaped! And quickly! Who would have thought it?"

Was that a command to him to go?

Then, louder still: "This is a terrible house. I want to go back to Marguerite. To Marguerite. *To Marguerite!*"

He ran back to the stairs — but Conrad was leaping down with a savage cry. Tommy stopped his rush with a straight blow on the point of the jaw; Conrad fell like a log. The second man tripped over him. From higher up, a flash; a bullet grazed Tommy's ear.

He leapt for the door. A baker's van waited at the kerb — evidently meant to bear his body out of London. He felled the driver and took to his heels as a hail of bullets followed. He stumbled over a prostrate figure who started up with a yell of alarm and dashed off down the street; Tommy drew back into a doorway and had the pleasure of seeing his pursuers track the red herring.

Half-past five; growing light. A passing policeman eyed him suspiciously, and Tommy laughed — he had not shaved or washed in three days. A Turkish bath, then breakfast at an A.B.C. shop. Reading the morning paper, he stiffened. A long article on Kramenin — the "man behind Bolshevism," just arrived in London — and in the centre of the page his portrait.

"So that's who Number 1 is," said Tommy with his mouth full of eggs and bacon.

At Whitehall he was admitted to the man who did not there go by the name of Mr. Carter, and detailed the last few days. Halfway through, Carter gave cryptic orders by telephone.

"Number 1 is Kramenin? Important. We want something against him badly. Look through these photos."

Tommy held one up.

"Ah, Westway! Poses as moderate." Carter handed him another. Tommy exclaimed. "I'm right then. Irishman. Prominent Unionist M.P. — all a blind. The 29th gives us very little time. We can deal with the General Strike menace — a sporting chance. But if that draft treaty turns up, England will be plunged in anarchy."

The Soho house had been emptied of every paper. No sign of Annette.

"You believe she deliberately went back?"

"It would seem so."

"H'm. She must belong to the gang — but, being a woman, didn't feel like standing by to see a personable young man killed."

"I can't believe she's really one of them, sir. She seemed so different —"

"Good-looking, I suppose?" said Carter, with a smile that made Tommy flush. "By the way — have you shown yourself to Miss Tuppence? She's been bombarding me with letters."

"Did she go to the police?"

Carter shook his head.

"Then I wonder how they twigged me."

"Curious. Unless the mention of the *Ritz* was accidental. — Remember, you're a marked man now."

In the taxi to the *Ritz* Tommy dwelt on the pleasure of startling Tuppence. Marguerite must be Mrs. Vandemeyer; the thought saddened him — it seemed to prove Annette was on intimate terms with that woman.

The taxi drew up. Tommy burst into the sacred portals — and his enthusiasm received a check. Miss Cowley had gone out a quarter of an hour ago.

## CHAPTER XVIII. THE TELEGRAM

Baffled for the moment, Tommy strolled into the restaurant and ordered a meal of surpassing excellence. His four days' imprisonment had taught him anew to value good food.

He was conveying a particularly choice morsel of Sole à la Jeanette to his mouth when he caught sight of Julius entering the room. At the sight of Tommy, Julius's eyes seemed as though they would pop out of his head. He strode across and pump-handled Tommy's hand with what seemed quite unnecessary vigour.

"Holy snakes!" he ejaculated. "Is it really you?"

"Of course it is. Why shouldn't it be?"

"Why shouldn't it be? Say, man, don't you know you've been given up for dead? I guess we'd have had a solemn requiem for you in another few days."

"Who thought I was dead?"

"Tuppence."

"She remembered the proverb about the good dying young, I suppose. There must be a certain amount of original sin in me to have survived. Where is Tuppence, by the way?"

"Isn't she here? I dropped her here in the car about an hour ago. But, say, can't you shed that British calm of yours, and get down to it?"

Julius drew up a chair, and Tommy plunged into his recital. Julius listened spellbound, forgetting half the dishes placed before him. At the end he heaved a long sigh.

"Bully for you. Reads like a dime novel!"

In his turn Julius narrated the home front — his unsuccessful reconnaissance at Bournemouth, the call upon Sir James, the sensational events of the previous night, Mrs. Vandemeyer's death, and the discovery that Jane Finn, when at last traced, had entirely lost her memory.

"But who killed her?"

"The doctor kidded himself she took it herself. Sir James, being a legal luminary, is likewise a human oyster. He 'reserved judgment.'" Julius detailed the morning's events.

"Lost her memory, eh?" said Tommy with interest. "By Jove, that explains why they looked at me so queerly when I spoke of questioning her."

"There's no doubt it was chloral?"

"They call it heart failure induced by an overdose. We don't want to be worried with an inquest. But Tuppence and I and even the highbrow Sir James have all got the same idea."

"Mr. Brown?"

"Sure thing."

Tommy nodded. "All the same, Mr. Brown hasn't got wings. I don't see how he got in and out."

"How about some high-class thought transference stunt? Some magnetic influence that irresistibly impelled Mrs. Vandemeyer to commit suicide?"

"Distinctly good, Julius. Especially the phraseology. But it leaves me cold. I yearn for a real Mr. Brown of flesh and blood. I think the gifted young detectives must get to work, study the entrances and exits, and tap the bumps on their foreheads until the solution dawns on them. Let's go round to the scene of the crime. I wish we could get hold of Tuppence."

Inquiry at the office revealed that Tuppence had not yet returned. Julius went upstairs to look in his sitting-room. Suddenly a diminutive page-boy spoke at Tommy's elbow.

"The young lady — she's gone away by train, I think, sir. I heard her tell the driver Charing Cross and to look sharp. So I thought, having asked for an A.B.C. and a Bradshaw."

"When did she ask for those?"

"When I took her up the telegram, sir. About half-past twelve. She opened it and gave a gasp, and then she said, very jolly like, 'Bring me up a Bradshaw and an A.B.C., and look sharp.' She looked something up, looked at the clock, called for a taxi, and was down in two ticks."

At that moment Julius rejoined him, an open letter in his hand.

"I say, Hersheimmer — Tuppence has gone off sleuthing on her own. She went off in a taxi to Charing Cross in the deuce of a hurry after getting a telegram." His eye fell on the letter. "Oh, she left a note for you. Where's she off to?"

Almost unconsciously, he held out his hand for the letter, but Julius folded it up and placed it in his pocket. He seemed a trifle embarrassed.

"I guess this is nothing to do with it. It's about something else — something I asked her that she was to let me know about."

"Oh!" Tommy looked puzzled.

"See here," said Julius suddenly, "I'd better put you wise. I asked Miss Tuppence to marry me this morning."

"Oh!" said Tommy mechanically. He felt dazed. For the moment Julius's words benumbed his brain.

"I'd like to tell you that before I suggested anything of the kind to Miss Tuppence, I made it clear that I didn't want to butt in in any way between her and you — "

Tommy roused himself. "That's all right. Tuppence and I have been pals for years. Nothing more." He lit a cigarette with a hand that shook ever so little.

"Oh, I guess it'll be the dollars that'll do the trick. Miss Tuppence put me wise to that right away. There's no humbug about her. We ought to gee along together very well."

Tommy looked at him curiously. Tuppence and Julius! Well, why not? Had she not lamented that she knew no rich men? Had she not openly avowed her intention of marrying for money if she ever had the chance?

Nevertheless, Tommy did blame her. He was filled with a passionate and utterly illogical resentment. It was all very well to *say* things like that — but a *real* girl would never marry for money. Tuppence was utterly cold-blooded and selfish, and he would be delighted if he never saw her again! And it was a rotten world!

"Yes, we ought to gee along together very well," Julius continued. "I've heard that a girl always refuses you once — a sort of convention."

Tommy caught his arm. "Refuses? Did you say *refuses*?"

"Sure thing. She just rapped out a 'no' without any reason to it. But she'll come round right enough."

"What did she say in that note?" Tommy demanded fiercely.

The obliging Julius handed it to him. The note, in Tuppence's well-known schoolboy writing, ran as follows:

"DEAR JULIUS,

"It's always better to have things in black and white. I don't feel I can be bothered to think of marriage until Tommy is found. Let's leave it till then.

"Yours affectionately,

"TUPPENCE."

Tommy handed it back, his eyes shining. His feelings had undergone a sharp reaction. Tuppence was all that was noble and disinterested. Had she not refused Julius without hesitation? Darling Tuppence, there was not a girl in the world to touch her!

"There's not a hint here as to what she's up to," he remarked, pulling himself together. He turned to the page-boy and produced five shillings. "One thing more. What did the young lady do with the telegram?"

"She crumpled it up into a ball and threw it into the grate, sir, and made a sort of noise like 'Whoop!'"

"Very graphic. Here's your five shillings. Come on, Julius."

They hurried upstairs. In the fireplace was a crumpled ball of orange and white. Tommy disentangled it and smoothed out the telegram.

*"Come at once, Moat House, Ebury, Yorkshire, great developments — TOMMY."*

They looked at each other in stupefaction.

"You didn't send it?"

"Of course not. What does it mean?"

"I guess it means the worst," said Julius quietly. "They've got her. They signed your name, and she fell into the trap like a lamb."

"My God! What shall we do?"

"Get busy, and go after her! It's almighty luck that she didn't take the wire with her. Where's that Bradshaw?"

After a few muttered imprecations Julius handed the Bradshaw to Tommy, who abandoned it in favour of an A.B.C.

"Ebury, Yorks. From King's Cross. (Boy must have made a mistake. It was King's Cross, not *Charing* Cross.) 12.50, that's the train she went by. 3.20 is the next — and a damned slow train too."

"She's a hostage, that's what she is," Tommy said as they prepared to leave. "She's in no immediate danger, because if we tumble on to anything, she'd be damned useful to them. As long as they've got her, they've got the whip hand of us. Besides, I've great faith in Tuppence."

The journey was wearisome, with many stops and crowded carriages. They had to change twice. Ebury was a deserted station with a solitary porter, who gave meticulous but perplexing directions to the Moat House. It was beginning to rain. Tommy ran back to the porter to ask about a young lady from the 12.50 train. Several people had arrived; he could call to mind no one in particular, but was certain no one had asked the way to the Moat House.

Depression settled on Tommy like a leaden weight. The enemy had over three hours' start — more than enough for Mr. Brown.

The way seemed endless. It was past seven o'clock when they came in sight of the house. A rusty iron gate swung dismally on its hinges; an overgrown drive lay thick with leaves. Something about the place struck a chill to both their hearts. The leaves deadened their footsteps. The daylight was almost gone. It was like walking in a world of ghosts. Overhead the branches flapped and creaked with a mournful note. A turn of the drive brought them in sight of the house, also empty and deserted, the shutters closed, the steps overgrown with moss. Was it indeed to this desolate spot that Tuppence had been decoyed?

Julius jerked the rusty bell handle. A jangling peal rang discordantly through the emptiness within. No one came. They rang again and again. They walked completely round the house. Everywhere silence and shuttered windows.

"Nothing doing," said Julius.

In the village they obtained the key from a woman at a cottage near the post office, who told them the place had been empty for years. As the front door swung back, protesting loudly, Julius struck a match and examined the floor.

"I'd swear no one's passed this way. Look at the dust. Thick. Not a sign of a footmark."

They wandered round the deserted house. Everywhere the same tale. Thick layers of dust, undisturbed.

"This gets me. I don't believe Tuppence was ever in this house."

"She must have been."

On the morrow they searched again, and were reluctantly forced to the conclusion that the house had not been invaded for some considerable time. They might have left altogether but for a fortunate discovery. As they retraced their steps to the gate, Tommy gave a sudden cry, and stooping, picked something up from among the leaves. It was a small gold brooch.

"That's Tuppence's! Absolutely. I've often seen her wear it."

Julius drew a deep breath. "I guess that settles it. She came as far as here, anyway. We'll make that pub our head-quarters, and raise hell round here until we find her."

The campaign began. Tommy and Julius worked separately and together, but the result was the same. Nobody answering Tuppence's description had been seen in the vicinity. Tuppence had certainly not remained long; that pointed to her being overcome and carried away in a car. Again they met no success. Julius wired to town for his own car, and they scoured the neighbourhood daily with unflagging zeal. Every car that had passed through the village on the fateful day was tracked down. Julius forced his way into country properties and submitted the owners to searching cross-examination. So well had the abduction been planned that the girl seemed literally to have vanished into thin air.

And another preoccupation was weighing on Tommy's mind.

"Do you know how long we've been here?" he asked one morning at breakfast. "A week! We're no nearer to finding Tuppence, *and next Sunday is the 29th!*"

"Shucks! I'd almost forgotten about the 29th. I've been thinking of nothing but Tuppence."

"To-day's the 23rd, and time's getting short. If we're ever going to get hold of her at all, we must do it before the 29th — her life won't be worth an hour's purchase afterwards. The hostage game will be played out by then. We've made a big mistake in the way we've set about this."

"I'm with you there. We've been a couple of mutts. I'm going to do what we ought to have done a week ago. I'm going right back to London to put the case in the hands of your British police. Sleuths! It was a piece of damn-fool foolishness! Scotland Yard for me!"

"You're right," said Tommy slowly. "I wish to God we'd gone there right away."

"Are you coming along?"

Tommy shook his head. "What's the good? One of us is enough. I might as well stay here and nose round a bit longer. Something *might* turn up."

But the course of events was not to follow Julius's plan. Later in the day Tommy received a wire:

*"Join me Manchester Midland Hotel. Important news — JULIUS."*

At 7.30 that night Tommy alighted from a slow cross-country train. Julius was on the platform.

Tommy grasped him by the arm. "What is it? Is Tuppence found?"

Julius shook his head. "No. But I found this waiting in London. Just arrived."

He handed the telegraph form to him. Tommy's eyes opened as he read:

*"Jane Finn found. Come Manchester Midland Hotel immediately — PEEL EDGERTON."*

Julius took the form back and folded it up.

"Queer," he said thoughtfully. "I thought that lawyer chap had quit!"

## CHAPTER XIX. JANE FINN

It was Sir James Peel Edgerton, however, who reached them first, with a wire summoning them both to Manchester. Julius had got in half an hour earlier and led the way out of the station as Tommy arrived.

"He's booked rooms for us, and will be round to dine at eight."

"What made you think he'd ceased to take any interest in the case?" Tommy asked curiously.

"What he said," Julius replied dryly. "The old bird's as close as an oyster. Wouldn't commit himself till he was sure he could deliver the goods."

Tommy shook his head, unconvinced.

Sir James arrived punctually. "I have heard so much about you from Miss Tuppence," he said, shaking Tommy's hand warmly, "that it really seems as though I already know you quite well."

Tommy felt at once the magnetism of the lawyer's personality. He was reminded of Mr. Carter — the same keen-edged quality of mind beneath the professional reserve. He was conscious, too, of being read through and through, while learning nothing in return. Sir James took in everything, but gave out only what he chose.

Julius broke into eager questions. How had Sir James tracked the girl? Why had he kept silent?

The lawyer stroked his chin and smiled. "Just so, just so. Well, she's found. That's the great thing, isn't it?"

"Sure it is. But just how did you strike her trail? We thought you'd quit for good."

"Did you really? H'm, dear me." He shot a lightning glance. "Well, I don't know that I should go so far as to say that."

"But where is she?"

"The young lady was knocked down in a street accident, and has sustained slight injuries to the head. She was taken to the infirmary, and on recovering consciousness gave her name as Jane Finn. I arranged for her to be removed to the house of a friend, and wired at once for you. She has not spoken since."

"Her memory's come back?" cried Julius.

"Undoubtedly, since she was able to give her real name."

"And you just happened to be on the spot," said Tommy. "Seems quite like a fairy tale."

"Coincidences are curious things," said Sir James dryly.

But Tommy was now certain of what he had only suspected. Sir James had not abandoned the case at all; by some means of his own, he had run the missing girl to earth.

"After dinner," Julius announced, "I'll go right away and see Jane."

"Quite impossible. They would not allow visitors at this hour. To-morrow morning at ten."

"All the same, I reckon I'll go round to-night and ginger them up."

"It will be quite useless, Mr. Hersheimmer."

The words came out like the crack of a pistol. For a moment hostility flared between the two men; then Julius lowered his eyes. "For the moment, I reckon you're the boss."

Sir James turned smoothly to Tommy. He had heard, he said, that his friends had been anxious. Helped by questions, Tommy gave an abbreviated account of his adventures, including the moment near the end when a girl in the house had cried out, wanting to be taken to 'Marguerite.'

"You got yourself out of a tight place very well," Sir James said gravely. "I congratulate you."

"I couldn't have got away but for the girl, sir."

"It was lucky for you she happened to take a fancy to you. There's no doubt about her being one of the gang?"

"I'm afraid not. She went back to them when she could have got away."

Sir James nodded. "Mrs. Vandemeyer always signed herself Rita — never Marguerite. Curious that the girl was crying out for her by her full name at the very moment Vandemeyer was either dead or dying. There are one or two points that strike me as obscure. The house was raided?"

"They'd all cleared out. Not a clue left behind."

"I wonder——" The lawyer tapped the table. "And since then? What have you been doing?"

For a moment Tommy stared. Then it dawned on him — Sir James didn't know.

"I forgot you didn't know about Tuppence."

The lawyer laid down his knife and fork sharply. "Has anything happened to Miss Tuppence?"

"She's disappeared," said Julius. "A week ago."

Between them they gave the history of the futile search. Sir James went at once to the root of the matter.

"A wire signed with your name? Their kidnapping of Miss Tuppence is the counter-move to your escape. They could seal your lips with a threat of what might happen to her."

"That's just what I thought, sir."

"Not bad. The curious thing is that they certainly did not know anything about you when they first held you prisoner. Some one put them wise — and not earlier than Sunday afternoon."

"That almighty omniscient Mr. Brown, of course!" There was a note of derision in Julius's voice.

"You don't believe in Mr. Brown?"

"No, sir. I reckon he's a figurehead — a bogy name to frighten the children. The real head is that Russian, Kramenin."

"I disagree. Mr. Brown exists." Sir James turned to Tommy. "We'll deal with Miss Jane Finn first. Afterwards, we'll set to work to rescue Miss Tuppence. She's in no immediate danger so long as they don't know we've got Jane Finn, and that her memory has returned. We must keep that dark at all costs."

At ten the next morning they were shown upstairs. Tommy's heart beat sensibly faster. Jane Finn at last! The long-sought, the elusive Jane Finn — the girl who held the future of England in her hands. If only Tuppence could have been at his side.

On the white bed, bandages round her head, lay the girl. The whole scene seemed unreal — so exactly what one expected that it gave the effect of being beautifully staged.

"Miss Finn," said Sir James, "this is your cousin, Mr. Julius P. Hersheimmer."

A faint flush flitted over the girl's face. "Are you really Uncle Hiram's son?"

Her voice, with its slight Western warmth, had an almost thrilling quality. It seemed vaguely familiar to Tommy, but he thrust the impression aside as impossible.

"Sure thing," said Julius, with a tremor in his voice.

"They've been telling me dreadful things — that my memory went, and that there are years lost out of my life. It seems no time since we were being hustled into those boats."

"Don't worry. Now, Jane — there was a man on that boat with some mighty important papers. The big guns in this country have a notion he passed them to you."

"Yes. He said they'd have a better chance with me, because they would save the women and children first. He said they might make all the difference to the Allies. Then you can hand them over right away?"

"But I haven't got them. I hid them. I got uneasy. People seemed to be watching me."

"Go on," said Sir James in his quiet penetrating tones. "What do you remember?"

"It was at Holyhead. In the confusion on the quay I slipped away. I took a car, told the man to drive me out of the town. The path led to the cliff, and down to the sea between big yellow gorse bushes — they were like golden flames. Just level with my head was a hole in the rock — small, but it went a long way back. I took the oilskin packet from round my neck and shoved it right in. Then I tore off a bit of gorse and plugged the hole. I marked the place in my own mind. There was a queer boulder in the path just there — for all the world like a dog sitting up begging. I went back and caught the train. By and by, I saw the man opposite me wink at the woman next to me, and was glad the papers were safe. I went out in the corridor. The woman called me back, said I'd dropped something, and when I stooped, something seemed to hit me — here." She placed her hand to the back of her head. "I don't remember anything more until I woke up in the hospital."

"Thank you, Miss Finn," said Sir James.

Julius took her hand. "So long, Cousin Jane. I'm going to get busy after those papers."

## CHAPTER XX. TOO LATE

In the street they held an informal council. Sir James drew out his watch.

"The boat train to Holyhead stops at Chester at 12.14. If you start at once you can catch the connection. I am due to speak at a meeting at two o'clock — unfortunate."

"Nothing complicated about this deal," Julius remarked. "Just a game of hide-and-seek."

"You are still young, Mr. Hersheimmer. At my age you will probably have learnt one lesson. 'Never underestimate your adversary.'"

"You think Mr. Brown might come along? I'm ready for him." Julius slapped his pocket and produced a murderous-looking automatic. "Little Willie travels round with me everywhere. But there's nobody to put Mr. Brown wise."

"There was nobody to put Mr. Brown wise to the fact that Mrs. Vandemeyer meant to betray him. Nevertheless, *Mrs. Vandemeyer died without speaking*."

Julius was silenced.

"Take no unnecessary risks once the papers are in your hands. If there is any reason to believe you have been shadowed, destroy them at once. The game is in your hands now."

For a long time on the train neither spoke. When at length Julius broke the silence, it was with a totally unexpected remark.

"Say — did you ever make a darned fool of yourself over a girl's face?"

"Can't say I have. Why?"

"Because for the last two months I've been making a sentimental idiot of myself over Jane. First moment I clapped eyes on her photograph my heart did all the usual stunts. I came over here determined to find her and take her back as Mrs. Julius P. Hersheimmer."

"Oh!" said Tommy, amazed.

"Just shows what an almighty fool a man can make of himself. One look at the girl in the flesh, and I was cured. No disparagement to Jane, mind you. She's a real nice girl. But she's not like her photo one bit."

"I thought her a very good-looking girl."

"Sure she is. But there was something about that photo——" Julius shook his head. "I guess romance is a mighty queer thing."

"It must be," said Tommy coldly, "if you can come over here in love with one girl, and propose to another within a fortnight."

Julius looked discomposed. "Well, I'd got a tired feeling I'd never find Jane. The French set about marriage in a businesslike way——"

"If you ask me," said Tommy, "we're all too damned businesslike nowadays."

"Cool down, son."

Tommy had cooled by the time they reached Holyhead. They hired a taxi and drove out toward Treaddur Bay, watching narrowly for the path. When Tommy spotted it, he stopped the car promptly, asked casually if it led down to the sea, and paid off the man. They watched the taxi out of sight, then turned to the narrow path.

"It's the right one, I suppose?"

"Sure it is. Look at the gorse."

The hedges of golden blossom bordered the path on either side. They went down in single file, Julius leading. Twice Tommy turned uneasily.

"What is it?"

"I've got the wind up. Keep fancying there's some one following us."

"Can't be. We'd see him."

The path ran along the side of the cliff, parallel to the sea. Suddenly Julius came to such an abrupt halt that Tommy cannoned into him. Standing out half obstructing the path was a huge boulder bearing a fanciful resemblance to a "begging" terrier.

"It's what we expected to see, isn't it?" said Tommy.

"Sure we expected it — but it kind of rattles me to see it sitting there just where we expected!"

Tommy pointed with a shaking hand. "What about that crevice there?"

"That's it — for sure."

"Damn it!" Tommy cried. "Five years! Bird's-nesting boys, picnic parties, thousands of people passing! It can't be there!" The thing was too easy, therefore it could not be.

"I guess you're rattled now all right," Julius drawled. "Well, here goes!" He thrust his hand into the crevice. "Tight fit. Don't feel anything — no — say, what's this? Gee whiz!" And with a flourish he waved aloft a small discoloured packet sewn up in oilskin. "Hold it while I get my penknife."

Tommy held the precious packet tenderly. They had succeeded.

"It's queer," he murmured. "You'd think the stitches would have rotted. They look just as good as new."

They cut them and ripped away the oilskin. Inside was a small folded sheet of paper. With trembling fingers they unfolded it.

The sheet was blank.

"A dummy?" hazarded Julius.

Tommy's face cleared. "I've got it! *Sympathetic ink!* Heat usually does the trick. Get some sticks."

In a few minutes a little fire of twigs was blazing. Tommy held the sheet near the glow. The paper curled. Then Julius grasped his arm and pointed to where characters were appearing in faint brown.

"Gee whiz! You've got it!"

Tommy held the paper longer until he judged the heat had done its work. Then he uttered a cry.

Across the sheet in neat brown printing ran the words: WITH THE COMPLIMENTS OF MR. BROWN.

## CHAPTER XXI. TOMMY MAKES A DISCOVERY

For a moment they stood staring at each other, dazed.

"How in tarnation did he get ahead of us?" Julius ended.

"It accounts for the stitches being new. We might have guessed."

"It's downright impossible for anyone to get here quicker than we did. And how did he know? Was there a dictaphone in Jane's room?"

"No one could have known beforehand that she'd be in that house — much less that particular room."

"Then one of the nurses listened at the door. The game's up. We've failed."

"There's only one thing for me to do," Tommy said. "Get back to London. Mr. Carter must be warned."

Half an hour after his arrival, haggard and pale, Tommy stood before his chief.

"I've come to report. I've failed."

"You mean the treaty——"

"Is in the hands of Mr. Brown, sir."

"Ah!" said Mr. Carter quietly. The expression on his face did not change, but Tommy caught the flicker of despair in his eyes.

"Don't take it to heart, lad. You did your best. You were up against one of the biggest brains of the century, and you came very near success."

"Thank you, sir."

"I have been blaming myself ever since I heard this other news."

A new fear gripped Tommy's heart. "Is there — something more, sir?"

Mr. Carter stretched out his hand to a sheet on the table. "Read for yourself."

The typewritten words danced before Tommy's eyes — the description of a green toque, a coat with a handkerchief in the pocket marked P.L.C.

"Tuppence——?"

"Washed up on the Yorkshire coast. I'm afraid — it looks very much like foul play."

"My God!" gasped Tommy. "*Tuppence!* Those devils — I'll never rest till I've got even with them!"

"I know what you feel, my poor boy. But it's no good. Cut your losses. Time's merciful. You'll forget."

"Forget Tuppence? Never!"

"That brave little girl. I'm confoundedly sorry."

Back at the *Ritz*, Tommy packed mechanically. *Tuppence — dead!* Little Tuppence, brimming over with life. It was a dream — it couldn't be true.

A note came: a few kind words of sympathy from Peel Edgerton, who had read the news. The letter ended with the offer of a post on a ranch in the Argentine.

The door opened, and Julius burst in with his usual violence, an open paper in his hand.

"Say — they seem to have got some fool idea about Tuppence."

"It's true."

"You mean they've done her in?"

Tommy nodded. "When they got the treaty she wasn't any good to them, and they were afraid to let her go."

"Well, I'm darned! Little Tuppence. She sure was the pluckiest——"

Something cracked in Tommy's brain. He rose to his feet.

"Oh, get out! You don't really care, damn you! You asked her to marry you in your rotten cold-blooded way, but I *loved* her. I'd have given the soul out of my body to save her from harm. I'd have stood by without a word and let her marry you, because you could have given her the sort of time she ought to have had, and I was only a poor devil without a penny. But it wouldn't have been because I didn't care!"

"See here——" Julius began.

"Oh, go to the devil! Tuppence is my girl! I've always loved her, from the time we played together as kids. I shall never forget when I was in hospital, and she came in in that ridiculous cap and apron — like a miracle to see the girl I loved turn up in a nurse's kit——"

"A nurse's kit!" Julius shouted. "Gee whiz! I could swear I've seen Jane in a nurse's cap too. By gum, I've got it! It was her I saw talking to Whittington at that nursing home in Bournemouth. She wasn't a patient there! She was a nurse!"

"I dare say she's been in with them from the start. I shouldn't wonder if she stole those papers from Danvers to begin with."

"I'm darned if she did! She's my cousin, and as patriotic a girl as ever stepped."

"I don't care a damn what she is — get out of here!"

The young men were on the point of blows. Then, with magical abruptness, Julius's anger abated.

"All right, son. I'm going. It's mighty lucky you did say it. I've been the most almighty blithering darned idiot. I'm going right away — to the London and North Western Railway depot, if you want to know."

"I don't care a damn where you're going."

When the door closed, Tommy returned to his suit-case. He re-read Sir James's letter and shook his head. Tuppence must be avenged.

"Better answer it." He went to the writing-table — innumerable envelopes and no paper. He remembered there was a supply in Julius's sitting-room. Julius had announced his immediate departure; there was no fear of running into him. He was beginning to be ashamed of what he had said. He'd apologize if he found him there.

But the room was deserted. Tommy walked to the writing-table and opened the middle drawer. A photograph, carelessly thrust in face upwards, caught his eye. For a moment he stood rooted to the ground. Then he took it out, walked slowly to an arm-chair, and sat down still staring at the photograph in his hand.

What on earth was a photograph of the French girl Annette doing in Julius Hersheimmer's writing-table?

## CHAPTER XXII. IN DOWNING STREET

The Prime Minister tapped the desk with nervous fingers. "Do you really mean things are not so desperate after all?"

"So this lad seems to think." Mr. Carter handed the letter across. It was written in a sprawling boyish hand.

> DEAR MR. CARTER,
>
> Something's turned up that has given me a jar. If my conclusions are right, that girl at Manchester was just a plant — the whole thing prearranged to make us think the game was up. Therefore we must have been pretty hot on the scent.
>
> I think I know who the real Jane Finn is, and I've an idea where the papers are. I enclose it in a sealed envelope; please don't open it until midnight on the 28th. Tuppence's drowning is a plant too. As a last chance they'll let Jane Finn escape, hoping she'll lead them to the cache; but if they know the papers have been recovered, neither girl's life will be worth an hour's purchase.
>
> I want a repeat of that telegram sent to Tuppence at the Ritz. And please have that house in Soho watched day and night.
>
> Yours,
> THOMAS BERESFORD.

"The enclosure?"

"In the vaults of the Bank. I am taking no chances."

"Hadn't we better open it now? Secure the document."

"Once it's known, I wouldn't give that"—Carter snapped his fingers—"for the lives of those two girls. The boy trusted me; I shan't let him down."

"What's he like?"

"Outwardly an ordinary clean-limbed, rather block-headed young Englishman, slow in his mental processes — but impossible to lead astray through his imagination; he hasn't got any. The little lady's quite different. More intuition and less common sense. Pace and stamina."

"And it is this — boy who will defeat the master criminal of our time?"

"This boy, as you say. But I sometimes fancy I see a shadow behind. Peel Edgerton — working in the dark. I've always felt that if anyone was to run Mr. Brown to earth, Peel Edgerton would be the man. He sent me an odd request the other day — a cutting about a man's body found near the docks in New York, badly disfigured, never identified."

"You think the matters connected?"

"Somehow I do."

Sir James was shown in. The Premier rose with a half-whimsical thought: *My successor, perhaps.*

"You've seen young Beresford, I suppose?" said Carter.

"You suppose wrong. He rang me up." Sir James stroked his chin. "He thanked me for a letter offering him a job, then reminded me of something I'd said at Manchester about that bogus telegram. He had discovered a photograph in a drawer in Mr. Hersheimmer's room. I asked if it bore a Californian photographer's mark. He replied: 'You're on to it, sir. It had.' The original was the French girl, Annette, who saved his life."

"What?"

"Exactly. He had put the photograph back where he found it — distinctly good, that. Once Manchester was proved a plant, everything was altered. He couldn't trust his judgment about Miss Cowley, however. I told him there was a very decided chance she was alive, and advised him to apply to you for the original wire."

Carter took a sheet out and read aloud: "Come at once, Astley Priors, Gatehouse, Kent. Great developments — TOMMY."

"Very simple, and very ingenious. The one important clue they overlooked — the page-boy's statement that Miss Cowley drove to Charing Cross. They took it for granted he had made a mistake."

"Then young Beresford is now—"

"At Gatehouse, Kent."

"I rather wonder you're not there too."

"I'm busy on a case. Any more facts about that American chap?"

"Is it important to find out who he was?"

"Oh, I know who he was. I can't prove it yet — but I know."

The other two asked no questions.

"But how," said the Prime Minister suddenly, "did that photograph come to be in Mr. Hersheimmer's drawer?"

"Perhaps it never left it," suggested the lawyer gently.

"But the bogus inspector — Inspector Brown, who conducted the search?"

"Ah," said Sir James thoughtfully. He rose. "I mustn't keep you. I must get back to my case."

Two days later Julius Hersheimmer returned from Manchester. A note from Tommy lay on his table: sorry for losing his temper, off to take a job in the Argentine.

A peculiar smile lingered on Julius's face. He threw the letter into the waste-paper basket.

"The darned fool!" he murmured.

## CHAPTER XXIII. A RACE AGAINST TIME

After ringing up Sir James, Tommy enlisted Tuppence's friend Albert, and the two took quarters at the inn in Gatehouse. Astley Priors, the village volunteered, was the property of a Dr. Adams — retired, but kept a few private patients ("balmy ones — you understand"). A popular figure, given to subscriptions, "a very pleasant, affable gentleman." Scientific; always visitors.

The volubility raised Tommy's doubts. Suppose it was all a gigantic mistake? He inquired carefully if there was a young lady amongst the patients, describing Tuppence and Annette. Nothing. The patients were seldom seen outside the grounds.

That evening they explored. The grounds, like those of any private house after nightfall, seemed untenanted. They reached a shrubbery near the house unmolested. The blinds of the dining-room window were up. A normal, pleasant company sat round the table; through the open window floated a heated discussion of county cricket. Again Tommy felt the cold chill of uncertainty. The fair-bearded gentleman at the head of the table looked singularly honest.

The next morning Albert took the place of the greengrocer's boy and ingratiated himself with the cook. Repeating the trick the following day, he brought confirmation: a French young lady was staying in the house.

Time pressed. To-day was the 27th; the 29th, the much-talked-of "Labour Day," loomed. Newspapers were full of sensational hints of a Labour *coup d'état*. With the fatal document in Mr. Brown's hands, public opinion would swing to the extremists. Failing that, the battle was even — at the cost of great suffering. But Tommy nourished a more preposterous dream: with Mr. Brown unmasked, the whole organization would crumble in panic. The strange permeating influence of the unseen chief held it together.

"This is a one-man show," said Tommy to himself. "The thing to do is to get hold of the man."

That evening they once more penetrated the grounds. As they approached, Tommy gave a sudden gasp. On the second floor a silhouette stood between window and light. He would have known it anywhere. Tuppence was in that house.

"Stay here. When I begin to sing, watch that window."

He retreated to the main drive, and began with a deep roar, coupled with an unsteady gait:

> *I am a Soldier*
> *A jolly British Soldier;*
> *You can see that I'm a Soldier by my feet…*

It had been a favourite on the gramophone in Tuppence's hospital days. Tommy had not a note of music in his voice, but his lungs were excellent.

Presently an unimpeachable butler issued from the front door with an equally unimpeachable footman. They ran him neatly down the drive and out of the gate, threatening police if he intruded again. It was beautifully done — only, as it happened, the butler was Whittington.

Tommy retired to the inn. Albert reappeared with a scrap of paper wrapped round a letterweight: *To-morrow — same time.*

"I wrote a message and chucked it through the window," he added breathlessly. "Said we was at the inn. If she could get away, to come there and croak like a frog."

"Your zeal will be the undoing of us, Albert. That butler's an old friend of mine — I bet he knew who I was. It's not their game to show suspicion. They don't want to discourage me altogether. I'm a pawn in their game. If the spider lets the fly walk out too easily, the fly might suspect a put-up job. But later, Mr. T. Beresford had better look out."

About twelve, his calm was shaken. Someone was demanding him in the bar — a rude-looking carter coated with mud.

"Might this be for you, sir?" The note was very dirty: *Take this to the gentleman at the inn near Astley Priors. He will give you ten shillings.*

The handwriting was Tuppence's. Tommy paid him and unfastened it.

> DEAR TOMMY,
> I knew it was you last night. Don't go this evening. They'll be lying in wait for you. They're taking us away this morning. I heard something about Wales — Holyhead, I think. I'll drop this on the road if I get a chance. Annette told me how you'd escaped. Buck up.
> Yours,
> TWOPENCE.

"Pack my bag!" Tommy shouted. "We're off!"

Holyhead. Did that mean—? He read the note again slowly. The boots above continued busy.

"Albert! I'm a damned fool! Unpack that bag!"

He smoothed out the note thoughtfully.

"Yes, a damned fool. But so's some one else. And at last I know who it is."

## CHAPTER XXIV. JULIUS TAKES A HAND

In his suite at Claridge's, Kramenin was informed that a Mr. Julius P. Hersheimmer was below. The name promised a millionaire several times over.

"Bring him up."

Julius was escorted in. "Monsieur Kramenin? I've got some very important business I'd like to talk over with you, if I can see you alone."

The secretary was dismissed. Julius watched him go, closed the door, and came back to the centre of the room.

"Now, Mr. Hersheimmer, perhaps you will be so kind as to come to the point."

"I guess that won't take a minute." Then, with an abrupt change of manner: "Hands up — or I shoot."

Kramenin stared into the big automatic, then with almost comical haste flung up his hands. In that instant Julius had taken his measure: an abject physical coward — the rest would be easy.

"This is an outrage!" cried Kramenin in a high hysterical voice. "Do you mean to kill me?"

"Not if you keep your voice down. I'm not proposing to kill you this trip — that is, if you're reasonable."

"What do you want? Money?"

"No. I want Jane Finn."

"Jane Finn? I — never heard of her."

"You're a darned liar. And Little Willie here is just hopping mad to go off."

The Russian wilted. "Granted I do know who you mean — what of it?"

"You will tell me where she is to be found."

"I daren't. Mr. Brown — he is a man to fear. I have seen him. Spoken to him face to face. I did not know it until afterwards. He was one of a crowd. He knows everything, and his vengeance is swift. Even I — Kramenin! — would not be exempt."

"He'll never know," said Julius. He raised the revolver. "I'm going to count five. One — two — three — four—"

"Do not shoot! I will do all you wish. The girl is at Gatehouse, in Kent. Astley Priors, the place is called. The little fool has lost her memory, curse her."

"And the other girl, the one you decoyed away over a week ago?"

"She's there too."

"Isn't it all panning out beautifully? And a lovely night for the run."

"What run?"

"Down to Gatehouse. I'm not such a kid as to leave you here to ring up your friends."

The Russian was hustled into a coat and down to the waiting car. Julius warned him that the chauffeur — a naval man whose brother had been murdered by Bolsheviks — would not assist any escape. To pass the time on the run, Julius narrated, with picturesque inaccuracy, a tale of a man he had once shot in Arizona, a tough from 'Frisco, and an episode in the Rockies. By the end of the hour, Kramenin was more dead than alive.

The car swept up the drive of Astley Priors.

"Turn the car first. Keep the engine going, and be ready to scoot like hell."

The front door opened. Kramenin felt the muzzle pressed against his ribs.

"It is I — Kramenin," he managed. "Bring down the girl at once. There is no time to lose."

Whittington came down the steps, astonished. "You! What's up? Surely you know the plan—"

"We have been betrayed. The girl! And at once. The other little fool had better come too."

"You have orders — from *him?*"

"Naturally. Hurry."

Whittington turned and ran back into the house. The agonizing minutes went by. Then two figures hastily huddled in cloaks were hustled into the car. The smaller resisted; Whittington shoved her in unceremoniously. As Julius leaned forward, the light from the open door lit his face. A man on the steps gave a startled exclamation.

"Get a move on!" shouted Julius.

The car bounded away. A flash, a report — the bullet just missed the taller girl by an inch.

"Get down, Jane!" Julius cried, thrusting her flat. He took aim and fired back. "Hit him — not killed."

"Where's Tommy?" cried Tuppence. "And who's this?"

"Tommy's making tracks for the Argentine. He thought you'd turned up your toes. May I present Monsieur Kramenin? I persuaded him to come on the trip for his health."

"But what made them let us go?"

"Monsieur Kramenin asked them so prettily they couldn't refuse."

The Russian burst out: "Curse you! They know now that I betrayed them. My life won't be safe an hour in this country."

"I'd advise you to make tracks for Russia right away. Pull up — the gentleman's not making the return trip."

Before the car had halted the Russian had swung out and disappeared into the night.

"How did you 'persuade' him?" asked the smaller girl — the first time she'd spoken.

Julius tapped his revolver. "Little Willie here takes the credit."

"Splendid!" The colour surged into her face.

"Annette and I didn't know what was going to happen," said Tuppence. "We thought it was lambs to the slaughter."

"Annette," said Julius. His mind seemed to be adjusting itself to a new idea.

"It's her name."

"Shucks. She may think it's her name, because her memory's gone, poor kid. But it's the one real and original Jane Finn we've got here."

"What?"

A bullet embedded itself in the upholstery just behind Tuppence's head.

"Down! It's an ambush." The car leapt forward; three more shots went wide. Julius raised his hand to his cheek.

"You are hurt?" said Annette quickly.

"Only a scratch."

The girl sprang up. "Let me out! It is me they're after. I'm the one they want. You shall not lose your lives because of me."

Julius caught her by both arms. She had spoken with no trace of foreign accent.

"Sit down, kid. I guess there's nothing wrong with your memory. Been fooling them all the time, eh?"

She nodded, and burst into tears.

"You're from home," she said indistinctly. "I can tell by your voice. It makes me homesick."

"Sure I'm from home. I'm your cousin — Julius Hersheimmer. I came over to Europe on purpose to find you — and a pretty dance you've led me."

The car slackened. "Cross-roads here, sir. I'm not sure of the way."

As it slowed, a figure climbed suddenly over the back, and plunged head first into the midst of them.

"Sorry," said Tommy, extricating himself. "Was in the bushes by the drive. Hung on behind. Now then, you girls, get out. There's a station up that road. Train due in three minutes."

"What the devil are you driving at?" demanded Julius. "You can't let those girls go off alone."

"You and I aren't going to leave the car. Only the girls. Get out at once, Tuppence. Take her with you. Take the train to London. Go straight to Sir James Peel Edgerton."

"Darn you! Jane, you stay where you are."

With a sudden swift movement Tommy snatched the revolver from Julius's hand, and levelled it at him.

"Now will you believe I'm in earnest? Get out, both of you, or I'll shoot."

Tuppence sprang out, dragging the unwilling Jane after her. "Come on. If Tommy's sure — he's sure."

They started running.

"What the hell—" Julius's pent-up rage burst forth.

"Dry up. I want a few words with you, Mr. Julius Hersheimmer."

## CHAPTER XXV. JANE’S STORY

Her arm through Jane's, dragging her along, Tuppence reached the station just as the train came to a standstill. They sank breathless into an empty first-class compartment.

A man looked in, then passed on. Jane started, her eyes dilating with terror.

"Is he one of them, do you think?" she breathed.

"No, no. It's all right." Tuppence took her hand. "Tommy wouldn't have told us to do this unless he was sure we'd be all right."

"But he doesn't know them as I do! Five years! Five long years! Sometimes I thought I should go mad."

"Never mind. It's all over."

"Is it?"

The train sped on. Tuppence sat bolt upright, much in the attitude of a watchful terrier on guard. Not that she disbelieved in Tommy, but occasionally she was shaken with doubts as to whether anyone so simple and honest as he could ever be a match for the fiendish subtlety of the arch-criminal. If they once reached Sir James Peel Edgerton in safety, all would be well. But would they reach him?

At Charing Cross they nipped into a taxi. "King's Cross," Tuppence directed loudly. A man peered in at the window as they started. "If they think we're going to Mr. Carter," she whispered to Jane, "this will put them off the scent."

Crossing Holborn they were held up in a block. "Quick—open the right-hand door!" Two minutes later they were in another taxi, retracing their steps, this time direct to Carlton House Terrace.

There was a grinding noise and a bump—another taxi had collided with them. In a flash Tuppence handed the driver five shillings, and she and Jane had merged themselves in the crowd.

"Do you think the collision was deliberate?"

"I don't know. It might have been either."

At the corner of Carlton House Terrace a large and apparently intoxicated man barred their way.

"Just a word with your pretty friend here." He clutched Jane by the shoulder. Tuppence heard other footsteps behind. Lowering her head, she repeated a manœuvre of childish days, and butted their aggressor full in the capacious middle. He sat down abruptly on the pavement. They took to their heels.

Their breath came in choking gasps as they reached Sir James's door. The man who had stopped them reached the foot of the steps; for a moment he hesitated, and as he did so the door opened. They fell into the hall together.

Sir James half carried Jane into the library and laid her on the leather couch. From a tantalus on the table he poured out a few drops of brandy.

"It's all right. Don't be afraid, my child."

He looked at Tuppence quizzically. "So you're not dead, Miss Tuppence, any more than that Tommy boy of yours was!"

"The Young Adventurers take a lot of killing," boasted Tuppence.

"So it seems. Am I right in thinking that this"—he turned to the girl on the couch—"is Miss Jane Finn?"

"Yes," she said quietly. "I am Jane Finn. I have a lot to tell you."

In a low voice Jane began her story.

"I came over on the *Lusitania* to take up a post in Paris. When she was torpedoed, a man came up to me—Mr. Danvers. He asked me if I was a patriotic American, and told me he was carrying papers which were just life or death to the Allies. He asked me to take charge of them. I was to watch for an advertisement in the *Times*; if it didn't appear, I was to take them to the American Ambassador.

"On the Irish boat I noticed a Mrs. Vandemeyer who'd been keen to chum up with me. I saw her talking to some queer-looking men, and from the way they looked I knew they were talking about me. I'd already ripped open the oilskin packet and substituted blank paper, then sewn it up again. The real thing I laid between two of the advertisement pages of a magazine, gummed round the edge, and carried carelessly stuffed in the pocket of my ulster.

"At Holyhead a crowd seemed always to be shoving me just the way I didn't want to go. In the end I found myself in a carriage with Mrs. Vandemeyer after all. There was quite a nice-looking man and his wife sitting opposite. I had leaned back and closed my eyes—but my eyes weren't quite shut, and suddenly I saw the nice-looking man hand something to Mrs. Vandemeyer, and as he did so he *winked*.

"I tried to get out into the corridor. Suddenly Mrs. Vandemeyer said 'Now,' and flung something over my nose and mouth. At the same moment I felt a terrific blow on the back of my head."

She shuddered, then resumed.

"When I came back to consciousness I was lying on a dirty bed behind a screen. They hadn't found the papers—they'd got the oilskin packet with the blanks. They spoke of torturing me to find out.

"Suddenly something put the thought of loss of memory into my head. The subject had always interested me, and I'd read an awful lot about it. I said a prayer, drew a long breath, and started babbling in *French*.

"Mrs. Vandemeyer came round the screen at once. I asked her in French where I was. The man with her—his face in shadow—spoke to me in French too. Suddenly he caught my wrist, and began twisting it. I screamed and screamed, but I managed to shriek out things in French. Luckily I fainted. The last thing I heard was his voice saying: 'That's not bluff! Anyway, a kid of her age wouldn't know enough.'

"By and by I got up and walked round the room. It was a squalid, dirty place with no windows. There were some battered old pictures on the walls, representing scenes from *Faust*."

Jane's two listeners gave a simultaneous "Ah!"

"Yes—it was the place in Soho where Mr. Beresford was imprisoned. My ulster was lying carelessly over the back of a chair, *and the magazine was still rolled up in the pocket!*

"I knew I was being watched. Mrs. Vandemeyer brought me supper and produced the oilskin packet. I shook my head in a puzzled way, said it was just as though it was all coming back, and then it went again. She told me I was her niece, and that I was to call her 'Aunt Rita.'

"That night I waited until about two o'clock in the morning, then crept up and unhooked one of the pictures from its nail—Marguerite with her casket of jewels. I damped the brown paper at the back, pulled it away, and slipped the two stuck-together pages with their precious enclosure between the picture and its backing. A little gum from some envelopes helped me to stick the latter up again. They'd never think of pulling to pieces one of their own pictures.

"They watched me constantly for weeks. They took me back to Ireland, over every step of the journey again. They spoke of me as a young relative of Mrs. Vandemeyer's whose mind was affected by the shock of the *Lusitania*."

Sir James nodded. "Mrs. Vandemeyer was a woman of great personality. With her social position she would have had little difficulty in imposing her view in preference to yours."

"That's what I thought. In the end I was sent to a sanatorium at Bournemouth. The hospital nurse who had charge of me—*she was one of them*. After a while, I almost forgot that I was really Jane Finn. Playing the part had become second nature. I sank into a sort of stupor. The years went on.

"Then suddenly things changed. The nurse who had looked after me went to Paris and consulted a specialist, representing herself to be me. He exposed her loss of memory as fraudulent—but she had taken note of his methods and reproduced them on me. I managed once again to hold my own.

"One night I was whisked off to London—back to the house in Soho. They sent me in to wait on Mr. Beresford. I was suspicious—I thought it was another trap. But he looked so honest. On the Sunday afternoon a message came. Word had come that he was to be killed. I screamed out that he was escaping, and said I wanted to go back to Marguerite. I shouted the name three times very loud. I knew the others would think I meant Mrs. Vandemeyer, but I hoped it might make Mr. Beresford think of the picture."

She paused.

"Then the papers," said Sir James slowly, "are still at the back of the picture in that room."

"Yes." The girl sank back exhausted.

Sir James rose to his feet. "Come, we must go at once."

"To-night?" queried Tuppence, surprised.

"To-morrow may be too late. Besides, by going to-night we have the chance of capturing that great man and super-criminal—Mr. Brown! You have been followed here—not a doubt of it. When we leave the house we shall be followed again, but not molested, *for it is Mr. Brown's plan that we are to lead him*. But the Soho house is under police supervision. When we enter that house, Mr. Brown will not draw back—he fancies the risk not great, since he will enter in the guise of a friend!"

Tuppence flushed. "But there's something you don't know— *You* know who Mr. Brown is, don't you?"

"Yes," said Sir James gravely. "At last I do."

"At last?" queried Tuppence doubtfully.

"I have been morally certain of his identity ever since the night of Mrs. Vandemeyer's mysterious death. There we are up against the logic of facts. Either the chloral was administered by her own hand, which I reject utterly—or else it was administered in the brandy you gave her. Only three people touched that brandy—you, Miss Tuppence, I myself, and one other—Mr. Julius Hersheimmer!"

Jane Finn stirred and sat up, regarding the speaker with wide astonished eyes.

"At first, the thing seemed utterly impossible. But you cannot escape from the logic of facts. Mr. Beresford rang me up and told me what I had already suspected—that the photograph of Miss Jane Finn had never really been out of Mr. Hersheimmer's possession—"

But the girl sprang to her feet. "What do you mean? That Mr. Brown is *Julius?* Julius—my own cousin!"

"No, Miss Finn," said Sir James unexpectedly. "Not your cousin. The man who calls himself Julius Hersheimmer is no relation to you whatsoever."

## CHAPTER XXVI. MR. BROWN

Sir James's words came like a bomb-shell. The lawyer crossed to his desk and returned with a small newspaper cutting—the paragraph about the man found dead in New York.

"I set to work to prove the impossible possible," he said. "Julius Hersheimmer set out to discover what had become of his cousin. He went out West and obtained her photograph. On the eve of his departure from New York he was set upon and murdered, his body dressed in shabby clothes, the face disfigured. Mr. Brown took his place. None of the real Hersheimmer's friends saw him before he sailed—and indeed, the impersonation was perfect. Only once did he come near disaster: Mrs. Vandemeyer knew his secret. Exposure stared him in the face. He took a desperate step, trusting in his assumed character to avert suspicion. He nearly succeeded—but not quite."

"I can't believe it," murmured Jane. "He seemed so splendid."

"The real Julius Hersheimmer _was_ a splendid fellow. And Mr. Brown is a consummate actor."

Tuppence nodded slowly. "I didn't want to say it, Jane—I knew it would hurt you. But I still don't understand why, if he's Mr. Brown, he rescued us."

"Can't you? As a last hope Jane Finn was to be allowed to escape—and the escape must be managed so that she harbours no suspicions. Then he would have driven you straight to the house in Soho and secured the document. Some accident would have happened to both of you afterwards. You see, you know rather an inconvenient amount." He paused. "I admit I was caught napping. But somebody else wasn't."

"Tommy," said Tuppence softly.

"Yes. I'm not too easy in my mind about him. It takes more than one man and a revolver to hold up Mr. Brown."

From a drawer he took a service revolver. "We'll go to Soho. If Beresford has the upper hand, there's nothing to fear. If otherwise, our enemy will not find us unprepared."

The car drew up at the corner of the square. Police were stationed at front and back; no one had entered. They mounted the rickety stairs. Tuppence looked with interest at the ragged curtain hiding the recess. Even now she could almost swear it moved—as though some one were behind it. Impossible, of course. Yet as they entered the prison room she could not shake the curious insistent feeling that _Mr. Brown was in the house_.

Jane unhooked the picture of Marguerite. Sir James handed her a knife, and she ripped away the brown paper. Two thin sheets covered with writing fell out.

"We've got it," said Tuppence. "At last—"

Sir James scrutinized them. "Yes. This is the ill-fated draft treaty."

He folded the paper carefully into his pocket-book, then looked round the dingy room. "A truly sinister room. You notice the absence of windows, the thickness of the close-fitting door. Whatever took place here would never be heard outside."

Tuppence shivered. She looked up to find him watching her. He gave an emphatic little nod.

"Quite right, Miss Tuppence. You scent danger. So do I. So does Miss Finn. You feel—as we all feel—_the presence of Mr. Brown_. Yes. Mr. Brown is here."

"In this house?"

"In this room.... _I am Mr. Brown_."

Stupefied, unbelieving, they stared at him. The very lines of his face had changed. He smiled a slow cruel smile.

"Neither of you will leave this room alive. The treaty is mine. The police will find three victims of Mr. Brown—the third only wounded, able to describe the attack with a wealth of detail. So no one will think of searching the pockets of Sir James Peel Edgerton."

He turned to Jane. "You outwitted me. You will not do it again."

He slipped his hand into his pocket. "Checkmate to the Young Adventurers," he said, and slowly raised the big automatic.

But, even as he did so, he felt himself seized from behind in a grip of iron. The revolver was wrenched from his hand, and the voice of Julius Hersheimmer said drawlingly: "I guess you're caught redhanded with the goods upon you."

The blood rushed to the K.C.'s face, but his self-control was marvellous as he looked from one to the other of his two captors. He looked longest at Tommy.

"You," he said beneath his breath. "_You!_ I might have known."

Their grip slackened. Quick as a flash his left hand, the hand which bore the big signet ring, was raised to his lips.

"'_Ave, Cæsar! te morituri salutant_,'" he said, still looking at Tommy.

Then his face changed, and with a long convulsive shudder he fell forward in a crumpled heap, while an odour of bitter almonds filled the air.

## CHAPTER XXVII. A SUPPER PARTY AT THE _SAVOY_

The supper party given by Mr. Julius Hersheimmer to a few friends on the evening of the 30th will long be remembered in catering circles. He gave carte blanche—and when a millionaire gives carte blanche he usually gets it. Every delicacy out of season was provided; waiters carried bottles of ancient vintage with loving care. The list of guests was small and select: Mr. Carter; an old friend of his, Sir William Beresford; Dr. Hall; those two youthful adventurers, Miss Prudence Cowley and Mr. Thomas Beresford; and last, as guest of honour, Miss Jane Finn.

Julius had spared no pains to make Jane's appearance a success. Earlier in the day, a mysterious knock had brought Tuppence to the door of the apartment she was sharing with the American girl. It was Julius, with a cheque in his hand.

"Say, Tuppence, will you do me a good turn? Get Jane regularly togged up for this evening. Spare no expense. You get me?"

"Sure thing," mimicked Tuppence. "It will be a pleasure dressing Jane. She's the loveliest thing I've ever seen."

"That's so," agreed Mr. Hersheimmer fervently. His fervour brought a momentary twinkle to Tuppence's eye.

To most people the 29th, the much-heralded "Labour Day," had passed much as any other day. Newspapers which had hinted at a general strike and a reign of terror were forced to hide their diminished heads. In the Sunday papers a brief notice of the sudden death of Sir James Peel Edgerton, the famous K.C., had appeared. The exact manner of his sudden death was never made public.

Tommy had been right in his forecast. It had been a one-man show. Deprived of their chief, the organization fell to pieces. Kramenin had made a precipitate return to Russia. The gang had fled from Astley Priors in a panic, leaving behind documents which compromised them hopelessly. With these proofs, aided by a small brown diary taken from the dead man's pocket containing a full and damning résumé of the whole plot, the Government had called an eleventh-hour conference. The Labour leaders were forced to recognize that they had been used as a cat's paw. It was to be Peace, not War.

But the Cabinet knew by how narrow a margin they had escaped utter disaster. Burnt in on Mr. Carter's brain was the strange scene which had taken place in the house in Soho the night before—he had entered the squalid room to find that great man, the friend of a lifetime, dead, betrayed out of his own mouth. From the dead man's pocket-book he had retrieved the ill-omened draft treaty, and then and there it had been reduced to ashes. England was saved.

And now, in a private room at the *Savoy*, Mr. Julius P. Hersheimmer was receiving his guests.

Mr. Carter was the first to arrive. With him was a choleric-looking old gentleman, at sight of whom Tommy flushed up to the roots of his hair.

"Ha!" said Sir William, surveying him apoplectically. "So you're my nephew, are you? Not much to look at—but you've done good work. Shall we let bygones be bygones? You're my heir; I propose to make you an allowance, and you can look upon Chalmers Park as your home."

"Thank you, sir, it's awfully decent of you."

"Where's this young lady I've been hearing such a lot about?"

Tommy introduced Tuppence.

"Girls aren't what they used to be in my young days."

"Yes, they are," said Tuppence. "Their clothes are different, perhaps, but they themselves are just the same. I'm a frightful minx myself."

"Minxes then—minxes now!" The old gentleman chuckled and pinched her ear in high good-humour. Most young women were terrified of the "old bear," as they termed him; Tuppence's pertness delighted the old misogynist.

When all were assembled, Julius indicated the place of honour with a wave of his hand. "Tuppence, will you—"

But Tuppence shook her head. "No—that's Jane's place. When one thinks of how she's held out all these years, she ought to be made the queen of the feast to-night."

Julius flung her a grateful glance, and Jane came forward shyly to the allotted seat. Beautiful as she had seemed before, it was as nothing to the loveliness that now went fully adorned. The model gown supplied by a famous dressmaker had been entitled "A tiger lily." It was all golds and reds and browns, and out of it rose the pure column of the girl's white throat, and the bronze masses of hair that crowned her lovely head. There was admiration in every eye as she took her seat.

Soon the supper party was in full swing, and with one accord Tommy was called upon for a full explanation.

"You've been too darned close about the whole business," Julius accused him. "The idea of you and Tuppence casting me for the part of Mr. Brown just tickles me to death!"

"The idea was not original to them," said Mr. Carter gravely. "It was suggested, and the poison very carefully instilled, by a past-master in the art."

"There's nothing to tell," said Tommy, acutely uncomfortable. "I was an awful mug—right up to the time I found that photograph of Annette, and realized that she was Jane Finn. Then I remembered how persistently she had shouted out that word 'Marguerite'—and I thought of the pictures, and—well, that's that."

"Go on," said Mr. Carter.

"That business about Mrs. Vandemeyer had worried me. On the face of it, Julius or Sir James must have done the trick. Finding that photograph in the drawer made me suspect Julius. Then I remembered that it was Sir James who had discovered the false Jane Finn. I decided to take no chances either way. I left a note for Julius saying I was off to the Argentine, and dropped Sir James's letter with the offer of the job by the desk so that he would see it was genuine. Then I rang up Sir James and told him everything except where I believed the papers to be hidden. The way he helped me track Tuppence and Annette almost disarmed me. Then I got a bogus note from Tuppence—and I knew."

"But how?"

Tommy passed the note round the table. "It's her handwriting all right, but I knew it wasn't from her because of the signature. She'd never spell her name 'Twopence,' but anyone who'd never seen it written might quite easily do so. Julius *had* seen it—he showed me a note of hers to him once—but *Sir James hadn't!* After that everything was plain sailing. I sent Albert to Mr. Carter, pretended to go away, and doubled back. I sent the girls off to Sir James—I was sure they'd fetch up at the house in Soho. The moment they were out of sight I told Julius to drive like hell for London. We met Mr. Carter outside, hid behind the curtain in the recess, and—that's all."

"By the way," said Julius suddenly, "you're all wrong about that photograph of Jane. It *was* taken from me, but I found it again—in that little safe on the wall in Mrs. Vandemeyer's bedroom."

"I knew you found something," said Tuppence reproachfully. "That's what started me off suspecting you."

"I guess I was a mite suspicious too. I determined I wouldn't let on I'd got it until a photographer had made a dozen copies."

"We all kept back something or other," said Tuppence thoughtfully. "I suppose secret service work makes you like that."

Mr. Carter took from his pocket a small shabby brown book.

"Not until I read the entries here could I bring myself fully to credit the amazing truth. This book will pass into the possession of Scotland Yard, but never be publicly exhibited. To you, who know the truth, I propose to read certain passages."

He opened the book and turned the thin pages.

"... It is madness to keep this book. I know that. But I have never shrunk from taking risks. The book will only be taken from my dead body....

"... From an early age I realized I had exceptional abilities. My appearance was the only thing against me—quiet and insignificant, utterly nondescript....

"... When I was a boy I heard a famous murder trial. I was deeply impressed by the eloquence of the counsel for the defence. Then I studied the criminal in the dock. The man was a fool—I felt an immeasurable contempt for him. It occurred to me that the criminal standard was a low one—wastrels, failures, the riff-raff of civilization. Strange that men of brains had never realized its extraordinary opportunities....

"... Supposing I became Prime Minister of England? Hampered at every turn, fettered by the democratic system of which I should be the mere figurehead! No—the power I dreamed of was absolute! An autocrat! A dictator! And such power could only be obtained by working outside the law....

"... I saw that I must lead two lives. I modelled myself upon famous K.C.'s. If I had chosen to be an actor, I should have been the greatest actor living! No disguises—no grease paint—no false beards! Personality! I put it on like a glove! When I shed it, I was myself, quiet, unobtrusive, a man like every other man. I called myself Mr. Brown. There are hundreds of men called Brown—hundreds of men looking just like me....

"... I have been reading a life of Napoleon. He and I have much in common....

"... Once or twice I have felt afraid. The first time was in Italy. The talk fell on insanity. The professor said, 'A great many men are mad, and no one knows it. They do not know it themselves.' I do not understand why he looked at me when he said that...."

Mr. Carter paused. "There are just two small entries that refer to the three of you. In the light of what happened they are interesting.

"... By inducing the girl to come to me of her own accord, I have succeeded in disarming her. But she has intuitive flashes that might be dangerous. She must be got out of the way. I can do nothing with the American. He suspects and dislikes me. But he cannot know. Sometimes I fear I have underestimated the other boy. He is not clever, but it is hard to blind his eyes to facts...."

Mr. Carter shut the book. "A great man. Genius, or insanity, who can say?"

There was silence. Then Mr. Carter rose to his feet.

"I will give you a toast. The Joint Venture which has so amply justified itself by success!"

It was drunk with acclamation.

"There's something more we want to hear," continued Mr. Carter. "We'll ask Miss Jane Finn to tell us the story that only Miss Tuppence has heard so far—but before we do so we'll drink her health. The health of one of the bravest of America's daughters, to whom is due the thanks and gratitude of two great countries!"

## CHAPTER XXVIII. AND AFTER

At a celebratory supper at the Savoy, Mr. Carter raised a glass to the Joint Venture and read brief passages from Sir James's notebook—revealing a man who had chosen absolute power outside the law and worn borrowed personalities. The treaty had been burnt; the conspiracy had collapsed.

In the Rolls-Royce returning to the Ritz, Julius spoke abruptly.

"Jane—I want you to marry me. I've loved you from the moment I set eyes on your photo. You shall take your own time, but I want the right to look after you."

"That's what I want," said Jane wistfully. "Some one who'll be good to me. Oh, you don't know how lonesome I feel."

A small hand slipped into his.

"I love you now, Julius," said Jane Finn. "I loved you that first moment in the car when the bullet grazed your cheek."

In another taxi, the Young Adventurers sat bolt upright, very stiff and ill at ease. A terrible constraint had settled down between them. They were tongue-tied—paralysed. All the old _camaraderie_ was gone.

At last Tuppence made a desperate effort. "Rather fun, wasn't it?"

"Rather."

"I like Julius."

Tommy was suddenly galvanized. "You're not going to marry him, do you hear? I forbid it."

"Oh!" said Tuppence meekly.

"Absolutely, you understand."

"He doesn't want to marry me—he really only asked me out of kindness. He's head over ears in love with Jane. I expect he's proposing to her now."

"She'll do for him very nicely," said Tommy condescendingly.

"But I suppose you prefer sterling worth," said Tuppence demurely.

"I—oh, dash it all, Tuppence, you know!"

Tuppence stole a glance at him sideways. "It has been fun, hasn't it, Tommy? I do hope we shall have lots more adventures."

"You're insatiable. I've had quite enough adventures for the present."

"Well, shopping is almost as good," said Tuppence dreamily. "Think of buying old furniture, and bright carpets, and futurist silk curtains, and a polished dining-table, and a divan with lots of cushions."

"Hold hard. What's all this for?"

"Possibly a house—but I think a flat."

"Whose flat?"

"You think I mind saying it, but I don't in the least. _Ours_, so there!"

"You darling!" cried Tommy, his arms tightly round her. "I was determined to make you say it."

The taxi proceeded round the north side of Regent's Park.

"You haven't really proposed now," pointed out Tuppence. "Not what our grandmothers would call a proposal. But after listening to a rotten one like Julius's, I'm inclined to let you off."

"You won't be able to get out of marrying me, so don't you think it."

"Marriage is called all sorts of things—a haven, a refuge, a crowning glory, a state of bondage. But do you know what I think it is?"

"What?"

"A sport!"

"And a damned good sport too," said Tommy.

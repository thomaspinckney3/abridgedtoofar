It was 2 p.m. on May 7, 1915. The Lusitania had been struck by two torpedoes and was sinking rapidly; the boats were being launched with all possible speed. Women and children were being lined up. Some clung to husbands and fathers; others clasped their children. One girl stood a little apart, quite young—eighteen—grave, steadfast eyes looking straight ahead.

“I beg your pardon.”

A man’s voice beside her made her start and turn. She had noticed him more than once amongst the first-class passengers: a hint of mystery, a nervous way of glancing over his shoulder, quick to rebuff any overture. Now he was greatly agitated; beads of perspiration stood on his brow. He did not strike her as the kind of man afraid to meet death, and yet he was in overmastering fear.

“Yes?” Her grave eyes met his.

He stood with desperate irresolution. “It must be! Yes—it is the only way.” Aloud, abruptly: “You are an American?”

“Yes.”

“A patriotic one?”

She flushed. “I guess you’ve no right to ask such a thing! Of course I am!”

“Don’t be offended. You wouldn’t be if you knew how much there was at stake. But I’ve got to trust some one—and it must be a woman.”

“Why?”

“Because of ‘women and children first.’” He lowered his voice. “I’m carrying papers—vitally important papers. They may make all the difference to the Allies in the war. These papers have got to be saved! They’ve more chance with you than with me. Will you take them?”

She held out her hand.

“Wait—I must warn you. There may be a risk—if I’ve been followed. I don’t think I have, but one never knows. If so, there will be danger. Have you the nerve to go through with it?”

The girl smiled. “I’ll go through with it all right. And I’m real proud to be chosen! What am I to do with them afterwards?”

“Watch the newspapers! I’ll advertise in the personal column of the Times, beginning ‘Shipmate.’ At the end of three days if there’s nothing—well, you’ll know I’m down and out. Then take the packet to the American Embassy, and deliver it into the Ambassador’s own hands. Is that clear?”

“Quite clear.”

“Then be ready—I’m going to say good-bye.” He took her hand. “Good-bye. Good luck to you,” he said in a louder tone.

Her hand closed on the oilskin packet that had lain in his palm.

The Lusitania settled with a more decided list to starboard. At a quick command, the girl went forward to take her place in the boat.

THE YOUNG ADVENTURERS, LTD.

“Tommy, old thing!”

“Tuppence, old bean!”

They almost blocked the Dover Street Tube exit with their affectionate greeting. The adjective “old” was misleading. Their united ages would certainly not have totalled forty-five. “Not seen you for simply centuries,” said the young man. “Come and chew a bun with me. We’re getting a bit unpopular here--blocking the gangway as it were. Let’s get out of it.”

They walked towards Piccadilly. “Where shall we go?” asked Tommy with a faint anxiety that did not escape Miss Prudence Cowley—“Tuppence” to her friends.

“Tommy, you’re stony!”

“Rolling in cash,” said Tommy unconvincingly.

“You always were a shocking liar,” said Tuppence. But she steered them into Lyons. The place was full; as they hunted for a table, Tommy said: “Funny scraps one overhears. I passed two Johnnies to-day talking about some one called Jane Finn. Did you ever hear such a name?”

Two elderly ladies rose; Tuppence slid into a seat. “Tea and buns,” ordered Tommy. “Tea and buttered toast. And mind the tea comes in separate teapots,” added Tuppence severely.

Tommy’s slicked-back red hair shone; his suit was perilously near the end of its tether. His face was pleasantly ugly--nondescript, yet unmistakably the face of a gentleman and a sportsman. They were an essentially modern-looking couple as they sat there. Tuppence had no claim to beauty, but there was character and charm in the elfin lines of her little face, with its determined chin and large, wide-apart grey eyes that looked mistily out from under straight, black brows. She wore a small bright green toque over her black bobbed hair, and her extremely short and rather shabby skirt revealed a pair of uncommonly dainty ankles.

“Now then,” said Tommy, “let’s get up-to-date. I haven’t seen you since 1916.”

“Abridged biography,” said Tuppence. “I left home early in the war, went to an officers’ hospital. Did chores, climbed, with mop and pail, to parlourmaid, then to waiting on Sisters. Met Lieutenant Thomas Beresford—bow, Tommy!—left in a blaze of glory. Drove a delivery van, a motor-lorry, then a Government office, and after the Armistice they combed me out. Since then: job-hunting.”

Tommy’s tale was shorter. “France, Mesopotamia, a second wound, hospital, Egypt; demobbed; ten long, weary months job-hunting. There aren’t any jobs. What good am I? I know nothing about business.”

“What about the colonies?”

“They wouldn’t like me.”

“Rich relations?”

“Wanted to adopt me once. I refused—for the mater’s sake.” Tuppence’s eyes grew misty. “You’re a good sort.”

“Rot! I’m desperate.”

“So am I!” burst out Tuppence. “I’ve touted, answered advertisements, screwed and saved. But it’s no good. I shall have to go home.”

“Money! I think about money morning, noon and night! There are only three ways to get it: to be left it, to marry it, or to make it. First is ruled out. I always help old ladies over crossings in case they’re eccentric millionaires—but not one has ever asked my name!”

“Marriage?” suggested Tommy.

“My best chance—so I made up my mind early to marry money. Only I never meet rich men. The boys I know are as hard up as I am.”

“Your general?”

“Keeps a bicycle shop in time of peace,” said Tuppence. “Now, you could marry a rich girl.”

“I don’t know any.”

“You can always get to know one. Tread on her foot as she comes out of the Ritz, pick up her handkerchief. If she thinks you want to know her she’s flattered.”

“You overrate my manly charms.”

“Whereas my millionaire would run for his life! No—remains to make money.”

“We’ve tried, and failed.”

“We’ve tried the orthodox ways. Suppose we try the unorthodox. Tommy, let’s be adventurers!”

“Certainly,” said Tommy. “How do we begin?”

“If we could make ourselves known, people might hire us to commit crimes for them.”

“Delightful—from a clergyman’s daughter!”

“The moral guilt would be theirs—not mine. There’s a difference between stealing a diamond necklace for yourself and being hired to steal it.”

“There wouldn’t be the least difference if you were caught!”

“I shouldn’t be caught. I’m so clever.”

“Modesty always was your besetting sin.”

“Don’t rag. Let’s form a business partnership. A joint venture! It struck me as such a romantic phrase to come across in the middle of musty old figures. It’s got an Elizabethan flavour about it--makes one think of galleons and doubloons. A joint venture! Trading under the name of the Young Adventurers, Ltd.”

“How do we get employers?”

“Advertisement. Have you a pencil?” Tommy produced a shabby green notebook. “Shall we begin: ‘Young officer, twice wounded—’”
“Certainly not.”

“Well, well! Now listen: ‘Two young adventurers for hire. Willing to do anything, go anywhere. Pay must be good.’ We might add: ‘No reasonable offer refused’—like flats.”

“Any offer we get will be unreasonable.”

“Genius! ‘No unreasonable offer refused.’ How’s that?”

“Don’t mention pay again. It looks eager.”

“It couldn’t look as eager as I feel! Now—straight through. ‘Two young adventurers for hire. Willing to do anything, go anywhere. Pay must be good. No unreasonable offer refused.’”
“It would strike me as either being a hoax, or else written by a lunatic.”

“It’s not half so insane as a thing I read this morning beginning ‘Petunia’ and signed ‘Best Boy.’”
“Times, reply to Box so-and-so. About five shillings. Here’s half a crown.”

“Shall we really try it?” said Tommy at last. “Just for the fun of the thing?”

“Tommy, you’re a sport! Let’s drink to success.” They solemnly raised cold dregs. “Here’s to our joint venture, and may it prosper!”

“The Young Adventurers, Ltd.!”

They laughed, a trifle uncertainly. Tuppence rose. “I must return to my palatial suite at the hostel.”

“Perhaps it’s time I strolled round to the Ritz,” said Tommy. “Where shall we meet?”

“Twelve to-morrow. Piccadilly Tube station.”

“My time is my own,” said Mr. Beresford magnificently.
“So long.”

“Good-bye, old thing.”

They went off in opposite directions. Half-way across St. James’s Park a man’s voice behind Tuppence made her start.

“Excuse me,” it said.

“But may I speak to you for a moment?”

Tuppence turned sharply. The rebuff that trembled on her lips went unsaid; the man’s appearance and manner did not bear out her first assumption. Big, clean-shaven, heavy-jowled; small cunning eyes that shifted under her direct gaze. She disliked and distrusted him, but believed he meant no disrespect.

“Well, what is it?”

“I happened to overhear part of your conversation with the young gentleman in Lyons’.”

“Well—what of it?”

“Nothing—except that I think I may be of some use to you.”

“You followed me here?”

“I took that liberty.” He handed her a card: Mr. Edward Whittington; Esthonia Glassware Co.; a City address. “If you will call upon me to-morrow morning at eleven o’clock, I will lay the details of my proposition before you.”

Tuppence considered, then nodded. “Very well. I’ll be there.”

He raised his hat with a flourish and walked away. Tuppence remained for some minutes gazing after him. Then she gave a curious movement of her shoulders, rather as a terrier shakes himself. “The adventures have begun,” she murmured to herself.

“What does he want me to do, I wonder? There’s something about you, Mr. Whittington, that I don’t like at all. But, on the other hand, I’m not the least bit afraid of you. And as I’ve said before, and shall doubtless say again, little Tuppence can look after herself, thank you!” She turned aside into a post office, weighed five shillings against ninepence. Disdaining the spiky pen and thick, black treacle which a beneficent Government had provided, Tuppence drew out Tommy’s pencil which she had retained and wrote rapidly: “Don’t put in advertisement. Will explain to-morrow.”

“It may catch him,” she murmured.

“Anyway, it’s worth trying.”

That night in her tiny cubicle she munched buns and wondered what earthly need an Esthonia Glassware Co. could have for her services; she dreamed Mr. Whittington set her to wash hospital plates masquerading as Esthonian glass.

At eleven to the minute she mounted to the top-floor door with “Esthonia Glassware Co.” on ground glass. A middle-aged clerk ushered her through a “Private” door.

Mr. Whittington, sleek prosperity and shifty eye, nodded her to a chair, shuffled papers, then leaned across the desk.

“Now, my dear young lady, let us come to business. You want work? I have work to offer you. What should you say now to £100 down, and all expenses paid?” He thrust his thumbs into his waistcoat.

“And the nature of the work?”

“Nominal—purely nominal. A pleasant trip, that is all.”

“Where to?”

“Paris.” He smiled. “To re-enter one of those charming pensionnats de jeunes filles—Madame Colombier’s, Avenue de Neuilly.”

Tuppence knew the name; more select could hardly be. “You want me to go there? For how long?”

“Possibly three months. You would go in the character of my ward. Absolute secrecy: no communication with your friends. By the way, you are English?”

“Yes.”

“Yet you speak with a slight American accent?”

“My great pal in hospital was a little American girl. I can soon get out of it again.”

“On the contrary, pass as an American. Details of an English past are harder to sustain.” He was smooth again. “Then—”

“One moment, Mr. Whittington! You seem to be taking my consent for granted.”

“Surely you are not thinking of refusing? Madame Colombier’s is most high-class and orthodox. And the terms are most liberal.”

“Exactly. The terms are almost too liberal. I cannot see any way in which I can be worth that amount of money to you.”

“No? I could obtain some one else for very much less. What I am willing to pay for is a young lady with sufficient intelligence and presence of mind to sustain her part well—and sufficient discretion not to ask too many questions.”

“There’s another thing. So far there has been no mention of Mr. Beresford. Where does he come in?”

“Mr. Beresford?”

“My partner. You saw us together yesterday.”

“I’m afraid we shan’t require his services.”

“Then it’s off!” Tuppence rose. “It’s both or neither. Sorry—but that’s how it is. Good morning, Mr. Whittington.”

“Wait. Let us see if something can’t be managed. Sit down again, Miss—?”

Her conscience pricked at “archdeacon.” The first name that came into her head leapt to her lips. “Jane Finn,” she said hastily—and stared. The geniality fell from his face; it went purple; veins stood out.

“So that’s your little game, is it?” he hissed.

Tuppence, utterly taken aback, kept her head. She had no idea what he meant, but she “kept her end up.”

“Been playing with me like a cat and mouse? Knew all the time what I wanted you for? Who’s been blabbing? Rita?”

“Rita knows nothing about me,” said Tuppence truthfully.

His eyes bored into her. “How much do you know?”

“Very little indeed,” said Tuppence—and saw his uneasiness grow.

“Anyway, you knew enough to come in here and plump out that name.”

“It might be my own name.”

“Likely, isn’t it? Two girls with a name like that?”

“Or I might just have hit upon it by chance,” said Tuppence, intoxicated with the success of truth.

He brought his fist down. “Quit fooling! How much do you know? And how much do you want?”

The last five words took Tuppence’s fancy mightily, especially after a meagre breakfast and a supper of buns the night before. Her present part was of the adventuress rather than the adventurous order, but she did not deny its possibilities. She sat up and smiled with the air of one who has the situation thoroughly well in hand.

“My dear Mr. Whittington,” she said, “let us by all means lay our cards upon the table. You heard me say yesterday I proposed to live by my wits. I have now proved I have some wits to live by! I admit I have knowledge of a certain name—but perhaps my knowledge ends there.”

“Yes—and perhaps it doesn’t. How much?”

“Suppose we say a little something down, and a fuller discussion later?”

“Blackmail, eh?”

“Oh no! Shall we say payment of services in advance? You see—I’m so very fond of money!”

“You’re about the limit,” growled Whittington, with unwilling admiration. “I thought you were a meek little kid with just enough brains for my purpose.”

“Life,” said Tuppence, “is full of surprises.”

A discreet knock. The clerk entered with a telephone message. Whittington read, frowned. “That’ll do, Brown.” He turned back. “Come to-morrow at the same time. I’m busy now. Here’s fifty to go on with.”

He pushed notes across. Tuppence counted them, slipped them into her handbag, and rose. “Au revoir, Mr. Whittington.”

“Exactly. Au revoir, my clever and charming young lady.”

Tuppence sped down the stairs, elated. A clock showed five minutes to twelve. “Let’s give Tommy a surprise!” She hailed a taxi to Piccadilly Tube. Tommy, just within the entrance, stared as she alighted.

“Pay the thing, will you, old bean? I’ve got nothing smaller than a five-pound note!”

A SET BACK

The moment was not quite so triumphant as it ought to have been. Tommy’s pockets were light; the cabman, gruff and incredulous, had to be coaxed to accept a muddle of coins and move on. “I think you’ve given him too much, Tommy,” said Tuppence innocently. “I fancy he wants to give some of it back.” Perhaps that was why he finally drove off.

“What the—dickens did you want to take a taxi for?” demanded Tommy.

“I was afraid I might be late and keep you waiting,” said Tuppence gently. Then, very demurely: “And really and truly I haven’t got anything smaller than a five-pound note.”

“You did that part very well,” grunted Tommy. “All the same, the fellow wasn’t taken in.”

“No,” said Tuppence thoughtfully. “That’s the curious part about speaking the truth. No one does believe it. I found that out this morning. Now—lunch. The Piccadilly. It’s nearer. We shan’t have to take another taxi.”

“Is your brain unhinged?”

“Your last supposition is the correct one. I have come into money, and the shock has been too much for me! For that particular form of mental trouble an eminent physician recommends unlimited Hors d’œuvre, Lobster à l’américane, Chicken Newberg, and Pêche Melba! Let’s go and get them!”

“Tuppence, old girl, what has really come over you?”

“Oh, unbelieving one!” She wrenched open her bag. Bank-notes fluttered.

“Great Jehosaphat! My dear girl, don’t wave Fishers aloft like that!”

“They’re not Fishers. They’re five times better than Fishers, and this one’s ten times better!”

Tommy blinked, and groaned. “I must have been drinking unawares! Am I dreaming, Tuppence, or do I really behold a large quantity of five-pound notes being waved about in a dangerous fashion?”

“Even so, O King! Now will you come and have lunch?”

They went below to the grill room, where Tuppence, surrounded by the “many hors d’œuvre of her dreams,” told him everything—how Whittington had believed she knew about Jane Finn, how he had paid, and how she had named a time to return.

“And the curious part of it is,” she ended, “that I really did invent the name of Jane Finn. I didn’t want to give my own.”

“Perhaps so,” said Tommy slowly. “But you didn’t invent it.”

“What?”

“I told it you. Yesterday I overheard two people talking about a female called Jane Finn. That’s what brought it into your mind.”

“So you did—how extraordinary.” She broke off, then: “Tommy! What were the two men like?”

“One was a big, fat sort of chap—clean-shaven and dark.”

“That’s him! That’s Whittington! And the other?”

“I didn’t notice him particularly. It was the outlandish name that caught me.”

Tommy sobered. “What is this going to lead to?”

“More money,” said Tuppence promptly; then, more seriously: “You’re right. It is a poser.”

“After all, you know, you can’t bluff him forever. He’ll want to know where you got your information. And it may be actionable—blackmail—”

“Nonsense. Blackmail is saying you’ll tell unless you are given money. I can’t tell anything because I don’t really know anything.”

“Well, anyway, what are we going to do?”

“We must think. Order some Turkish coffee, Tommy. Stimulating to the brain. Oh, dear, what a lot I have eaten!”

“You have made rather a hog of yourself! So have I for that matter, but I flatter myself that my choice of dishes was more judicious than yours. Two coffees.” He flicked this to the waiter. “One Turkish, one French.”

Tuppence sipped with a deeply reflective air. “Be quiet. I’m thinking.”

“Shades of Pelmanism!” said Tommy, and relapsed into silence.

“There!” said Tuppence at last. “I’ve got a plan. We can only find out through Whittington. We must discover where he lives, what he does—sleuth him! He only saw you for a minute in Lyons’. He’s not likely to recognize you. After all, one young man is much like another.”

“I repudiate that remark utterly.”

“My plan is this: I go alone to-morrow, put him off again. You hang about outside. When he comes out I’ll drop a handkerchief or something—and off you go.”

“Off I go where?”

“Follow him, of course!”

He grinned. “Sort of thing one reads in books—but it will be rather a lark. Dinner and a show to-night?”

“Rather.”

Two of the five-pound notes died gallantly. Next morning they met and went citywards. Tommy waited opposite while Tuppence plunged into the building. In a moment she was back.

“The place is shut. I can’t make anyone hear.”

They went up together. On the third-floor landing a young clerk emerged.

“Were you wanting the Esthonia Glassware?”

“Yes, please.”

“It’s closed down. Since yesterday afternoon. Company being wound up, they say. The office is to let.”

“And Mr. Whittington’s address?”

“Afraid I don’t. They left rather suddenly.”

Back in the street they stared at one another.

“That’s torn it,” said Tommy.

“And I never suspected it,” wailed Tuppence—then her chin went up. “Do you think this is the end? If so, you’re wrong. It’s just the beginning!”

“The beginning of what?”

“Of our adventure! If they’re scared enough to bolt, there must be a lot in this Jane Finn business. We’ll run them down! We’ll be sleuths in earnest!” She snatched a pencil, scribbled, and handed him a slip.

He read aloud: “WANTED, any information respecting Jane Finn. Apply Y. A.”

They pinched and walked on Thursday; “walking is cheap,” decreed Tuppence. On Friday, letters might be expected. Tommy, honour-bound not to open them, met her at ten in the National Gallery. Tuppence, on a red velvet seat, stared blindly at Turners till he came.

“Well?”

“Well,” said Tommy provokingly. “Which is your favourite picture?”

“Don’t be a wretch. Aren’t there any answers?”

Tommy lengthened his face. “It’s too bad. Good money wasted. Still—there it is. The advertisement has appeared, and—there are only two answers!”

“Tommy, you devil! Give them to me. How could you be so mean!”

“Your language, Tuppence, your language! They’re very particular at the National Gallery. Government show, you know. And do remember, as I have pointed out to you before, that as a clergyman’s daughter——”

“I ought to be on the stage!” finished Tuppence with a snap.

“That is not what I intended to say. But if you are sure that you have enjoyed to the full the reaction of joy after despair with which I have kindly provided you free of charge, let us get down to our mail, as the saying goes.”

She snatched the envelopes and scrutinized them. “Thick paper—this looks rich—we’ll keep it to the last.” She ripped open the first.

“DEAR SIR, Referring to your advertisement, I may be able to be of some use to you. Perhaps you could call and see me at the above address at eleven o’clock to-morrow morning. Yours truly, A. CARTER. 27 Carshalton Gardens.”

“The plan of campaign,” said Tommy. “Ushered in: ‘Please take a seat, Mr.—er?’ I reply: ‘Edward Whittington!’ He turns purple and gasps: ‘How much?’ I pocket the fifty—”

“Don’t be absurd. Now the other. Oh, this is from the Ritz! ‘DEAR SIR, Re your advertisement, I should be glad if you would call round somewhere about lunch-time. Yours truly, JULIUS P. HERSHEIMMER.’”

“Ha!” said Tommy. “Do I smell a Boche? Or only an American millionaire of unfortunate ancestry? At all events we’ll call at lunch-time. It’s a good time—frequently leads to free food for two.”

They hurried to Carshalton Gardens, were shown into a small study, and a tall man with a lean, hawklike face and a tired manner came in.

“Mr. Y. A.?” He smiled. “Do sit down, both of you.”

“We wanted to know—would you be so kind as to tell us anything you know about Jane Finn?” Tuppence began.

“Jane Finn? Ah!” He reflected. “Well, the question is, what do you know about her?”

“That’s got nothing to do with it,” said Tuppence, drawing herself up.

“No? But it has, you know.” He smiled again. “Come now—what do you know? Suppose you tell me....”

Tuppence struggled against his quiet persuasiveness. “We couldn’t do that, could we, Tommy?”

But Tommy’s tone held deference. “I dare say the little we know won’t be any good to you, sir. But such as it is, you’re welcome to it.”

His eyes questioned; Tommy nodded. “Yes, sir, I recognized you at once. Saw you in France—with the Intelligence.”

“No names, please,” he said quickly.

“I’m known as Mr. Carter here. Now—who’s going to tell me the story?”

Tuppence told it from the forming of the Young Adventurers, Ltd., downwards. Mr. Carter listened in silence, the tired manner resumed.

“Not much. But suggestive. Quite suggestive. You’re a curious young couple. You might succeed where others have failed. I believe in luck, you know—always have.... Well, how about it? You’re out for adventure. How would you like to work for me? All quite unofficial. Expenses paid, and a moderate screw?”

“What should we have to do?”

“Just go on with what you’re doing now. Find Jane Finn.”

“Yes, but—who is Jane Finn?”

“You’re entitled to know that.”

He leaned back. “Secret diplomacy (which, by the way, is nearly always bad policy!) does not concern you. In early 1915, a certain document—a draft of a secret agreement—was drawn up in America. It was dispatched to England by a special messenger, a young fellow called Danvers. He sailed on the Lusitania, carrying the papers in an oilskin packet next his skin. The Lusitania was torpedoed. Danvers was among the missing; later his body was washed ashore—identified beyond doubt. But the oilskin packet was missing.

“Had it been taken? Or had he passed it on? After the torpedo struck, Danvers was seen speaking to a young American girl. No one actually saw him pass anything, but it’s likely he entrusted the papers to her, believing she had a better chance. Her name was Jane Finn. She appeared among the survivors; then vanished. We traced her across Ireland; once she set foot in England, nothing. The treaty was never used; the war shifted; the thing was forgotten.”

Tuppence broke in: “But why has it cropped up again? The war’s over.”

“Because it seems the papers were not destroyed after all, and might be resurrected with a new and deadly significance. Five years ago that draft treaty was a weapon in our hands; to-day it is a weapon against us. If its terms were made public, it would mean disaster.... It might possibly bring about another war—not with Germany this time! It implicates a number of our statesmen whom we cannot afford to have discredited. As a party cry for Labour it would be irresistible; but that is a mere nothing to the real danger.”

He went on quietly: “There is Bolshevist influence at work behind the present Labour unrest. Bolshevist gold is pouring into this country for the specific purpose of procuring a Revolution. And there is a certain man—his real name unknown—working in the dark for his own ends. The Bolshevists are behind the Labour unrest—but this man is behind the Bolshevists. He is always spoken of by the unassuming title of ‘Mr. Brown.’ He is the master criminal of this age. He controls a marvellous organization. Most of the Peace propaganda during the war was originated and financed by him. His spies are everywhere.”

“A naturalized German?” asked Tommy.

“On the contrary, I have every reason to believe he is an Englishman.”

“Where we find his tracks,” Carter continued, “somebody else has the chief rôle; afterwards we discover some nonentity in the background—and Mr. Brown has escaped us once more.”

“I wonder—” said Tuppence slowly. “In Mr. Whittington’s office the clerk—he called him Brown. He brought a telephone message.”

“Very likely,” said Carter. “That ‘message’ was Mr. Brown’s way of giving an order. He overheard the whole conversation. Was it after that that Whittington gave you money and an appointment for the following day?”

Tuppence nodded.

“Yes, undoubtedly the hand of Mr. Brown! Well, there it is—you see what you’re pitting yourselves against. I don’t quite like it. You’re such young things. I shouldn’t like anything to happen to you.”

“It won’t,” said Tuppence positively.

“I’ll look after her, sir,” said Tommy.

“And I’ll look after you,” retorted Tuppence.

“Then look after each other.” He smiled, then grew grave. “There’s something mysterious about this draft treaty. We’ve been threatened with it. The Revolutionary element as good as declare it’s in their hands—and intend to produce it. Yet they’re at fault about many provisions. It’s as though they’d got hold of an incriminating document, but couldn’t read it because it was in cipher—only we know it wasn’t in cipher. But there’s something. Of course Jane Finn may be dead—but I don’t think so. The curious thing is that they’re trying to get information about the girl from us.”

“What?”

“They know we’re looking. They’ll produce a Jane Finn of their own—say at a pensionnat in Paris.” Tuppence gasped; he smiled. “No one knows what she looks like. She’s primed with a trumped-up tale; her real business is to get as much information as possible out of us. See?”

“Then you think it was as Jane Finn they wanted me to go to Paris?”

“I believe in coincidences, you know,” said Mr. Carter wearily.

“Well,” said Tuppence, recovering herself, “it really seems as though it were meant to be.”

“I know what you mean,” said Carter. “I’m superstitious myself. Luck, and all that. But I warn you the pace may be forced. When you saw Whittington, they had time before them. I have information that the big coup was planned for early in the new year. The Government contemplates legislative action to deal effectually with the strike menace. They’ll get wind of it, and that may bring things to a head. The less time they have to mature their plans the better. Don’t be cast down if you fail. It’s not an easy proposition.”

Tuppence grew businesslike. “What exactly can we count upon you for, Mr. Carter?”

“Funds within reason, detailed information on any point, and no official recognition. If you get into trouble with the police, I can’t officially help you. You’re on your own.”

“I quite understand. I’ll write out a list of things I want to know. Now—about money—”

“Do you want to say how much?”

“Not exactly. We’ve got plenty to go with for the present, but when we want more—”

“It will be waiting for you.”

“Yes, but—you know one really has the devil of a time getting anything out of it! And if we have to fill up a blue form and send it in, and then, after three months, they send us a green one, and so on—well, that won’t be much use, will it?”

Mr. Carter laughed outright.

“Don’t worry, Miss Tuppence. You will send a personal demand to me here, and the money, in notes, shall be sent by return of post. As to salary—shall we say three hundred a year? And an equal sum for Mr. Beresford.”

Tuppence beamed. “How lovely. You are kind. I do love money! I’ll keep beautiful accounts—debit and credit, and the balance on the right side, and a red line with the totals the same at the bottom. I really know how to do it when I think.”

“I’m sure you do. Good-bye, and good luck.”

A minute later they were on the steps of 27 Carshalton Terrace, heads in a whirl.

“Tommy! Tell me at once—who is ‘Mr. Carter’?” He murmured a name.

“Oh!” said Tuppence, impressed. “I like him, don’t you? He looks so awfully tired and bored, and yet you feel that underneath he’s just like steel, all keen and flashing. Oh!” She gave a skip. “Pinch me, Tommy, do pinch me. I can’t believe it’s real!”

Mr. Beresford obliged.

“Ow! That’s enough! Yes, we’re not dreaming. We’ve got a job!”

“The joint venture has really begun,” said Tommy. “Julius P. Hersheimmer!”

“We never told Mr. Carter about hearing from him,” said Tommy. “Come on—we’d better take a taxi.”

“All expenses paid,” said Tuppence luxuriously. “I’m sure blackmailers never arrive in buses.”

“We’ve ceased being blackmailers,” Tommy pointed out.

“I’m not sure I have,” said Tuppence darkly.

Shown at once to Mr. Hersheimmer’s suite, they heard an impatient “Come in.” Julius P. Hersheimmer was younger than they had imagined, squarely built, pugnacious but pleasant, unmistakably American.

“Get my note? Sit down and tell me right away all you know about my cousin.”

“Your cousin?”

“Sure thing. Jane Finn. My father and her mother were brother and sister.”

“Oh!” cried Tuppence. “Then you know where she is?”

“No!” He brought his fist down. “I’m darned if I do! Don’t you?”

“We advertised to receive information, not to give it,” said Tuppence severely.

“I can read,” said Julius dryly. “But I thought maybe it was her back history you were after, and that you’d know where she was now?”

Tommy explained that they were “private inquiry agents,” commissioned to find her, and would be glad of any details.

“Put me wise,” said Julius; and, when Tommy had given him a guarded outline, he nodded.

“That’s all right. I was just a mite hasty. London gets my goat! I only know little old New York. Trot out your questions.”

Tuppence plunged: “When did you last see your—cousin?”

“Never seen her,” said Julius. He elaborated with American directness. His father had quarrelled with Aunt Jane when she married a poor school-teacher out West; they had never corresponded. The old man did pile it up. “He went into oil, and he went into steel, and he played a bit with railroads, and I can tell you he made Wall Street sit up!” Then he died, and Julius got the dollars—and a conscience. He hired a man to hunt Aunt Jane down. Result: Aunt and Amos Finn were dead, but there was a daughter—Jane—torpedoed in the Lusitania on her way to Paris. Saved, but not heard of over this side. “I guessed they weren’t hustling any, so I thought I’d come along over, and speed things up. I phoned Scotland Yard and the Admiralty first thing. The Admiralty rather choked me off, but Scotland Yard were very civil—said they would make inquiries, even sent a man round this morning to get her photograph. I’m off to Paris to-morrow, just to see what the Prefecture is doing. I guess if I go to and fro hustling them, they ought to get busy!”

“Then we can work together,” said Tuppence. Lunch was proposed and accepted. Oysters had given place to sole when a card was brought in. “A Scotland Yard inspector again,” grumbled Julius. “Another man this time. What does he expect I can tell him that I didn’t tell the first chap? I hope they haven’t lost that photograph. The Western photographer’s place was burned down—this is the only copy.”

A sudden dread swept over Tuppence. “You—you don’t know the name of the man who came this morning?”

“Yes, I do. No, I don’t—half a second—it was on his card. Oh, I know! Inspector Brown. Quiet, unassuming sort of chap.”

A veil might with profit be drawn over the next half-hour. Suffice it to say that no such person as “Inspector Brown” was known to Scotland Yard, and the photograph of Jane Finn, which would have been of the utmost value, was lost beyond recovery. Once again “Mr. Brown” had triumphed.

The reverse brought Julius Hersheimmer and the Young Adventurers into sudden intimacy. Barriers down, Tommy and Tuppence told him the whole story; Julius declared himself “tickled to death.” He turned to Tuppence at the close of the narration. “I’ve always had a kind of idea that English girls were just a mite moss-grown. Old-fashioned and sweet, you know, but scared to move round without a footman or a maiden aunt. I guess I’m a bit behind the times!”

At Tuppence’s suggestion that they keep in touch with Jane’s only relation, they installed themselves forthwith at the Ritz. “Put like that,” she added to Tommy, “nobody could boggle at the expense!” Nobody did.

“And now—to work!” she announced next morning.

“My union, Tuppence, my union! It does not permit me to work before 11 a.m.”

“Tommy, you’ll have something thrown at you. It is absolutely essential that we should without delay map out a plan of campaign.”

Tommy laid his paper finally aside. “There’s something of the simplicity of the truly great mind about you, Tuppence. Fire ahead. I’m listening.”

“To begin with—what have we to go upon?”

“Absolutely nothing,” said Tommy cheerily.

“Wrong. We have two distinct clues.”

“What are they?”

“First, we know one of the gang.”

“Whittington?”

“Yes. I’d recognize him anywhere.”

“Hum. I don’t call that much of a clue. You don’t know where to look for him, and it’s about a thousand to one against your running against him by accident.”

“I’m not so sure. Once coincidences start happening they go on happening in the most extraordinary way. Still, we can’t rely on that. But there are places in London where simply everyone is bound to turn up sooner or later—Piccadilly Circus, for instance. One of my ideas was to take up my stand there every day with a tray of flags.”

“What about meals?” inquired the practical Tommy.

“How like a man! What does mere food matter?”

“That’s all very well. You’ve just had a thundering good breakfast. No one’s got a better appetite than you have, Tuppence, and by tea-time you’d be eating the flags, pins and all. But honestly, I don’t think much of the idea. Whittington mayn’t be in London at all.”

“That’s true. Anyway, clue No. 2 is more promising. We’ve a Christian name—Rita. Whittington mentioned it that day. Danvers was shadowed on the way over, and it’s more likely to have been a woman, and a good-looking one. Obviously she was saved—if she wasn’t, how would they have known Jane had the papers? Now there’s just a chance that this woman may have been ‘Rita.’ If so, we’ve got to hunt through the survivors of the Lusitania till we find her.”

“Then the first thing is to get a list.”

“I wrote to Mr. Carter. I got his reply this morning, and among other things it encloses the official statement of those saved from the Lusitania.”

“But very few Christian names. Nearly all Mrs. or Miss.”

“That complicates matters.”

“We’ll just get down to it. London first. Note the addresses of any women in or near town while I put on my hat.”

They set out for the first address, The Laurels. At the gate, as Tuppence was about to ring, Tommy checked her. “What are you going to say?”

“What am I going to say? Oh dear, I don’t know. It’s very awkward.”

“I thought as much,” said Tommy with satisfaction. “How like a woman! No foresight! Now just stand aside, and see how easily the mere male deals with the situation.” He pressed the bell, produced notebook and pencil, and, as a slatternly servant appeared, rattled off his glib formula from the “Borough Council” about the new voting register; “Mrs. Edgar Keith—Christian name?”

“Eleanor Jane.”

“Thank you.” He rejoined Tuppence.

“I don’t mind admitting that for once you’ve scored handsomely. I should never have thought of that.”

They repeated the wheeze ad lib; a couple of fruitless calls later—Tommy’s impersonation had supplied many a Christian name and slammed many a door—they lunched briefly, and Tuppence read from the notebook: “Mrs. Vandemeyer, 20 South Audley Mansions.”

“Then the Mayfair lady is clearly indicated,” said Tommy. “And stifle this longing for vulgar sensation, Tuppence. It’s a wonder Mr. Brown has not ere now done us to death.”

“Still, it is queer he hasn’t wreaked vengeance upon us. We pass on our way unscathed,” she returned, and rose.

South Audley Mansions rose imposing just off Park Lane. No. 20’s door was opened by an elderly woman of the housekeeper type. Tommy delivered his glib formula.

“Christian name?”

“Margaret.”

Tommy spelt it; the woman corrected him: “No—g u e.”

“Oh, Marguerite—the French way. We had her down as ‘Rita’ Vandemeyer—”

“She’s mostly called that, sir, but Marguerite’s her name.”

“Thank you. That’s all.”

Hardly containing himself, Tommy rejoined Tuppence. “You heard?”

“Yes. Oh, Tommy!”

“I know, old thing.”

“It’s—it’s so lovely to think of things—and then for them really to happen!” cried Tuppence enthusiastically.

Her hand still in his, they reached the entrance hall. Voices and footsteps sounded from above. Suddenly Tuppence dragged him into the shadow by the lift.

“Hush!”

Two men descended and passed out. Tuppence’s fingers tightened. “Quick—follow them. I daren’t. He might recognize me. I don’t know who the other man is, but the bigger of the two was Whittington.”

Whittington and his companion walked fast. Tommy started in pursuit at once, and, as the small Mayfair streets were comparatively deserted, he judged it wise to content himself with keeping them in sight. The sport was a new one to him. Though familiar with the technicalities from a course of novel reading, he had never before attempted to “follow” anyone, and it appeared to him at once that, in actual practice, the proceeding was fraught with difficulties. Supposing, for instance, that they should suddenly hail a taxi? In books, you simply leapt into another, promised the driver a sovereign—or its modern equivalent—and there you were. In actual fact, Tommy foresaw that it was extremely likely there would be no second taxi. Therefore he would have to run. In a main road he might hope to create the illusion that he was merely running for a bus, but in these obscure aristocratic byways he could not but feel that an officious policeman might stop him to explain matters.

They zigzagged to Oxford Street, and just before the Bond Street Tube crossed to the big Lyons’. On the first floor, with the place thinning, they took a window table. Tommy slipped in behind Whittington, ordered a Welsh rarebit and coffee he did not want, and studied the other man: fair, weak, unpleasant; small crafty eyes; shoulders cringing a little. “Boris,” Whittington called him.

Tommy caught “Ireland” several times, and “propaganda.” Then, in a lull: “Ah, but you don’t know Flossie,” said Whittington. “She’s a marvel. An archbishop would swear she was his own mother. She gets the voice right every time—and that’s really the principal thing.”

They lowered their voices again. Presently two words pricked Tommy’s ears like needles: “Mr. Brown.”

“Why not, my friend?” the other laughed softly.

“It is a name most respectable—most common. Did he not choose it for that reason? Ah, I should like to meet him—Mr. Brown.”

“Who knows? You may have met him already,” said Whittington, his voice steely.

“Bah! That is children’s talk—a fable for the police. Sometimes I say to myself he is invented by the Inner Ring—a bogy to frighten us. It might be so. And it might not. I wonder—perhaps he is with us and amongst us, unknown to all but a chosen few. If so, he keeps his secret well. We never know. We look at each other—one of us is Mr. Brown—which? He commands—but also he serves. Among us—in the midst of us. And no one knows which he is....”

Boris glanced at his watch. “Yes, we might as well go.”

Outside, Whittington hailed a taxi for Waterloo. Tommy sprang into another. At the station Whittington took a first-class single to Bournemouth. Boris said, “You are early. You have nearly half an hour.”

Tommy hesitated an instant, then dived into a telephone box. Tuppence was somewhere near South Audley Mansions; there was no time to find her. He rang the Ritz.

“Hersheimmer? Beresford speaking. I’m at Waterloo. I’ve followed Whittington and another man. Whittington by the 3.30 to Bournemouth. Can you get there?”

“Sure. I’ll hustle.”

Tommy returned to the concourse and watched minutes creep: 3.15—3.20—3.27—3.29. Doors were banging when a hand fell on his shoulder.

“Here I am, son. Your British traffic beats description. Put me wise to the crooks.”

“That’s Whittington—getting in now. The foreign chap is the other.”

“Which is my bird?”

“Got any money?” asked Tommy anxiously.

Julius looked blank, then confessed: “I guess I haven’t more than three or four hundred dollars with me at the moment.”

“Oh, Lord, you millionaires!” Tommy thrust a ticket into his hand. “Whittington’s your man.”

“Me for Whittington!” The train slid out. “So long, Tommy.”

The platform, emptier, showed Boris moving off. Tommy let him pass, then followed.

Tube to Piccadilly Circus; up Shaftesbury Avenue; into the mean streets of Soho. They came to a small dilapidated square where the houses had an evil, decayed air. The place was a cul-de-sac; no traffic passed. Boris looked round furtively; Tommy shrank into a doorway. He mounted the steps of a particularly sinister house and rapped sharply with a peculiar rhythm. The door opened at once; a word to the doorkeeper, and he went in.

What he ought to have done, what any sane man would have done, was to remain patiently where he was and wait for his man to come out again. What he did do was entirely foreign to the sober common sense which was, as a rule, his leading characteristic. Something, as he expressed it, seemed to snap in his brain. He went up and reproduced, as far as he was able, the peculiar knock.

A villainous-faced man with close-cropped hair opened.

“Well?” he grunted.

“Mr. Brown?” said Tommy.

The man stood aside. “Upstairs—second door on your left.”

If audacity had carried him so far, let it carry him farther. Tommy mounted the filthy ramshackle stairs; the grimy paper hung in festoons, cobwebs choked every angle. The man below had retired—clearly no suspicion attached to a caller for Mr. Brown.

At the top a narrow passage ran right and left. From the nearest door on the left came a murmur of voices: the very room indicated. But opposite, half hidden by a torn velvet curtain, yawned a small recess commanding both that door and the head of the stairs—an ideal hiding-place. Tommy weighed his chances. “Mr. Brown” had likely been a password. Luck had carried him to the threshold; to go in was to risk immediate detection. He slipped into the recess and drew the curtain across.

The first arrival he saw was a man from the very dregs: beetling brows, bestial jaw. He knocked, was challenged: “Your number, comrade?”

“Fourteen, gov’nor.”

“Correct.” Through the brief opening Tommy glimpsed a long table, four or five seated round it, and at the head a tall man with close-cropped hair and a short pointed naval beard. His enunciation was correct and curiously precise. “If that isn’t a Hun, I’m a Dutchman!” Tommy told himself. “Lucky I didn’t roll in. I’d have given the wrong number.”

Another knock: an Irish Sinn Feiner. Certainly Mr. Brown’s organization was a far-reaching concern. The common criminal, the well-bred Irish gentleman, the pale Russian, and the efficient German master of the ceremonies! Truly a strange and sinister gathering! Who was this man who held in his finger these curiously variegated links of an unknown chain?

Then a shabbily dressed city clerk; a working-man with a vaguely familiar face; a commanding, exquisitely dressed gentleman Tommy felt he had seen before. Silence—Tommy was creeping out when a last-comer glided up so softly he was upon him before Tommy sensed it: a small very pale man, Slav cheek-bones, strange light eyes that seemed to burn through the curtain. Tommy shivered. He was no more fanciful than the majority of young Englishmen, but he could not rid himself of the impression that some unusually potent force emanated from the man. The creature reminded him of a venomous snake.

The newcomer knocked. Inside, every man rose. The bearded German came forward, heels clicking. “We are honoured. I much feared it would be impossible.”

“There were difficulties,” hissed the other. “It will not be possible again, I fear. But one meeting is essential—to define my policy. I can do nothing without—Mr. Brown. He is here?”

“We have received a message,” the German said, and hesitated.

“It is impossible for him to be present in person—”

A very slow smile passed over the pale face. He looked round at the uneasy circle. “Ah! I understand. He works in the dark and trusts no one. But all the same, it is possible that he is among us now....” He looked round him again, and again that expression of fear swept over the group. Each man seemed eyeing his neighbour doubtfully. The Russian tapped his cheek. “So be it. Let us proceed.”

The German indicated the head of the table; the other demurred; the German insisted: “It is the only possible place—for Number One. Perhaps Number Fourteen will shut the door?”

Tommy was once more facing bare panels. Impatience pricked him. He slipped off his shoes, tried the handle inch by inch; it would not yield. Locked or bolted. With the same infinite care he reversed the handle. There was a certain bulldog tenacity about Tommy that made him slow to admit defeat. Checkmated for the moment, he was far from abandoning the conflict. He still intended to hear what was going on in the locked room. As one plan had failed, he must hunt about for another.

He crept to the next room along, found it empty and filthy as the rest—and, blessedly, a communicating door by the window. The rusty bolt gave to patient wriggling; he turned the handle; a velvet portière within checked his view, but he could hear.

The Sinn Feiner’s rich voice: “That’s all very well. But more money is essential. No money—no results!”

Boris: “Will you guarantee there are results?”

“In a month from now—as you wish—I will guarantee you such a reign of terror in Ireland as shall shake the British Empire to its foundations.”

Number One’s soft sibilants: “Good! You shall have the money. Boris, you will see to that.”

“Via the Irish Americans, and Mr. Potter as usual?”

“I guess that’ll be all right,” drawled an American voice, “though I’d like to point out things are getting a mite difficult. There’s not the sympathy there was, and a disposition to let the Irish settle their own affairs.”

“Does that matter, since the money only nominally comes from the States?”

“The chief difficulty is landing the ammunition,” said the Sinn Feiner. “The money is conveyed in easily enough—thanks to our colleague here.”

“Think of the feelings of Belfast if they could hear you!” said the commanding Englishman drily.

“That is settled, then,” hissed Number One. “Now, the loan to an English newspaper—you have arranged the details, Boris?”

“I think so.”

“That is good. An official denial from Moscow will be forthcoming if necessary.”

Papers rustled; the German’s clear voice: “I am directed by—Mr. Brown to place the summaries of the reports from the different unions. That of the miners is most satisfactory. We must hold back the railways. There may be trouble with the A.S.E.”

Silence while papers turned; then the light tap-tap of fingers drumming on wood. “And—the date, my friend?”

“The 29th.”

“That is rather soon.”

“I know. But it was settled by the principal Labour leaders, and we cannot seem to interfere too much. They must believe it to be entirely their own show.”

The Russian laughed softly. “Yes, yes. They must have no inkling that we are using them for our own ends. They are honest men—and that is their value to us. It is curious—but you cannot make a revolution without honest men. The instinct of the populace is infallible. Every revolution has had its honest men. They are soon disposed of afterwards.”

“The next point,” resumed the German. “Clymes must go. He is too far-seeing. Number Fourteen will see to that.”

A hoarse assent: “That’s all right, gov’nor…. Suppose I’m nabbed?”

“You will have the best legal talent,” said the German coolly. “In any case you will wear gloves fitted with the finger-prints of a notorious housebreaker. You have little to fear.”

“Oh, I ain’t afraid. All for the good of the cause. The streets is going to run with blood, so they say.” He chuckled. “Dreams of it, sometimes, I does. And diamonds and pearls rolling about in the gutter for anyone to pick up!”

“Then all is arranged. We are assured of success?”

“I—think so.” Less confidence than before.

“What has gone wrong?”

“Nothing; but—the Labour leaders. Without them we can do nothing. If they do not declare a general strike on the 29th—”

“Why should they not?”

“They are honest. And, in spite of everything we’ve done to discredit the Government, I’m not sure they haven’t a sneaking belief in it. On the whole, public opinion swings to the Government. They will not go against it.”

“To the point. I was given to understand there was a certain document which assured success.”

“That is so. If that document were placed before the leaders, they would publish it broadcast and declare for revolution without a moment’s hesitation. The Government would be broken.”

“Then what more do you want?”

“The document itself.”

“Ah! It is not in your possession? But you know where it is?”

“No.”

“Does anyone know?”

“One person—perhaps. And we are not sure even of that.”

“Who is this person?”

“A girl.”

Tommy held his breath.

“A girl?” The Russian’s contempt was icy. “And you have not made her speak? In Russia we have ways of making a girl talk.”

“This case is different,” said the German sullenly.

“How—different? Where is the girl now?”

“She is—”

A crashing blow descended on Tommy’s head. All was darkness.

When Tommy set forth on the trail of the two men, it took all Tuppence’s self-command not to run after him. Consoling herself that her “Rita” deduction had panned out, she drifted back to the entrance hall of South Audley Mansions. A small lift-boy was polishing brass and whistling. He glanced up. There was a certain amount of the gamin element in the girl, at all events she invariably got on well with small boys. A sympathetic bond seemed instantly to be formed.

“Well, William,” she said cheerfully, “getting a good shine up?”

“Albert, miss,” he corrected, grinning.

“Albert be it.” She looked round mysteriously, leaned in, and, with an ample gesture, flipped back her coat to show a small enamelled badge. It had lately pinned some flowers, but she had spied the corner of a threepenny detective tale in Albert’s pocket.

“American Detective Force!” she hissed.

Albert fell for it, eyes growing round. “Lord!”

“Know who I’m after?” She jerked a thumb upstairs. “No. 20. Calls herself Vandemeyer. Vandemeyer! Ha! ha!”

“A crook?” breathed Albert.

“A crook? I should say so. ‘Ready Rita’ they call her in the States.”

“Ready Rita,” he repeated ecstatically.

“Oh, ain’t it just like the pictures!”

It was. Tuppence was a great frequenter of the cinema.

“Has she been wearing any of the emeralds?” Tuppence asked carelessly.

“Emeralds? Them’s the green stones, ain’t they?”

“Peter B. Rysdale, the oil king,” said Tuppence promptly. “Finest collection of emeralds in the world. Worth a million dollars!”

“Lumme! It sounds more like the pictures every minute.”

She nodded, gratified. “We haven’t exactly proved it yet. But we’re after her. And”—Tuppence favoured him with a long-drawn wink—“I guess she won’t get away with the goods this time. Mind you, not a word—in the States we know a smart lad when we see one.”

“I’ll not breathe a word,” protested Albert eagerly. “Ain’t there anything I could do? A bit o’ shadowing?”

“Not the moment,” said Tuppence, considering. “But see here—didn’t you say a girl upstairs was leaving?”

“Annie. Regular turn up there’s been.”

“How would it be if you mentioned a young cousin—or a friend of yours had—who’d suit the place?”

“I’m there,” said Albert, radiant. “Leave it to me. I’ll fix the whole thing up in two ticks.”

“You might say the young woman could come in right away. Let me know at the Ritz—name of Cowley.”

“It must be a good job, this tec business,” sighed Albert, envious.

“It sure is—when old man Rysdale backs the bill. But don’t fret, son. If this goes well, you shall come in on the ground floor.”

Well pleased with her morning’s work, Tuppence walked briskly away. At the Ritz she scrawled a brief note to Mr. Carter; then, Tommy not yet returned (which did not surprise her), she set forth to shop—cheap clothing, a second-hand place or two, and finally a fashionable hairdresser, with tea and creamy cakes by the way. In her room, five minutes turned a dark-headed V.A.D. into a demure fair-haired parlourmaid. With an actress’s pencil she altered her brows; a new luxuriant growth of pale hair sat well above; she would add elevators to her shoes; the cap and apron would do the rest. “Yes,” said Tuppence to the pert face in the glass, “you’ll do.”

Dinner was solitary, and Tommy had not returned. Julius, likely, was harrying Prefects of Police on the Continent or telephones at the Admiralty. “All very well—but this is very dull for me!” said Tuppence. She summoned a small boy and, ten minutes later, was deep in Garnaby Williams, the Boy Detective, fortifying herself with local colour before further intercourse with Albert.

Morning brought Mr. Carter’s reply:

“Dear Miss Tuppence,—You have made a splendid start. But I warn you again: those people are desperate, and I can promise you no protection. If you choose to withdraw now, no one could blame you. If, despite my warnings, you go on, you will find everything arranged. You have lived for two years with Miss Dufferin, The Parsonage, Llanelly; Mrs. Vandemeyer can apply there for a reference. Stick as near to the truth as possible—it minimizes the danger of slips. Represent yourself as what you are, a former V.A.D. who has chosen domestic service as a profession. Whichever way you decide, good luck.—Your sincere friend, MR. CARTER.”

Tuppence’s spirits rose. His warnings passed unheeded; but she sensibly adopted his plan. A dirty postcard arrived too: “It’s O.K.” scrawled in Tommy’s hand.

By half-past ten a battered tin trunk held her purchases, artistically corded. With a blush, she had it placed in a taxi, deposited it at Paddington cloak-room, and repaired to the ladies’ waiting-room. Ten minutes later a metamorphosed Tuppence walked demurely out and took a bus.

At a few minutes past eleven she re-entered South Audley Mansions. Albert, vaguely relegated by a magnificent hall porter, was on the look-out, and did not at first recognize her.

“Blest if I’d have known you! That rig-out’s top-hole.”

“By the way, am I your cousin, or am I not?”

“I said as a friend o’ mine knew a young gal,” returned Albert, delighted with her “as English as anything!” voice. “Annie’s stopped on till to-day—to oblige, she said—but really it’s so as to put you against the place.”

“Nice girl,” said Tuppence, and quelled his wink with a glance as he whisked her up in the lift.

She rang No. 20. A smart maid answered, and in a moment Tuppence was ushered into a room on the right of the long passage.

A woman stood by the fireplace. Beauty there was, but hardened and menacing: pale gold hair, coiled low; eyes of a piercing electric blue that seemed to bore into one’s very soul; a red curving mouth with a cruel line. Indigo charmeuse enhanced a figure of exquisite grace, and yet through all that loveliness glinted a metallic strength—the steely ring in her voice, the gimlet quality of her gaze. For the first time Tuppence felt afraid. She had not feared Whittington, but this woman was different. As if fascinated, she watched the long cruel line of the red curving mouth, and again the sensation of panic swept her. Her usual self-confidence deserted her. Vaguely she felt that deceiving this woman would be very different to deceiving Whittington. Mr. Carter’s warning recurred to her mind. Here, indeed, she might expect no mercy.

Fighting down that instinct of panic which urged her to turn tail and run without further delay, Tuppence returned the lady’s gaze firmly and respectfully.

Mrs. Vandemeyer motioned to a chair. “You can sit down. How did you hear I wanted a house-parlourmaid?”

“Through a friend who knows the lift-boy here. He thought the place might suit me.”

“You speak like an educated girl?”

Glibly and on Carter’s lines, Tuppence told her tale: V.A.D. service, now taking up domestic work. The tension seemed to relax.

“Is there anyone I can write to for a reference?”

“I lived last with Miss Dufferin, The Parsonage, Llanelly. I was with her two years.”

“And then you thought you would get more money by coming to London, I suppose? Well, it doesn’t matter to me. I will give you £50—£60—whatever you want. You can come in at once?”

“To-day, if you like. My box is at Paddington.”

“Go and fetch it in a taxi, then. It is an easy place. I am out a good deal. By the way, what’s your name?”

“Prudence Cooper, ma’am.”

“Very well, Prudence. Go away and fetch your box. I shall be out to lunch. The cook will show you where everything is.”

Tuppence withdrew meekly past the magnificent porter, Albert nowhere in evidence. The adventure had begun; but, suddenly, her elation dropped. If the unknown Jane Finn had fallen into Mrs. Vandemeyer’s hands, it was likely to have gone hard with her.

ENTER SIR JAMES PEEL EDGERTON

Tuppence betrayed no awkwardness in her new duties. The archdeacon’s daughters had been well grounded in household tasks and in training a “raw girl.”

Mrs. Vandemeyer’s cook puzzled her: a first-rate hand, yet in deadly terror of her mistress, as though there were a hold somewhere.

A guest was expected to dinner. Tuppence, laying the beautifully polished table for two, hoped the visitor would not prove to be Whittington. At a few minutes past eight the bell rang. She opened the door with inward trepidation, and was relieved to see the second of the two men whom Tommy had followed.

“Count Stepanov,” he said.

“It is delightful to see you, Boris Ivanovitch,” purred Mrs. Vandemeyer, rising from the divan.

In the kitchen, Tuppence affected frank curiosity. “Who’s he?”

“A Russian gentleman, I believe,” said the cook shortly.

“Comes here much?”

“Once in a while.”

Whilst waiting at table, Tuppence listened closely. The talk ran on indifferent things—plays, dances, gossip. After dinner she carried in coffee and liqueurs to the boudoir where Mrs. Vandemeyer, stretched on the divan, looked more wickedly beautiful than ever.

“New, isn’t she?” said Boris.

“She came in to-day. The other was a fiend. This girl seems all right. She waits well.”

“Quite safe, I suppose?”

“Really, Boris, you are absurdly suspicious. I believe she’s the cousin of the hall porter, or something of the kind. And nobody even dreams that I have any connection with our—mutual friend, Mr. Brown.”

“For heaven’s sake, be careful, Rita. That door isn’t shut.”

“Well, shut it then,” laughed Mrs. Vandemeyer.

Tuppence, who had lingeringly “forgotten” the door, fled, and, clearing away with hospital speed, cast about for a safer vantage. The balcony! Mrs. Vandemeyer’s bedroom opened on to a long balcony that ran the length of the flat. Slipping through the French windows, Tuppence crept along to the boudoir. The window there stood a little ajar; the voices within came plainly.

They were at variance.

“With your persistent recklessness, you will end by ruining us!” Boris burst out.

“Bah!” laughed the woman. “Notoriety of the right kind is the best way of disarming suspicion. You will realize that one of these days—perhaps sooner than you think!”

“In the meantime you go everywhere with Peel Edgerton. Not only is he, perhaps, the most celebrated K.C. in England, but his special hobby is criminology! It is madness!”

“I know that his eloquence has saved untold men from the gallows,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer calmly. “What of it? I may need his assistance in that line myself some day. If so, how fortunate to have such a friend at court—or shall I say, in court?”

“You are a clever woman, Rita; but you are also a fool! Be guided by me, and give up Peel Edgerton.”

“I think not.”

“You refuse?” There was an ugly ring in the Russian’s voice.

“I do.”

“Then, by Heaven, we will see——”

She rose too, eyes flashing. “You forget, Boris. I am accountable to no one. I take my orders only from—Mr. Brown.”

He threw up his hands. “Impossible! Already it may be too late. They say Peel Edgerton can smell a criminal! Perhaps even now his suspicions are aroused.”

“Reassure yourself, my dear Boris. I am commonly accounted a beautiful woman. I assure you that is all that interests Peel Edgerton.”

“He has studied crime as no other man has studied it. Do you fancy that you can deceive him?”

Her eyes narrowed. “If he is all that you say—it would amuse me to try!”

“Good heavens, Rita——”

“Besides, he is extremely rich. I am not one who despises money. The sinews of war, you know, Boris!”

“Money—money! That is always the danger with you. I believe you would sell your soul for money. I believe——” He dropped his voice, slow and sinister. “Sometimes I believe you would sell—us!”

Mrs. Vandemeyer shrugged. “The price, at any rate, would have to be enormous,” she said lightly.

“Beyond the power of anyone but a millionaire to pay.”

“Ah!” snarled the Russian. “You see!”

“My dear Boris, can you not take a joke?”

“Was it a joke?”

“Of course.”

“Then your ideas of humour are peculiar.”

“Let us not quarrel. Touch the bell. We will have some drinks.”

Tuppence beat a retreat, smoothed her apron in the long glass, and answered the summons demurely. The talk she had overheard proved the complicity of both Rita and Boris, named Mr. Brown, and showed Rita’s cool audacity; but there was no word of Jane Finn, nor anything of Tommy. The omission gnawed at her. Before leaving the Ritz she had arranged that all messages should be sent at once to a small stationer’s near at hand, where Albert was to call frequently. Morning brought the report: nothing.

A cold hand seemed to close round her heart. Supposing— She choked it down. Worry was no use.

“What day do you usually go out, Prudence?” asked Mrs. Vandemeyer later, idly watching the bright polish travel over silver.

“Friday’s my usual day, ma’am.”

“And to-day is Friday! But I suppose you hardly wish to go out to-day, as you only came yesterday.”

“I was thinking of asking if I might, ma’am.”

Mrs. Vandemeyer studied her, smiling, catlike. “I wish Count Stepanov could hear you. He made a suggestion about you last night.” The smile broadened. “Your request is very—typical. You can go out to-day. It makes no difference to me, as I shall not be dining at home.”

Tuppence thanked her, and, once out of that stare, breathed more freely. Beautiful as the woman was, Tuppence confessed to herself that she was horribly afraid of her.

The front bell rang. Tuppence went to answer it. This time the visitor was neither Whittington nor Boris, but a man of striking appearance. A shade over average height, he yet conveyed the impression of a big man. Clean-shaven, mobile face—power and force stamped upon it; magnetism seemed to radiate from him.

“Sir James Peel Edgerton,” he said.

Tuppence’s interest quickened. The name was famous. She had heard it said that he might one day be Prime Minister. He was known to have refused office in the interests of his profession, preferring to remain a simple Member for a Scotch constituency.

About a quarter of an hour later the bell rang for his departure. He had given her a piercing glance before. Now, as she handed him his hat and stick, she was conscious of his eyes raking her through.

“Not been doing this long, eh?”

Tuppence stared, astonished.

He nodded to his own thought. “V.A.D. and hard up, I suppose?”

“Did Mrs. Vandemeyer tell you that?” asked Tuppence suspiciously.

“No, child. The look of you told me. Good place here?”

“Very good, thank you, sir.”

“Ah, but there are plenty of good places nowadays. And a change does no harm sometimes.”

“Do you mean——?” began Tuppence.

But he was already on the topmost stair. He looked back with his kindly, shrewd glance. “Just a hint,” he said.

“That’s all.”

Tuppence went back to the pantry more thoughtful than ever.

JULIUS TELLS A STORY

Dressed for her “afternoon out,” Tuppence went first to the stationer’s—no message—then to the Ritz. Tommy had not returned. She resolved to appeal to Mr. Carter, and was addressing a note in Julius Hersheimmer’s sitting-room when the door burst open.

“What the hell—” began Julius, checking himself. “I beg your pardon, Miss Tuppence. Those fools said Beresford hadn’t been here since Wednesday. Is that so?”

Tuppence nodded. “You don’t know where he is?”

“I? How should I know? I haven’t had one darned word from him, though I wired yesterday morning.”

“Your wire’s probably unopened at the office.”

“But where is he?”

“I don’t know. I hoped you might.”

“I haven’t had one darned word from him since we parted at the depot on Wednesday.”

“What depot?”

“Waterloo—your London and South Western road.”

“Waterloo?” frowned Tuppence.

“Didn’t he tell you? He called me up, said to hustle. He was trailing two crooks. The big one was mine—the guy you bluffed. Tommy shoved a ticket into my hand, told me to get aboard. He was going to sleuth the other.” Julius paused. “I thought for sure you’d know all this.”

“Julius,” said Tuppence firmly, “stop walking up and down. It makes me giddy. Sit down in that armchair, and tell me the whole story with as few fancy turns of speech as possible.”

“Sure,” said Julius. “Where shall I begin?”

“At Waterloo.”

“I got into one of your dear old-fashioned first-class British compartments. A guard told me mighty politely I wasn’t in a smoking-carriage. I handed him half a dollar, and that settled that. I prospected along to the next coach—Whittington was there right enough. When I saw the skunk and thought of poor little Jane, I felt real mad I hadn’t got a gun.

“We reached Bournemouth. He took a cab, named an hotel. I did likewise. He sat in the lounge, read papers, dawdled through dinner. I figured his real business would be later, since he hadn’t changed.

“Sure enough, about nine he took a car across town—mighty pretty place by the way, I guess I’ll take Jane there for a spell when I find her—and then paid it off and struck out along the pine-woods on the cliff. I was there too. By degrees the villas thinned out till we came to a big place with piny grounds. It was black as pitch; I heard him ring and get admitted.

“I waited in the rain till I got almighty cold and kind of restive. All the ground-floor windows were shuttered, but upstairs I spotted a light with the curtains not drawn. Opposite there was a tree. I figured if I climbed it, I might see in. The rain had made the boughs mighty slippery, but bit by bit I got level with the window—too far left. Just a strip of curtain and wallpaper. I was going to climb down, when somebody moved. A shadow fell on my bit of wall—by gum, it was Whittington!

“After that, my blood was up. A long branch ran out towards the window. If I could swarm along, I’d get my look. It was mighty uncertain if it’d bear me, but I started. Very cautiously, inch by inch, I crawled along. The bough creaked and swayed in a nasty fashion, and it didn’t do to think of the drop below, but at last I got safely to where I wanted to be.

“The room was bare and hygienic. A table with a lamp. Whittington sat facing me, talking to a woman dressed as a hospital nurse, her back to me. The window was shut; I couldn’t hear. He seemed emphatic—banged the table once or twice. Presently he got up; she came across to the window and looked out. Just then the moon came out. I tried to move back. The jerk was too much for that rotten old branch. With an almighty crash, down it came—and Julius P. Hersheimmer with it!”

“Oh, Julius,” breathed Tuppence. “Go on.”

“I pitched into a good soft bed of earth, but it put me out of action. Next thing I knew, I was in bed with a hospital nurse (not Whittington’s) and a little black-bearded man with gold glasses—medical man written all over him. ‘Ah! So our young friend is coming round again. Capital. Capital.’ He called himself Dr. Hall. ‘This,’ he said, ‘is my private nursing home.’

“I noticed you walked lame,” interpolated Tuppence.

“‘A slight sprain,’ explained the doctor. ‘Nothing serious. You’ll be about again in a couple of days.’ He rubbed his hands, then: ‘I think the facts do call for an explanation.’ ‘Sure, doc,’ I said. ‘I’m sorry about the tree—and the bulbs will be on me. But perhaps you’d like to know what I was doing in your garden?’ ‘I think the facts do call for an explanation,’ he repeated. ‘Well, to begin with, I wasn’t after the spoons.’ He smiled. ‘My first theory. But I soon altered my mind.’ ‘By the way, you are an American, are you not?’ I told him my name. ‘And you?’ ‘I am Dr. Hall, and this is my private nursing home.’ I didn’t know, but I wasn’t going to put him wise.

“I made up my mind in a flash. I mumbled a piece about a girl—stern guardian, nervous breakdown, thought I recognized her among his patients—hence my nocturnal stunt. He took it genial. ‘Quite a romance.’ So I said: ‘Now, doc, will you be frank? Have you here now, or have you had at any time, a young girl called Jane Finn?’ He repeated it, thoughtful. ‘Jane Finn? No. It’s an uncommon name. I should not have been likely to forget it.’

“That laid me out. ‘Another matter,’ I said. ‘When I was hugging that darned branch I thought I recognized an old friend talking to one of your nurses.’ He answered at once: ‘Mr. Whittington, perhaps?’ ‘That’s the fellow. What’s he doing down here?’ Dr. Hall laughed. ‘He came to see one of my nurses—Nurse Edith—who is a niece of his.’ ‘Fancy that! Is he still here?’ ‘No, he went back to town almost immediately.’ ‘But perhaps I could speak to his niece—Nurse Edith?’ The doctor shook his head. ‘I’m afraid that, too, is impossible. Nurse Edith left with a patient to-night also.’ ‘Real unlucky,’ I said. ‘Have you Mr. Whittington’s address?’ ‘I don’t know it. I can write to Nurse Edith for it if you like.’ ‘Don’t say who wants it. I’d like to give him a little surprise.’

“That was about all I could do. I wired Beresford where I was, laid up with a sprained foot, told him to come if he wasn’t busy. No answer. The foot was only ricked, so to-day I said good-bye to the little doctor, asked him to send word if he heard from Nurse Edith, and came right back to town. Say, Miss Tuppence, you’re looking mighty pale!”

“It’s Tommy,” said Tuppence. “What can have happened?”

“Buck up. I guess he’s all right. Maybe he’s gone abroad—Poland or something?”

“He couldn’t—passports and things. Besides, I’ve seen that man Boris since. He dined with Mrs. Vandemeyer last night.”

“Mrs. Who?”

“I forgot—you don’t know.” And Tuppence related the last two days—how she had entered Mrs. Vandemeyer’s service, seen Boris, and the steely glitter in her mistress’s eyes.

“Bully for you! Fancy you a menial. It just tickles me to death!” Then, gravely: “But I don’t like it. You’re plucky as they make ’em, but these crooks would as soon croak a girl as a man.”

“Do you think I’m afraid?” said Tuppence indignantly. “I’ve written to Mr. Carter,” she added, telling him the gist of her letter.

“I guess that’s good as far as it goes. But it’s for us to get busy and do something.”

“What can we do?” asked Tuppence, her spirits rising.

“We’d better get on the track of Boris. You say he’s been to your place. Is he likely to come again?”

“He might. I don’t know.”

“I’ll buy a car, dress as a chauffeur, and hang about outside. If Boris comes, you make a signal, and I’ll trail him. How’s that?”

“Splendid—but he mightn’t come for weeks.”

“We’ll have to chance that. I’m glad you like the plan.” He rose.

“Where are you going?”

“To buy the car, of course. What make do you like? I guess you’ll do some riding in it before we’ve finished.”

“I like Rolls-Royces, but—”

“Sure. What you say goes. I’ll get one.”

“But you can’t at once. People wait ages sometimes.”

“Little Julius doesn’t. Don’t you worry. I’ll be round in half an hour.”

“Oh; but I forgot—I met Sir James Peel Edgerton this morning,” said Tuppence, and told of the ambiguous warning.

Julius negatived it sharply. “We don’t want any lawyers mixed up. That guy couldn’t help us any.”

“I believe he could,” said Tuppence obstinately.

“Don’t you think it. So long.”

Thirty-five minutes later Julius returned, took her to the window. “There she is.”

“Oh!” said Tuppence reverently, gazing at the enormous car.

“She’s some pace-maker,” said Julius complacently.

“How did you get it?”

“She was being sent home to some bigwig. I went round to his house. I said a car like that was worth every penny of twenty thousand dollars. Then I told him it was worth just about fifty thousand to me if he’d get out.”

“Well?” said Tuppence, intoxicated.

“Well,” returned Julius, “he got out, that’s all.”

A FRIEND IN NEED

Friday and Saturday passed uneventfully. Tuppence had a brief answer from Mr. Carter: they had undertaken the work at their own risk; if anything had happened to Tommy he regretted it, but he could do nothing. Without Tommy all the savour went out of the adventure; though she prided herself on quick wits, she felt rudderless. Julius, though cleverer, did not steady her as Tommy’s sober common sense had done. He might be slow, but he was very sure. For the first time the mission looked sinister, its romance shorn off; Tommy--that was all that mattered. Many times a day she blinked resolutely back tears. “Little fool,” she apostrophized herself, “don’t snivel. Of course you’re fond of him. You’ve known him all your life. But there’s no need to be sentimental about it.”

Boris did not come; Julius and the car waited in vain. Tuppence, despite Julius’s objections, had not relinquished the idea of appealing to Sir James Peel Edgerton. He had looked kindly at her; perhaps he had meant to warn her; perhaps he might tell them something of Mrs. Vandemeyer and so lead to Tommy. Sunday being her afternoon out, she would meet Julius and beard the lion in his den.

Julius needed persuading, but at last gave in. An irreproachable butler showed them into Sir James’s library. Sir James rose from a roll-top desk strewn with papers.

“You have a message for me? Ah—” he recognized Tuppence with a smile—“it’s you, is it? Brought a message from Mrs. Vandemeyer, I suppose?”

“Not exactly,” said Tuppence; then, introducing Julius: “This is Mr. Hersheimmer, Sir James Peel Edgerton.”

“Won’t you both sit down?” said Sir James.

Tuppence plunged: “You’ll think it awful cheek, but I want to know what you meant the other day. Did you mean to warn me against Mrs. Vandemeyer?”

“Well, perhaps I did,” admitted Sir James gravely. “Without prejudice, then: if I had a young sister forced to earn her living, I should not like to see her in Mrs. Vandemeyer’s service.”

“I see,” said Tuppence. “But I’m not really inexperienced. I knew she was a bad lot when I went—indeed that’s why I went—” She broke off. “I think I’d better tell you the whole story. You’d know in a minute if I didn’t tell the truth.” Julius nodded her on.

“I want to know who Tommy is,” added Sir James.

She told him everything. He listened closely. “Very interesting. A great deal of what you tell me, child, I already know. You’ve done extraordinarily well so far; it’s rather too bad of—what do you know him as?—Mr. Carter to pitchfork you two young things into an affair of this kind. By the way, where did Mr. Hersheimmer come in originally?”

“I’m Jane’s first cousin,” said Julius.

“H’m.” Sir James paced slowly. “When you arrived, young lady, I was just packing up my traps. Going to Scotland by the night train for a few days’ fishing. But there are different kinds of fishing. I’ve a good mind to stay, and see if we can’t get on the track of that young chap.”

“Oh!” Tuppence clasped her hands.

“All the same,” he went on kindly, “don’t be offended because I think you’re young, Miss—er—”

“Cowley. Prudence Cowley. But my friends call me Tuppence.”

“Well then, Miss Tuppence. Frankly, things look bad for him—he’s been butting in somewhere where he wasn’t wanted. But don’t give up hope. And this petty little business, as Mr. Hersheimmer calls it, bears directly on a very big business—bigger perhaps than either of you know. If this boy is alive, he may have very valuable information. Therefore, we must find him.”

“Yes, but how?” cried Tuppence.

“There’s one person near at hand who probably knows where he is, or where he is likely to be,” said Sir James. “Mrs. Vandemeyer.”

“She’d never tell us.”

“That is where I come in. I think it quite likely that I shall be able to make Mrs. Vandemeyer tell me what I want to know.” He tapped with his finger on the table, and Tuppence felt again the intense power that radiated from the man. “If she will not, there is always bribery.”

“Sure—and that’s where I come in!” cried Julius. “You can count on me, if necessary, for one million dollars.”

Sir James subjected him to a long scrutiny. “Mr. Hersheimmer, that is a very large sum.”

“I can deliver the goods.”

“There is no time to be lost,” said Sir James at last. “The sooner we strike the better. Is Mrs. Vandemeyer dining out to-night?”

“Yes; but not late.”

“Good. I will call upon her at ten o’clock. Be back by nine-thirty; do not return earlier. Mr. Hersheimmer will wait below in a car. If I obtain the address from her, we go there at once, taking Mrs. Vandemeyer with us if necessary. You understand?”

“Yes,” cried Tuppence, lightened at heart.

They parted. Tuppence, restless, walked, but as she neared Hyde Park Corner an unreasonable uneasiness grew. She would do no harm just to look at South Audley Mansions. Its stolidity reassured her a shade—when a piercing whistle brought Albert flying.

“I say, miss, she’s a-going!”

“Who?”

“Ready Rita. Mrs. Vandemeyer. She’s a-packing up, and she’s just sent down for a taxi.”

Tuppence clutched his arm. “Albert, you’re a brick. Quick—there’s a telephone box round the corner? Ring up the Ritz, ask for Mr. Hersheimmer; if you can’t get him, ring Sir James Peel Edgerton. Tell them to come at once, as Mrs. Vandemeyer is trying to hook it. Don’t forget the names.”

“You trust to me, miss. But what about you? Aren’t you afraid to trust yourself with her?”

“No—go and telephone. Be quick.”

Drawing a long breath, Tuppence rang No. 20. After delay, Mrs. Vandemeyer herself opened. She lifted her eyebrows.

“You?”

“I had a touch of toothache, ma’am,” said Tuppence glibly. “So thought it better to come home and have a quiet evening.”

“Cook is out,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer disagreeably. “You had better go to bed.”

There was a new ring in her voice that Tuppence did not like; she was being edged up the passage. She turned at bay—when cold steel touched her temple.

“You damned little fool! Do you think I don’t know? If you struggle or cry out, I’ll shoot you like a dog. Now then, march—into my room. In a minute, when I’ve done with you, you’ll go to bed as I told you to. And you’ll sleep—oh yes, my little spy, you’ll sleep all right!”

Clothes lay flung about; a suit-case and a hat-box stood half-packed. The pistol never left Tuppence’s forehead.

“You can’t shoot me,” she said steadily.

“Every one in the building would hear the report.”

“I’d risk that,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer cheerfully. “But as long as you don’t sing out, you’re all right. Sit on the bed. Hands above your head. If you value your life, don’t move them.”

She laid the revolver on the washstand within reach, took a little stoppered bottle, poured, and filled it up with water.

“What’s that?” demanded Tuppence.

“Something to make you sleep soundly.”

“Are you going to poison me?”

“Perhaps,” said the other, smiling agreeably. “Don’t be a little fool. Poisoning you wouldn’t suit my book at all. It’s a sleeping draught. I don’t want the bother of tying and gagging you. Drink this down like a good girl, and you’ll be none the worse.”

In her heart Tuppence believed her; nevertheless she would not be quietly put to sleep without a bid for freedom. She lurched from the bed, clutching the other’s skirts, sobbing for mercy, edging nearer her mark.

“Drink it at once!” Imperiously the glass was pressed to her lips.

“You swear it won’t hurt me?” temporized Tuppence.

“Yes, yes,” said the other impatiently. “I swear it.”

Tuppence’s left hand rose tremulously—her mouth opened meekly—then like lightning she jerked the glass up; the fluid splashed into Mrs. Vandemeyer’s face; Tuppence’s right hand shot out and grasped the revolver. Springing back, she covered the other’s heart, the weapon steady. In the moment of victory, Tuppence betrayed a somewhat unsportsmanlike triumph. “Now who’s on top and who’s underneath?” she crowed.

Rage convulsed the beautiful face. With an effort Mrs. Vandemeyer controlled herself; a slow evil smile crept over her lips. “Not a fool, then, after all! You did that well, girl. But you shall pay for it—oh, yes, you shall pay for it! I have a long memory!”

“Supposing we sit down,” said Tuppence pleasantly. “Our present attitude is a little melodramatic. No—not on the bed. Draw a chair up to the table. I’ll sit opposite—with the revolver in front of me—just in case of accidents. Now, let’s talk.”

“What about?” sullenly.

Tuppence remembered Boris’s words: “I believe you would sell—us!” and her own light answer: “The price would have to be enormous.” Keeping her eyes steadily on the other: “Money—” she said quietly.

Mrs. Vandemeyer started. “What do you mean?”

“I mean that a long purse is more practical than a long memory. Revenge is very unsatisfactory. Money—well, there’s nothing unsatisfactory about money, is there?”

“Do you think I am the kind of woman to sell my friends?” scornfully.

“Yes,” said Tuppence promptly, “if the price was big enough.”

“A paltry hundred pounds or so!”

“No. I should suggest—” Tuppence’s voice was cool—“a hundred thousand!”

A faint flush rose. Fingers played nervously with a brooch; in that instant Tuppence knew the fish was hooked, and felt a sudden horror of her own money-loving spirit.

“You haven’t got it,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer at last.

“No—but I know some one who has.”

“Who?”

“A friend of mine.”

“Must be a millionaire,” unbelievingly.

“As a matter of fact he is. He’s an American. He’ll pay you that without a murmur. It’s a perfectly genuine proposition.”

“I’m inclined to believe you,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer slowly. After a pause: “What does he want to know?”

Julius’s interests must come first. “Where Jane Finn is,” said Tuppence boldly.

“I’m not sure where she is at the present moment,” said the other carelessly. “There would be no difficulty about finding out.”

“And—there’s a boy, a friend of mine. I’m afraid something’s happened to him, through your pal Boris. Tommy Beresford.”

“Never heard of him. But I’ll ask Boris. He’ll tell me anything he knows.”

“Thank you.” Tuppence’s spirits rose. One thing more—she leaned forward, lowered her voice:

“Who is Mr. Brown?”

The beautiful face paled. With an effort Mrs. Vandemeyer tried to resume her manner; the parody deceived no one. She shrugged. “You can’t have learnt much about us if you don’t know that nobody knows who Mr. Brown is....”

“You do,” said Tuppence quietly.

Again the colour ebbed. “What makes you think that?”

“I don’t know,” said the girl truthfully. “But I’m sure.”

Mrs. Vandemeyer stared before her a long time. “Yes,” she said hoarsely at last, “I know. I was beautiful, you see—very beautiful—”

“You are still,” said Tuppence with admiration.

“Not beautiful enough,” came the soft, dangerous voice. “Not—beautiful—enough! And sometimes, lately, I’ve been afraid.... It’s dangerous to know too much!” She leaned across. “Swear that my name shan’t be brought into it—that no one shall ever know.”

“I swear it. And, once he’s caught, you’ll be out of danger.”

“Shall I? Shall I ever be?” Terror swept her face. She clutched Tuppence’s arm. “You’re sure about the money?”

“Quite sure. He’ll be here presently. He may have to send cables—but there won’t be any delay—he’s a terrific hustler.”

“I’ll do it,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer resolutely. “It’s a great sum of money—and besides—” she smiled curiously—“it is not—wise to throw over a woman like me!”

She sat lightly tapping her fingers on the table; suddenly she started, blanching. “What was that?”

“I heard nothing.”

“If there should be some one listening—Even the walls might have ears. I tell you I’m frightened. You don’t know him!”

“Think of the hundred thousand pounds,” said Tuppence soothingly.

“You don’t know him,” she reiterated hoarsely. “He’s—ah!”

With a shriek she sprang up; her hand pointed over Tuppence’s head; she swayed and dropped in a dead faint.

Tuppence looked round. In the doorway stood Sir James Peel Edgerton and Julius Hersheimmer.

Sir James brushed past Julius and bent over the fallen woman.

“Heart,” he said sharply.

“Brandy—and quickly, or she’ll slip through our fingers.”

Julius made for the washstand.

“Not there,” said Tuppence. “In the tantalus in the dining-room. Second door down the passage.”

Between them Sir James and Tuppence carried Mrs. Vandemeyer to the bed. Water on the face did nothing. The lawyer kept a finger on her pulse.

“Touch and go,” he muttered.

Julius came back with a half-glass of brandy. While Tuppence raised the heavy head, Sir James forced a little between the closed lips. Presently the woman’s lids fluttered; colour crept back. She tried to sit up, then fell with a groan, a hand at her side.

“It’s my heart,” she whispered.

“I mustn’t talk.”

She lay still. Sir James watched the wrist a minute longer, then nodded.

“She’ll do now.”

All three moved away, and stood together talking in low voices. One and all were conscious of a certain feeling of anticlimax. Clearly any scheme for cross-questioning the lady was out of the question for the moment.

Tuppence told how Mrs. Vandemeyer had been on the point of naming Mr. Brown, and had promised to disclose Jane Finn’s hiding-place. Julius was jubilant.

“That’s fine, Miss Tuppence. I guess that hundred thousand pounds will look just as good in the morning to the lady as it did over night. She won’t speak without the cash, you bet!”

Sir James was more cautious. “I must confess I could wish we had not interrupted when we did. Still—only a matter of waiting till morning.”

He looked across at the inert figure. Through half-shut lids Mrs. Vandemeyer’s face wore for a second such fear and malice that Tuppence’s protest died. The look vanished instantly; the woman seemed inert as before. Nevertheless the girl determined to be on the alert.

“We must not leave the flat,” said Sir James quietly. “If only for Mrs. Vandemeyer’s sake.”

Julius stared. “You think he’d get after her? Between now and to-morrow morning. How could he know, even?”

“You forget your own dictaphone,” said Sir James dryly. “We have a formidable adversary. If we exercise all due care, there is a good chance of delivering him into our hands. But we must neglect no precaution. Miss Tuppence should go to bed; you and I, Mr. Hersheimmer, will share the vigil.”

Tuppence, turning to the bed, saw the lids lift again. The lips shaped a breath:

“Don’t—leave——”

“Sleepy,” the whisper broke, then tried again. Tuppence bent lower.

“Mr.—Brown——”

The eyes sent an agonized message. On impulse Tuppence said quickly:

“I shan’t leave the flat. I shall sit up all night.”

A flash of relief; the lids fell. Apparently Mrs. Vandemeyer slept. But her words had awakened a new uneasiness in Tuppence. What had she meant by that low murmur: “Mr. Brown?” Tuppence caught herself nervously looking over her shoulder. The big wardrobe loomed up in a sinister fashion before her eyes. Plenty of room for a man to hide in that.... Half-ashamed of herself, Tuppence pulled it open and looked inside. No one--of course! She stooped down and looked under the bed. There was no other possible hiding-place.

Tuppence gave her familiar shake of the shoulders. It was absurd, this giving way to nerves! Slowly she went out of the room. Julius and Sir James were talking in a low voice. Sir James turned to her.

“Lock the door outside, please, Miss Tuppence, and take the key. There must be no chance of anyone entering.”

“Say,” remarked Julius suddenly, “there’s Tuppence’s bright boy below. I’ll ease his mind.”

“How did you get in?” asked Tuppence.

“Albert phoned me; I ran round for Sir James; we came up by the coal lift.” Julius departed.

“Where do we take up our quarters?” asked Sir James.

“Mrs. Vandemeyer’s boudoir,” said Tuppence, leading the way.

“Very well. But do go to bed, child.”

“I couldn’t. I should dream of Mr. Brown all night!”

Julius returned presently, having reassured and rewarded Albert. Tuppence refusing bed, he foraged and came back with a cold pie and three plates. After they had eaten, her spirits rose.

“And now, Miss Tuppence, your adventures,” said Sir James.

She told her tale with complacence; Julius interjected an admiring “Bully.” Sir James’s quiet “Well done, Miss Tuppence,” brought the warm colour to her face.

“There’s one point I don’t get,” said Julius. “What put her up to clearing out?”

“I don’t know,” confessed Tuppence.

“The room was in disorder,” mused Sir James. “Looks as though her flight was unpremeditated—as though she got a sudden warning.”

“Mr. Brown, I suppose,” said Julius scoffingly.

“Why not?” returned Sir James. “Remember, you yourself have once been worsted by him.”

Julius flushed. “I feel mad when I think how I handed out Jane’s photograph to him like a lamb. If I ever lay hands on it again, I’ll freeze on to it like—like hell!”

“That contingency is likely to be remote,” said the other dryly.

“In any case, it’s the original I’m after. Where do you think she can be?”

“Impossible to say,” said Sir James. “But I have a very good idea where she has been.”

“Where?”

“At the scene of your nocturnal adventures—the Bournemouth nursing home.”

“Impossible. I asked.”

“You asked for ‘Jane Finn.’ If the girl had been placed there it would almost certainly be under an assumed name.”

“Bully for you! I never thought of that.”

“Perhaps the doctor’s in it too,” suggested Tuppence.

“I don’t think so,” said Julius. “I took to him. Dr. Hall’s all right.”

“Hall?” Sir James lifted his brows. “Curious—very curious. I met him this morning. He is staying at the Métropole. You did not mention his name this afternoon, or I would have given you a card.”

“I’m a mutt,” said Julius humbly. “But it doesn’t matter now. We’ve got Mrs. Vandemeyer on a string.”

“Yes,” said Tuppence, without conviction.

Silence fell. The night took hold—creaks, rustlings. Suddenly Tuppence sprang up.

“I can’t help it. I know Mr. Brown’s somewhere in the flat! I can feel him.”

“Why, Tuppence,” protested Julius, “this door’s open to the hall. No one could have come in the front way without our seeing.”

“With due deference to your feelings—and mine,” said Sir James gravely, “I do not see how it is humanly possible for anyone to be here without our knowledge.”

“Sitting up at night is always rather jumpy,” admitted Tuppence.

“We are like people at a séance,” observed Sir James. “With a medium, we might get marvellous results.”

“Do you believe in spiritualism?” asked Tuppence.

“There is some truth in it,” said the lawyer, shrugging. “But most of the testimony would not pass muster in the witness-box.”

With the first grey, Sir James drew the curtains. They beheld the slow rising of the sun over the sleeping city. Dreads seemed absurd; Tuppence’s spirits flew.

“Hooray! It’s going to be gorgeous. And we shall find Tommy. And Jane Finn. And everything will be lovely. I shall ask Mr. Carter if I can’t be made a Dame!”

At seven she volunteered to make tea, and came back with a tray and four cups.

“Who’s the other cup for?” asked Julius.

“The prisoner, of course.”

“Taking tea seems a kind of anticlimax,” Julius mused.

“Come along, both of you. We don’t know what mood she’ll wake in.”

Sir James and Julius went to the door with her.

Tuppence turned the key she had carried all night. “Supposing, after all, she’s escaped?” she whispered.

“Plumb impossible,” said Julius. Sir James said nothing.

They entered. Mrs. Vandemeyer lay on the bed. Relief—Tuppence went to pull up the blinds, then turned. The figure had not moved. A fear clutched her; she ran to the bed. The hand she lifted was ice-cold.

Her cry brought the others. A very few minutes sufficed. Mrs. Vandemeyer was dead—must have been dead for hours. She had died in her sleep.

“If that isn’t the cruellest luck,” cried Julius.

Sir James’s eyes had a curious gleam. “If it is luck,” he said.

“You don’t think—Why, that’s plumb impossible—no one could have got in.”

“I don’t see how,” admitted the lawyer. “And yet—she is on the point of betraying Mr. Brown, and—she dies. Is it only chance?”

“But how——”

“Yes—how! That we must find out.” He stood there silently, gently stroking his chin. “We must find out,” he said quietly, and Tuppence felt that if she was Mr. Brown she would not like the tone of those simple words.

Julius glanced at the window. “The window’s open. Do you think——”

“The balcony only runs to the boudoir,” said Tuppence. “We were there.”

“Mr. Brown’s methods are not so crude,” Sir James cut Julius short. “In the meantime we must send for a doctor. Before we do so, is there anything here that might be of value to us?”

The grate held a charred mass; papers had been burned on the eve of flight. They searched the other rooms in haste. Nothing.

“There’s that,” said Tuppence, pointing to a small old-fashioned wall safe. “It’s for jewellery—but there might be something else.”

The key was in the lock. Julius swung the door open and searched long. Tuppence waited.

“Well?” she said impatiently.

A pause. Julius withdrew his head and shut the door.

“Nothing,” he said.

A brisk young doctor came, deferential to Sir James.

“Heart failure—or possibly an overdose of some sleeping-draught.” He sniffed. “There’s a distinct odour of chloral.”

Tuppence remembered the little glass she had upset. She went to the washstand. The bottle from which Mrs. Vandemeyer had poured a few drops the night before had been three parts full. Now— _it was empty_.

A CONSULTATION

Nothing was more surprising and bewildering to Tuppence than the ease and simplicity with which everything was arranged, owing to Sir James’s skilful handling. The doctor accepted readily that Mrs. Vandemeyer had accidentally taken an overdose of chloral, doubted an inquest would be necessary, and promised to communicate with Sir James. Shortly afterwards a nurse arrived to take charge, and the others left the ill-omened building.

“And what now?” asked Julius, with a gesture of despair. “I guess we’re down and out for good.”

“No,” said Sir James quietly. “There is still Dr. Hall. He is staying at the Métropole. Let us call on him after a bath and breakfast.”

It was arranged that Tuppence and Julius should return to the Ritz and call for Sir James in the car. A little after eleven they drew up before the hotel, and a page went in search of Dr. Hall. In a few minutes the little doctor came hurrying towards them.

“Can you spare us a few minutes, Dr. Hall?” said Sir James. “Miss Cowley. Mr. Hersheimmer, I think you already know.”

“Aha—my young friend of the tree episode! Ankle all right, eh?”

“I guess it’s cured owing to your skilful treatment, doc.”

“And the heart trouble? Ha ha!”

“Still searching,” said Julius.

“To the point—can we have a word in private?” asked Sir James.

“Certainly.” He led them to a quiet room, and looked inquiringly at Sir James.

“I am anxious to find a certain young lady for the purpose of obtaining a statement. I believe she has been, at one time or another, in your establishment at Bournemouth. I hope I transgress no professional etiquette in questioning you?”

“I suppose it is a matter of testimony?”

Sir James hesitated, then nodded.

“I shall be pleased to give you any information in my power. What is the young lady’s name? Mr. Hersheimmer asked me, I remember——”

“The name is immaterial,” said Sir James bluntly. “She would almost certainly have been sent to you under an assumed one. Are you acquainted with a Mrs. Vandemeyer?”

“Mrs. Vandemeyer, of 20 South Audley Mansions? Slightly.”

“You are not aware she is dead?”

“Dear, dear! When did it happen?”

“She took an overdose of chloral last night.”

“Purposely?”

“Accidentally, it is believed. I found her dead this morning.”

“Very sad. A singularly handsome woman.” He paused. “But I do not see—”

“It bears thus: did not Mrs. Vandemeyer commit a young relative to your charge?”

Julius leaned forward.

“That is the case,” said the doctor quietly.

“Under the name of——?”

“Janet Vandemeyer. I understood her to be a niece.”

“And she came to you——?”

“In June or July of 1915, as far as I remember.”

“Was she a mental case?”

“She is perfectly sane, if that is what you mean. I understood from Mrs. Vandemeyer that the girl had been with her on the Lusitania, and had suffered a severe shock.”

“We’re on the right track,” said Sir James.

“As I said before, I’m a mutt,” muttered Julius.

“You spoke of wanting a statement,” said Dr. Hall. “Supposing she is not able to give one?”

“What? You have just said she is perfectly sane,” said Sir James.

“So she is. Nevertheless, if you want a statement concerning any events prior to May 7, 1915, she will not be able to give it to you.”

They stared. The little man nodded cheerfully.

“An interesting case—very interesting. Not so uncommon as you would think. It’s the first of the kind I’ve had under my own observation, and I must admit that I’ve found it of absorbing interest.” There was something rather ghoulish in the little man’s satisfaction. “Janet Vandemeyer is suffering from a complete loss of memory.”

“Nothing prior to May 7,” he went on.

“After that her memory is as good as yours or mine. The first thing she remembers is landing with the survivors. She did not know her own name, or where she had come from; she could not even speak her own tongue.”

“But surely that is unusual?” said Julius.

“No, my dear sir, quite normal under the circumstances. Severe shock to the nervous system. I suggested a specialist—a very good man in Paris—but Mrs. Vandemeyer opposed the publicity that might result.”

“I can imagine she would,” said Sir James grimly.

“I fell in with her views. The girl was very young—nineteen, I believe. There is really no special treatment; it is a matter of waiting.”

“Waiting?” said Sir James.

“Yes—sooner or later the memory returns—suddenly, as it went. Often the intervening period is forgotten, and life is taken up where it left off—at the sinking of the Lusitania.”

“And when?” asked Sir James.

The doctor shrugged. “Sometimes months, sometimes years—twenty years have been known. Sometimes another shock does the trick. One restores what the other took away.”

“Another shock, eh?” said Julius thoughtfully.

The little man’s voice trailed on. Julius, frowning, struck the table with his fist. They all jumped.

“I’ve got it! Doc, suppose we re-created the whole catastrophe—the submarine, the sinking ship, the rush to the boats. Wouldn’t that big bump to her subconscious self set it going again?”

“A very interesting speculation. In my opinion, it would be successful. It is unfortunate there is no chance of the conditions repeating themselves.”

“Not by nature,” said Julius. “I’m talking about art.” In a few hard phrases he sketched hiring ship and passengers, and a panic well drilled for the screen.

Dr. Hall stared, then found his voice.

“But the expense—my dear sir—the expense! It will be colossal!”

“Money doesn’t worry me,” said Julius simply.

Sir James smiled slightly. “Mr. Hersheimmer is very well off—very well off indeed.”

The doctor’s glance came back to Julius with a new and subtle quality in it. This was no longer an eccentric young fellow with a habit of falling off trees. The doctor’s eyes held the deference accorded to a really rich man.

“Very remarkable plan. Very remarkable,” he murmured.

“And you really mean to carry out this remarkable plan of yours.”

“You bet your bottom dollar I do.”

The doctor believed him—which was a tribute to his nationality. If an Englishman had suggested such a thing, he would have had grave doubts as to his sanity.

“I cannot guarantee a cure,” he pointed out.

“Sure. You just trot out Jane and leave the rest to me.”

“Jane?”

“Miss Janet Vandemeyer. Can we get on long distance to your place and ask them to send her up—or shall I fetch her in my car?”

The doctor stared. “I beg your pardon, Mr. Hersheimmer. I thought you understood.”

“Understood what?”

“That Miss Vandemeyer is no longer under my care.”

Julius sprang up. “What?”

“I thought you were aware of that.”

“When did she leave?”

“To-day is Monday? Then last Wednesday. Yes—after your—er—fall from my tree. A very urgent message came from Mrs. Vandemeyer. The young lady and the nurse in charge of her left by the night train.”

“Nurse Edith—left with a patient,” muttered Julius. “My God, to have been so near!”

Dr. Hall looked bewildered. “I don’t understand. Is the young lady not with her aunt, after all?”

Tuppence was about to speak; a glance from Sir James checked her. The lawyer rose.

“I’m much obliged to you, Hall. We’re grateful for all you’ve told us. We must track Miss Vandemeyer anew. The nurse—do you know where she is?”

The doctor shook his head. “We’ve not heard. I understood she was to remain with Miss Vandemeyer for a while. But what can have happened? Surely the girl has not been kidnapped.”

“That remains to be seen,” said Sir James gravely.

“You do not think I ought to go to the police?”

“No, no. In all probability the young lady is with other relations.”

Not wholly satisfied, the doctor wished them good-bye, and they left the hotel.

“How maddening,” cried Tuppence, “to think Julius was actually under the same roof with her for a few hours.”

“I was a darned idiot,” muttered Julius.

“You couldn’t know,” said Tuppence. “Could he?” she appealed.

“I should advise you not to worry,” said Sir James kindly. “No use crying over spilt milk.”

“The great thing is what to do next,” said Tuppence.

“You might advertise for the nurse,” said Sir James. “I do not hope for much. Otherwise there is nothing to be done.”

“Nothing?” said Tuppence blankly. “And—Tommy?”

“We must hope for the best. Oh yes, we must go on hoping.”

Over her bent head his eyes met Julius’s; he shook his head, almost imperceptibly. Julius understood: the lawyer considered the case hopeless. Sir James took Tuppence’s hand.

“You must let me know if anything further comes to light. Letters will always be forwarded.”

“You are going away?” said Tuppence dully.

“To Scotland. My dear young lady, I can do nothing more. Our clues have ended in thin air. If anything should arise, I shall be glad to advise you.”

Desolation swept her. “Thank you for trying. Good-bye.”

Sir James’s keen eyes softened. “Don’t be too disconsolate, Miss Tuppence,” he said low.

“Remember, holiday-time isn’t always all playtime. One sometimes manages to put in some work as well.”

She looked up sharply. He shook his head with a smile. “No, I shan’t say any more. Great mistake to say too much. Remember that. Never tell all you know—not even to the person you know best. Understand? Good-bye.”

He strode away. Tuppence stared after him. Once before he had thrown a hint in this careless fashion. Was this a hint now? Had he not abandoned the case after all? Would he be working secretly while——

“Get right in,” said Julius. They started. “You’re looking thoughtful. Did the old guy say anything more?”

Tuppence opened her mouth, then shut it again. “Never tell all you know.” Another memory flashed: Julius at the safe; her own question; his pause before “Nothing.” Had he, too, held back? If he could make a reservation, so could she.

“Nothing particular,” she said.

“Shall we go for a spin in the Park?”

“If you like.”

As they drove, Julius spoke, discouraged: “Say, Miss Tuppence, do you think I’m ever going to find Jane?”

“Sir James hadn’t got any hope at all—that’s my take. He wouldn’t quit if there was a chance, would he?”

Tuppence was uncomfortable, but clung to her small reticence. “He suggested advertising for the nurse,” she said.

“Yes—with a forlorn-hope flavour. I’m about fed up. I’ve half a mind to go back to the States right away.”

“Oh no!” cried Tuppence. “We’ve got to find Tommy.”

“I sure forgot Beresford,” said Julius contritely. “That’s so. We must find him. But after—well, I’ve been day-dreaming since I started on this trip, and these dreams are poor business. Say, Miss Tuppence, there’s something I’d like to ask you.”

“Yes?”

“You and Beresford—what about it?”

“Tommy and I are friends—nothing more,” said Tuppence with warmth.

“I guess every pair of lovers has said that sometime or another,” observed Julius.

“Nonsense!” snapped Tuppence. “Do I look the sort of girl that’s always falling in love with every man she meets?”

“You do not. You look the sort of girl that’s mighty often getting fallen in love with!”

“What about marriage? Got any views?” he asked bluntly.

“I intend to marry—if I can find some one rich enough to make it worth my while. That’s frank, isn’t it?” She threw it like a challenge.

“I never despise business instinct,” said Julius. “What about me?”

“You?”

“Sure.”

“I couldn’t.”

“Why not?”

“It would seem so unfair.”

“I don’t see anything unfair. I admire you immensely. You’re so darned plucky. I’d love to give you a real good time. Say the word and we’ll run round to a jeweller and fix the ring business.”

“I can’t,” gasped Tuppence.

“Because of Beresford?”

“No—no—no!”

“Well then?”

She shook her head. “Thank you very much—and all that—but I think I’d better say no.”

“I’d be obliged if you’d think it over till to-morrow.”

“It’s no use.”

“Still, we’ll leave it like that.”

“Very well,” said Tuppence meekly.

They drove in silence to the Ritz. In her room, Tuppence sat before the glass and stared herself down.

“Fool,” she murmured.

“Little fool. Everything you want—and you bleat out ‘no’ like a sheep. It’s your one chance. Why don’t you snatch it? What more do you want?”

Her eyes fell on the small snapshot of Tommy in its shabby frame. Control went; she caught it to her lips and sobbed.

“Oh, Tommy, Tommy, I do love you so—and I may never see you again....”

After five minutes she sat up, blew her nose, pushed back her hair.

“That’s that,” she said sternly.

“Facts. I’ve fallen in love—with an idiot of a boy who probably doesn’t care two straws.” She paused. “Anyway, I don’t know that he does. He’d never have dared to say so. I’ve always jumped on sentiment—and here I am more sentimental than anybody. What idiots girls are! I’ve always thought so. I suppose I shall sleep with his photograph under my pillow, and dream about him all night. It’s dreadful to feel you’ve been false to your principles.”

Tuppence shook her head sadly, as she reviewed her backsliding.

“I don’t know what to say to Julius,” she went on.

“He’ll insist on a reason. I wonder if he did find anything in that safe——”

Her thoughts veered; she reviewed the night, Sir James’s enigmatic words. Suddenly she started; the colour ebbed; her eyes stared, pupils dilating.

“Impossible,” she whispered.

“Impossible! I must be going mad to think such a thing....”

Monstrous—yet it explained everything.

After a moment she sat down and wrote a note, weighing each word, then nodded, satisfied, and slipped it into an envelope addressed to Julius. She took it to his sitting-room and left it on the table.

A small page-boy stood outside her door on her return.

“Telegram for you, miss.”

Tuppence tore it open carelessly—then gave a cry. The telegram was from Tommy!

FURTHER ADVENTURES OF TOMMY

Out of a darkness stabbed with throbs of fire, Tommy dragged himself back to life. His head blazed, the room was unfamiliar, and the memory of the sinister house in Soho slid into place. He lay artistically inert as a voice at his ear—the bearded, efficient German’s—said, “He is coming to.” Then, with a muttered “Damn!” he sat up. The German held a glass to his lips. “Drink.” The fierce draught cleared his brain.

He was on a couch in the meeting-room. On one side stood the German, on the other the villainous doorkeeper who had let him in. One face was missing: Number One was gone.

“Feel better?” asked the German.

“Yes, thanks,” said Tommy cheerfully.

“Ah, my young friend, it is lucky for you your skull is so thick. The good Conrad struck hard.” The doorkeeper grinned. Tommy twisted his head.

“So you’re Conrad, are you? It strikes me my thick skull was lucky for you too. When I look at you, it’s almost a pity I’ve enabled you to cheat the hangman.”

Conrad snarled. The German’s cold accents took up the tale.

“Have you anything to say before you are put to death as a spy?”

“Simply lots of things,” returned Tommy, unruffled.

Tommy Beresford was one of those young Englishmen not distinguished by any special intellectual ability, but who are emphatically at their best in what is known as a “tight place.” Their natural diffidence and caution fall from them like a glove.

“Do you deny that you were listening at that door?”

“I do not. Your conversation was so interesting that it overcame my scruples.”

“How did you get in?”

“Dear old Conrad here. Don’t blame a faithful watchdog; he let me in—with your own password.”

A movement ran through them; the German stilled it with a hand. “Dead men tell no tales,” he said evenly.

“Ah,” said Tommy, “but I’m not dead yet!”

“You soon will be, my young friend.”

Tommy kept his tone pleasant. “I think not. I should have a great objection to dying.” He saw their uncertainty and pressed it.

“Why didn’t you kill me at once before I regained consciousness? Because you didn’t know how much I knew—and where I obtained that knowledge. If you kill me now, you never will know.”

Boris burst out, arms waving: “You hell-hound of a spy! Kill him! Kill him!” The working man cut across him gloomily: “Comrades, we have been betrayed!” An ugly murmur arose.

“You will tell us who has betrayed us,” said the German. “That shall not save you. Boris knows pretty ways of making people speak!”

“Bah!” said Tommy scornfully, mastering the unpleasant lurch of his stomach. “You will neither torture me nor kill me.”

“And why not?” growled Boris.

“Because you’d kill the goose that lays the golden eggs.”

They wavered. The working man eyed Tommy closely. “He’s bluffing you, Boris,” he said; Tommy hated him for the words. The German swung back.

“What do you mean?”

“What do you think I mean?” parried Tommy. Then, quietly: “I propose a bargain. My life and liberty against——”

“Against what?”

The room held its breath.

“The papers that Danvers brought over from America in the _Lusitania_.”

The effect was electrical. Men sprang to their feet. The German leaned over Tommy, face purpling. “_Himmel!_ You have got them, then?”

Tommy shook his head. “No.”

“You know where they are?”

“Not in the least.”

“Then—” baffled anger choked him.

“I don’t know where they are—but I believe I can find them. I have a theory—and facts known to no one but myself. What do you lose? If I produce the papers, you give me my life and liberty. Is it a bargain?”

“And if we refuse?” said the German quietly.

Tommy lay back. “The 29th,” he said thoughtfully, “is less than a fortnight ahead——”

“Take him into the other room,” said the German.

Five minutes in the dingy chamber next door, enlivened by flippant remarks that drove Conrad to the verge of homicidal mania—then: “The prisoner is at the bar, gentlemen,” said Tommy as he was marched back.

The German was at his table again. “We accept,” he said harshly, “on terms. The papers must be delivered to us before you go free.”

“Idiot! How can I hunt for them tied by the leg here?”

“You will remain. One of our number will carry out your instructions. If the operations are complicated, he returns for further orders.”

“You’re tying my hands,” grumbled Tommy. “But—be curt if you must. So be it. One thing is essential: I must see the girl.”

“What girl?”

“Jane Finn, of course.”

The other looked at him curiously. “Do you not know that she can tell you nothing?”

“I shall not ask her to tell me anything. I want to watch her face when I ask her one question.”

“She will not be able to answer.”

“That does not matter. I shall have seen her face.”

The German laughed shortly, still studying him. “I wonder whether, after all, you know as much as we think?” he said softly.

“There may be things you know that I do not,” said Tommy. “Equally, I’ve something up my sleeve that you don’t know about. Danvers was a damned clever fellow——” He broke off. The German’s face lightened.

“Danvers. I see——” He waved to Conrad. “Take him away. Upstairs—you know.”

“Wait. What about the girl?”

“That may perhaps be arranged.”

“It must be.”

“We will see. Only one person can decide that.”

“Who?”

“Mr. Brown——”

“Shall I see him?”

“Perhaps.”

“Come,” said Conrad harshly.

Upstairs, Conrad thrust him into a small room, lit a hissing gas jet, and locked the door. There was no window. The air was peculiarly stale. Four crooked pictures from Faust—Marguerite with her jewels, the church scene, Siebel and his flowers, Faust and Mephistopheles—hung on the foul walls. In the sealed, close-fitting chamber, the sinister power of the arch-criminal seemed more real. Shout as he would, no one could hear. The place was a living tomb....

Tommy sat on the bed, head aching, stomach empty, silence pressing upon him. “Anyway,” he told himself, “I shall see the chief—the mysterious Mr. Brown—and, with a bit of luck in bluffing, the mysterious Jane Finn also. After that——”

After that, he was forced to admit, the prospect looked dreary.

ANNETTE

Hunger soon made itself master of all other troubles. Tommy, prowling his prison, pounded on the door once or twice and got no answer.

“They can’t mean to starve me to death,” he muttered indignantly; but he dismissed the notion that this was one of Boris’s “pretty ways.”

“It’s that sour-faced brute Conrad. I shall enjoy getting even with him one of these days.”

Further meditations induced in him the feeling that it would be extremely pleasant to bring something down with a whack on Conrad’s egg-shaped head. Tommy stroked his own head tenderly, and gave himself up to the pleasures of imagination. Finally a bright idea flashed across his brain. Why not convert imagination into reality? Conrad was undoubtedly the tenant of the house; therefore why not wait in ambush behind the door, and when he entered bring down a chair—or one of the decrepit pictures—smartly on to his head? One would, of course, be careful not to hit too hard. Such an affair was infinitely more in his line than the verbal encounter of this afternoon. Intoxicated by his plan, Tommy gently unhooked the picture of the Devil and Faust, and settled himself in position. His hopes were high. The plan seemed to him simple but excellent. He waited. No one came. At ten he flung himself on the bed and slept.

The sound of the key turning in the lock awoke him. Not belonging to the type of hero who is famous for awaking in full possession of his faculties, Tommy merely blinked at the ceiling and wondered vaguely where he was; then he remembered. It was eight o’clock. “Early tea or breakfast? Pray God it’s the latter!” The door opened—not Conrad, but a girl with a tray. In the feeble gaslight he decided she was one of the most beautiful girls he had ever seen—wild-rose face, brown hair with sunbeams caught in it, eyes of golden hazel.

“Are you Jane Finn?” he asked breathlessly.

The girl shook her head. “My name is Annette, monsieur.” Her English was soft and broken.

“Française?” hazarded Tommy.

“Oui, monsieur. Monsieur parle français?”

“Not for any length of time. What’s that—breakfast?” He inspected loaf, margarine, coffee. “The living is not equal to the Ritz, but for what we are at last about to receive the Lord has made me truly thankful. Amen.”

She turned to go.

“Wait a sec. What are you doing in this house? Don’t tell me you’re Conrad’s niece or daughter.”

“I do the service, monsieur. I am not related to anybody.”

“You’ve heard the name I asked—Jane Finn?”

“I have heard people speak of Jane Finn, I think.”

“You don’t know where she is? Not in this house?”

“Oh no, monsieur. I must go now—they will be waiting for me.” The key turned on him again.

“At one o’clock” she reappeared—this time with Conrad. “Good morning,” said Tommy amiably. “You have not used Pear’s soap, I see.”

Conrad growled. “Talk away. It’s little enough time you’ll have to talk in, maybe.”

“Retire, varlet,” Tommy waved him off. But the ominous hint lingered.

That evening, when the key turned once more, the girl was alone. “Shut the door,” he whispered.

“Annette, help me get out of this.”

“Impossible. There are three of them on the floor below.”

He stored that. “But you would help me if you could?”

“No, monsieur.” Then, after a moment: “I think—they are my own people. You have spied upon them. They are quite right to keep you here.”

“They’re a bad lot, Annette. Help me, and I’ll take you away—from the lot of them. A good whack of money too.”

“I dare not, monsieur; I am afraid of them.”

“Wouldn’t you help another girl? She’s about your age. Save her from their clutches—Jane Finn.”

“It is her you came here to look for? Yes?” She passed a hand across her forehead. “Jane Finn. Always I hear that name. It is familiar.”

“You must know something—”

“I know nothing—only the name.” She turned—caught sight of the picture he had set against the wall. A flash of terror—then, as swiftly, relief. She went out abruptly. He re-hung the picture thoughtfully.

Three dreary days dragged by. Conrad and Annette were his only visitors; the girl had grown dumb, her eyes watching him darkly. From Conrad he learned they waited for orders from “Mr. Brown.”

On the third evening came the tramp of feet. Conrad entered with the evil-looking Number 14. “Got those ropes, mate?” The man’s fingers were horribly deft. In another minute Tommy was a helpless bundle.

“Thought you’d bluffed us, did you?” said Conrad at last. “With what you knew, and what you didn’t know. Bargained with us! And all the time it was bluff! Bluff! You know less than a kitten. But your number’s up now all right, you b—— swine.”

“A very good speech,” said Tommy. “But why the bonds? Why not cut my throat without delay?”

“Garn,” said Number 14. “Think we’re as green as to do you in here and ’ave the police nosing round? Not ’alf! We’ve ordered the carriage for your lordship to-morrow mornin’, but in the meantime we’re not taking any chances, see!”

After they had gone, Tommy lay stiff and helpless, wondering how Mr. Brown had twigged him. Had Tuppence gone to the police? Or had some careless mention—“the Ritz,” say—given him away?

The key turned softly. Annette, with a tray. Conrad’s voice sounded outside: “He doesn’t want any supper to-night.”

“Oui, oui, je sais bien. But I must take the other tray. We need the things on it.” She came in, turned out the gas. “Curse you—why did you do that?” snarled Conrad at the door.

“I always turn it out. You should have told me. Shall I relight it, Monsieur Conrad?”

“No, come on out of it.”

“Le beau petit monsieur,” she cried near the bed in the darkness, “you have tied him up well, hein? He is like a trussed chicken!” Her tone jarred him—till he felt her hand run lightly over his bonds, and something small and cold was slipped into his palm.

“Come on, Annette.”

“Mais me voilà.” The door shut. “Lock it and give me the key,” said Conrad. Their steps died away.

A little penknife lay open in his hand. So—that was why she turned out the light. The room must be overlooked; there was a peep-hole. He had betrayed little—only his wish to escape and to find Jane—but Annette’s denials might have been for listeners. No time to puzzle it out. He rubbed the open blade up and down the cord around his wrists. It cut his skin; he grunted; doggedly he sawed on. The cord slackened. Five minutes later he stood upright, cramp-racked, bound his bleeding wrist, and in the dark unhooked the famous picture. He felt an economical pleasure that his first plan would not be wasted. He waited.

Footsteps. He drew a deep breath and gripped the frame. The door opened; a faint light from the passage. Conrad went straight to the gas. Number 14 followed. Tommy brought the picture down with terrific force. Number 14 dropped in a stupendous crash of broken glass. Tommy slipped out, pulled to the door, turned the key, drew it out as Conrad hurled himself against the panels with a volley of curses.

The German’s voice floated up: “Gott im Himmel! Conrad, what is it?”

A small hand found his. Annette stood beside him, pointing up a rickety ladder. “Quick—up here!” They reached a dusty garret.

“This won’t do. It’s a trap.”

“Hush! Wait.” She listened at the ladder top. The battering below was terrific. “They will think you are still inside. They cannot hear what Conrad says—the door is too thick.”

“But—”

“Leave it to me.” Swiftly she fastened a string to the handle of a big cracked jug and laid the line carefully. “Have you the key of the door?”

“Yes.”

“Give it to me. I am going down. Can you swing yourself down behind the ladder, so they will not see you? There’s a big cupboard in the shadow—stand behind it. Take the end of this string. When I’ve let the others out—pull!”

She flitted down. “Mon Dieu! Qu’est-ce qu’il y a?” she cried.

“Get out of this. Go to your room!” snarled the German.

Cautiously Tommy slid behind the ladder, crouched by the cupboard. “Mon Dieu, voilà la clef!” Annette stooped, cried out, produced it. The German snatched it, unlocked the door. Conrad stumbled out, swearing.

“Where is he? Have you got him?”

“We have seen no one,” said the German sharply, paling.

“He’s got away.”

“Impossible. He would have passed us.”

Tommy pulled the string. Crash! Crockery above. In a trice the men hustled up the rickety ladder into the dark. Tommy leapt from hiding, dragged the girl, and flew down the stairs. He fumbled bolts and chain; the door yielded—he turned—Annette had disappeared.

He stood spell-bound. Then, from above, the German’s exclamation—and Annette’s clear voice: “Ma foi, he has escaped! And quickly! Who would have thought it? This is a terrible house. I want to go back to Marguerite. To Marguerite. To Marguerite!”

Was that a command to go? It sounded like it. But Conrad came leaping down, savage at sight of him; the others behind. Tommy stopped Conrad’s rush with a straight blow on the point of the jaw; the man fell like a log; the second tripped over him; a flash from the stairs and a bullet grazed Tommy’s ear. Prudence, suddenly, was health.

He leapt out, slamming the door. The square was deserted save for a baker’s van. Evidently he was to have been carried off in that, his body later found miles away. The driver jumped down to bar him; Tommy’s fist shot out; the man sprawled. A hail of bullets from the doorway; none hit. He turned the corner. “They can’t go on shooting—they’ll have the police on them.”

Footsteps gained; he redoubled his pace. He stumbled over a prostrate figure which started up with a yell and tore off; Tommy drew into a doorway as his two pursuers, the German among them, raced after the red herring. He sat down to recover breath, then strolled away, and, restoring himself at a Turkish bath, went in search of breakfast at an A.B.C., eggs and bacon and coffee before him, a paper propped up.

He stiffened. An article on Kramenin, “the man behind Bolshevism,” newly arrived in London, with his portrait. “So that’s who Number 1 is,” said Tommy, mouth full. “Not a doubt about it.”

He went to Whitehall, sent up his name, and was presently with the man who did not here go by the name of “Mr. Carter,” whose first frown vanished as he listened. Between Tommy’s sentences he spoke into the telephone; at the end he nodded.

“Quite right. Every moment’s of value. They wouldn’t wait—still, there may be a clue. You say Number 1 is Kramenin? Important. We want something against him badly. Two faces were familiar? One a Labour man? Look through these.” Tommy picked a photograph. “Ah—Westway! Shouldn’t have thought it—poses as moderate. The other?” He handed another; Tommy exclaimed.

“Right. Irishman. Prominent Unionist M.P.—all a blind. Yes—you’ve done very well. The 29th is the date. Very little time indeed.”

He read Tommy’s hesitation. “We can deal with the General Strike menace—have a sporting chance. But if that draft treaty turns up—we’re done. Ah—the car? Come on, Beresford; we’ll have a look at this house of yours.”

Two constables were on duty in Soho. “The birds have flown,” said Mr. Carter. They went over the deserted rooms: Tommy’s prison with its crooked pictures, the broken jug in the attic, the meeting-room table. No papers, nothing left. And no Annette.

“What you tell me about the girl puzzles me,” said Mr. Carter. “You believe she deliberately went back?”

“It would seem so. She ran upstairs while I was getting the door open.”

“H’m. Then she must belong to the gang, though not liking to stand by and see a personable young man killed. Evidently she’s in with them, or she wouldn’t have gone back.”

“I can’t believe she’s really one of them, sir. She—seemed so different—” He flushed when Mr. Carter smiled: “Good-looking, I suppose?” He admitted her beauty shamefacedly.

“By the way,” said Mr. Carter, “have you shown yourself to Miss Tuppence? She’s been bombarding me with letters about you.”

“I was afraid she might get rattled. Did she go to the police?”

Mr. Carter shook his head.

“Then how did they twig me?” Tommy explained his surmise. “True, that’s curious. Unless the mention of the Ritz was an accidental remark?”

“It might have been. But they must have found out suddenly.”

“Well, there’s nothing more to be done here. What about some lunch with me?”

“Thanks awfully, sir, but I think I’d better get back and rout out Tuppence.”

“Of course. Give her my kind regards and tell her not to believe you’re killed too readily next time.”

“I take a lot of killing, sir.”

“So I perceive. Good-bye. Remember you’re a marked man now, and take reasonable care of yourself.”

Tommy hailed a taxi, the thought of startling Tuppence warming him. “Dogging ‘Rita’ most likely. By the way—‘Marguerite’ must be Mrs. Vandemeyer’s name. Annette’s cry—‘To Marguerite!’—looks like it.” The thought saddened him. The taxi drew up at the Ritz. He burst in eagerly, and was checked.

Miss Cowley had gone out a quarter of an hour ago.

THE TELEGRAM

Baffled for the moment, Tommy strolled into the restaurant and ordered a meal of surpassing excellence. He was in the act of conveying a particularly choice morsel of Sole à la Jeanette to his mouth when he caught sight of Julius. Tommy waved cheerfully. Julius’s eyes seemed as though they would pop out of his head. He pump‑handled Tommy’s hand with unnecessary vigour.

“Holy snakes! Is it really you?”

“Of course. Why shouldn’t it be?”

“Why shouldn’t it be? Say, man, don’t you know you’ve been given up for dead? We’d have had a solemn requiem for you in another few days.”

“Who thought I was dead?”

“Tuppence.”

“She remembered the proverb about the good dying young, I suppose. There must be a certain amount of original sin in me to have survived. Where is Tuppence, by the way?”

“Isn’t she here? I dropped her in the car about an hour ago.”

They sat; Julius ordered; Tommy told his tale; Julius, spellbound, forgot half his dishes. Then in turn he narrated his own doings—Bournemouth a washout; back to London; the car; Tuppence growing anxious; a call on Sir James; and the sensational occurrences of the previous night at Mrs. Vandemeyer’s.

“The doctor kidded himself she took it herself,” he ended dryly.

“And Sir James?”

“Being a legal luminary, he is likewise a human oyster. I should say he reserved judgment.” He added the morning’s news.

“Lost her memory, eh?” said Tommy, interested. “By Jove, that explains why they looked queer when I spoke of questioning her. Bit of a slip on my part, that! But it wasn’t the sort of thing a fellow would be likely to guess.”

They agreed no hint had been given of Jane’s whereabouts.

“I guess you’re lucky to be here at all,” said Julius. “That bluff of yours was the goods all right. How you ever came to think of it all so pat beats me to a frazzle!”

“I was in such a funk I had to think of something,” said Tommy simply.

On Mrs. Vandemeyer’s death, Tommy said thoughtfully: “Mr. Brown?”

“Sure thing.”

“All the same, Mr. Brown hasn’t got wings. I don’t see how he got in and out.”

They bandied a moment’s nonsense about “magnetic influence,” then Tommy sprang up. “Let’s go round to the scene of the crime. I wish we could get hold of Tuppence. The Ritz would enjoy the spectacle of the glad reunion.”

Inquiry at the office revealed she had not returned. Julius went upstairs to look in his rooms. A diminutive page hovered by Tommy and whispered shyly that the young lady had taken a taxi “to Charing Cross,” after asking for an A.B.C. and a Bradshaw—“when I took her the telegram, sir.”

“A telegram? When?”

“About half‑past twelve, sir.”

Julius rejoined them with an open letter in his hand. “Tuppence has gone off sleuthing on her own,” said Tommy. “She went off to Charing Cross in the deuce of a hurry after getting a telegram. Oh; she left a note for you. Where’s she off to?”

Julius folded the letter and pocketed it, a trifle embarrassed. “I guess this is nothing to do with it. It’s about something else—something I asked her to let me know about.” He looked Tommy in the eye. “I’d better put you wise. I asked Miss Tuppence to marry me this morning.”

Tommy felt dazed. “That’s all right,” he said quickly, lighting a cigarette with a hand that shook.

“Tuppence and I have been pals for years. Nothing more.”

“Oh, I guess it’ll be the dollars that’ll do the trick. Miss Tuppence put me wise to that right away. We ought to gee along together very well.”

Tommy looked at him curiously for a minute, as though he were about to speak, then changed his mind and said nothing. Nevertheless, Tommy did blame her. He was filled with a passionate and utterly illogical resentment. It was all very well to say things like that—but a real girl would never marry for money. Tuppence was utterly cold‑blooded and selfish, and he would be delighted if he never saw her again! And it was a rotten world!

“What did she say in that note?” he demanded fiercely.

“There’s no earthly clue in it,” said Julius, handing it over.

In Tuppence’s well‑known schoolboy scrawl:

“DEAR JULIUS,

“It’s always better to have things in black and white. I don’t feel I can be bothered to think of marriage until Tommy is found. Let’s leave it till then.

“Yours affectionately,

“TUPPENCE.”

Tommy handed it back, his eyes shining. His feelings had undergone a sharp reaction. He now felt that Tuppence was all that was noble and disinterested. Had she not refused Julius without hesitation? True, the note betokened signs of weakening, but he could excuse that. It read almost like a bribe to Julius to spur him on in his efforts to find Tommy, but he supposed she had not really meant it that way. Darling Tuppence, there was not a girl in the world to touch her!

He wheeled on the page. “One thing more. What did the young lady do with the telegram?”

“She crumpled it into a ball, sir, and threw it into the grate.”

“Come on, Julius. We must find that telegram.”

They hurried to Tuppence’s room. In the fireplace lay a crumpled orange‑and‑white ball. Tommy smoothed it out:

“Come at once, Moat House, Ebury, Yorkshire, great developments--TOMMY.”

“You didn’t send it?”

“Of course not.”

“I guess it means the worst,” said Julius quietly. “They’ve got her. They signed your name, and she fell into the trap like a lamb.”

“My God! What shall we do?”

“Get busy, and go after her! Right now! Where’s that Bradshaw?”

Tommy abandoned the Bradshaw for an A.B.C. “Here we are. Ebury, Yorks. From King’s Cross. 12.50—that’s the train she went by; 3.20 the next.”

“What about the car?”

“Send it up if you like, but we’d better stick to the train. The great thing is to keep calm.”

“That’s so,” groaned Julius, “but it gets my goat to think of that innocent young girl in danger!”

Tommy puckered his brow. “I don’t think it’s their game to do her any harm. She’s a hostage, that’s what she is. She’s in no immediate danger; if we tumble on to anything, she’s damned useful to them. As long as they’ve got her, they’ve got the whip hand of us. See?”

“Sure thing,” said Julius thoughtfully. “That’s so.”

The journey was wearisome. At a deserted station they got meticulous but perplexing directions to “the big house near the sea.” Rain, an overgrown drive, shuttered windows, mossed steps—silence everywhere. The rusty bell jangled discordantly; no one came. They walked round—still no sign of life.

“There must be a village handy,” said Julius. In a hamlet a workman told them the Moat House had been empty for years; the post office held the key. Presently they stood in the dark hall; thick dust lay everywhere, undisturbed. They searched in vain; returned in daylight; the tale was the same. They were about to give up when, near the gate among the leaves, Tommy stooped with a cry and held up a small gold brooch.

“That’s Tuppence’s!”

“I guess that settles it. She came as far as here, anyway. We’ll make the pub our headquarters and raise hell till we find her. Somebody must have seen her.”

They campaigned forthwith, separately and together, but nobody in the neighbourhood had seen a girl like Tuppence, or a car by the Moat House. Julius wired for his own car; daily they scoured lanes and properties; a grey limousine was traced to Harrogate—and a highly respectable maiden lady. Owners of motors were cross‑examined and apologised to. So well had the abduction been planned that the girl seemed literally to have vanished into thin air.

A week wore by. Over breakfast Tommy said grimly: “Do you know how long we’ve been here? And next Sunday is the 29th!”

“Shucks,” said Julius. “I’d almost forgotten the 29th. I’ve been thinking of nothing but Tuppence.”

“So have I. But time’s getting short. If we’re ever going to get hold of her at all, we must do it before the 29th—her life won’t be worth an hour’s purchase afterwards. I’m beginning to feel we’ve made a big mistake. We’ve wasted time.”

“I’m with you. We’ve been a couple of mutts. I’m going right back to London to put the case in the hands of your British police. Scotland Yard for me! Better late than never. We’ve been like a couple of babes playing ‘Here we go round the Mulberry Bush.’ Now I’m going right along to Scotland Yard to ask them to take me by the hand and show me the way I should go. I guess the professional always scores over the amateur in the end. Are you coming along?”

“What’s the good? One of us is enough. I’ll nose round here a bit longer. Something might turn up.”

“So long. I’ll be back in a couple of shakes with a few inspectors along.”

But the plan miscarried. Later in the day Tommy received a wire:

“Join me Manchester Midland Hotel. Important news--JULIUS.”

At 7.30 he arrived by a slow cross‑country train. Julius was on the platform.

“What is it? Is Tuppence found?”

“No. But I found this waiting in London. Just arrived.”

He handed over a telegraph form. Tommy’s eyes opened wide:

“Jane Finn found. Come Manchester Midland Hotel immediately--PEEL EDGERTON.”

Julius folded the form. “Queer,” he said thoughtfully.

“I thought that lawyer chap had quit!”

JANE FINN

“My train got in half an hour ago,” said Julius as they left the station. “I reckoned you’d come by this before I left London, and wired accordingly to Sir James. He’s booked rooms for us, and will be round to dine at eight.”

“What made you think he’d ceased to take any interest in the case?” asked Tommy.

“What he said,” replied Julius dryly. “The old bird’s as close as an oyster!”

Tommy shook his head, unconvinced.

Sir James arrived punctually. Julius introduced Tommy, and the great lawyer’s handclasp was warm.

“I am delighted to make your acquaintance, Mr. Beresford. I have heard so much about you from Miss Tuppence that it really seems as though I already know you quite well.”

He scanned the great lawyer eagerly. Like Tuppence, he felt the magnetism of the other’s personality. He was reminded of Mr. Carter. The two men, totally unlike so far as physical resemblance went, produced a similar effect. Beneath the weary manner of the one and the professional reserve of the other, lay the same quality of mind, keen-edged like a rapier.

In the meantime he was conscious of Sir James’s close scrutiny. When the lawyer dropped his eyes the young man had the feeling that the other had read him through and through like an open book. He could not but wonder what the final judgment was, but there was little chance of learning that. Sir James took in everything, but gave out only what he chose.

Julius broke out at once: how had he tracked the girl, why the secrecy, why let them think he’d quit? Sir James stroked his chin, smiling.

“Just so, just so. Well, she’s found. And that’s the great thing, isn’t it?”

“But where is she?” demanded Julius. “I thought you’d bring her along.”

“That would hardly be possible,” said Sir James gravely. “The young lady was knocked down in a street accident and sustained slight injuries to the head. She was taken to the infirmary, and on recovering consciousness gave her name as Jane Finn. When—ah!—I heard that, I arranged for her to be removed to the house of a doctor—a friend of mine—and wired at once for you. She relapsed into unconsciousness and has not spoken since.”

“She’s not seriously hurt?”

“Oh, a bruise and a cut or two; really, absurdly slight injuries to have produced such a condition. Her state is probably to be attributed to the mental shock consequent on recovering her memory.”

“It’s come back?” cried Julius.

“Undoubtedly, since she was able to give her real name.”

“After dinner,” Julius announced, “I shall go right away and see Jane.”

“That will be impossible, I fear. It is very unlikely they would allow her to see visitors at this time of night. I should suggest to-morrow morning about ten o’clock.”

“All the same, I reckon I’ll go round there to-night and see if I can’t ginger them up to break through their silly rules.”

“It will be quite useless, Mr. Hersheimmer.”

The words came out like the crack of a pistol, and Tommy looked up with a start. Julius was nervous and excited. The hand with which he raised his glass to his lips shook slightly, but his eyes held Sir James’s defiantly. For a moment the hostility between the two seemed likely to burst into flame, but in the end Julius lowered his eyes, defeated.

“For the moment, I reckon you’re the boss.”

“Thank you,” said the other. “We will say ten o’clock then?” With consummate ease he turned to Tommy. “The last I heard of you was that your friends were in grave anxiety on your behalf. Miss Tuppence was inclined to think you had got into difficulties.”

“I had, sir,” said Tommy cheerfully; and, helped by Sir James’s questions, he sketched his capture and escape. The lawyer’s interest sharpened at the mention of the girl who had aided him.

“There’s no doubt about her being one of the gang, I suppose?”

“I’m afraid not, sir. I thought perhaps they were keeping her by force, but the way she acted didn’t fit. She went back when she could have got away. She kept crying out to be taken to Marguerite.”

“She always signed herself Rita Vandemeyer,” mused Sir James. “Still, the girl may have used her full name. Curious that, at the very moment she cried out, Mrs. Vandemeyer was either dead or dying! There are one or two points that strike me as obscure—their sudden change of attitude towards yourself, for instance. By the way, the house was raided?”

“Yes, sir, but they’d all cleared out. Not a clue left.”

“I wonder——” He tapped the table. “I wish I had been there to go over the house.” He looked up. “And since then? What have you been doing?”

“I forgot that you didn’t know about Tuppence,” said Tommy slowly; and the sickening anxiety swept over him anew.

Sir James laid down his knife and fork sharply. “Has anything happened to Miss Tuppence?”

“She’s disappeared,” said Julius. Swiftly, between them, they gave the history of the week.

“A wire signed with your name? They knew enough of you both for that. They weren’t sure how much you had learnt. Their kidnapping of Miss Tuppence is the counter-move to your escape. If necessary they could seal your lips with a threat of what might happen to her.”

“That’s just what I thought, sir,” said Tommy.

“You had worked that out? Not bad—no, not at all bad. The curious thing is they certainly did not know anything about you at first. You are sure you did not disclose your identity?”

Tommy shook his head.

“That’s so,” said Julius. “Therefore some one put them wise—and not earlier than Sunday afternoon.”

“Yes, but who?”

“That almighty omniscient Mr. Brown, of course!”

Sir James looked up sharply. “You don’t believe in Mr. Brown, Mr. Hersheimmer?”

“No, sir, I do not. I reckon he’s a figurehead—a bogy name to frighten the children with. The real head is that Russian chap Kramenin. Whittington’s probably head of the English branch.”

“I disagree with you,” said Sir James shortly. “Mr. Brown exists.” He turned to Tommy. “Did you notice where that wire was handed in?”

“No, sir.”

“H’m. Got it with you?”

“It’s upstairs in my kit.”

“I’d like to have a look at it some time. You’ve wasted a week—” Tommy hung his head—“a day or so more is immaterial. We’ll deal with Miss Jane Finn first. Afterwards we’ll set to work to rescue Miss Tuppence from bondage. I don’t think she’s in any immediate danger. That is, so long as they don’t know that we’ve got Jane Finn, and that her memory has returned. We must keep that dark at all costs. You understand?”

They assented, and arrangements were made for the morning.

At ten o’clock they met Sir James on the doorstep and were introduced to the doctor.

“How’s the patient?”

“Going on well,” said the doctor. “Evidently no idea of the flight of time. Asked this morning how many had been saved from the Lusitania.”

“May we go up?”

“Certainly.”

Tommy’s heart beat sensibly faster as they followed the doctor upstairs. Jane Finn at last! The long-sought, the mysterious, the elusive Jane Finn! How wildly improbable success had seemed! And here in this house, her memory almost miraculously restored, lay the girl who held the future of England in her hands. A half groan broke from Tommy’s lips. If only Tuppence could have been at his side to share in the triumphant conclusion of their joint venture! Then he put the thought of Tuppence resolutely aside. His confidence in Sir James was growing. There was a man who would unerringly ferret out Tuppence’s whereabouts. In the meantime Jane Finn! And suddenly a dread clutched at his heart. It seemed too easy.... Suppose they should find her dead ... stricken down by the hand of Mr. Brown?

In another minute he was laughing at these melodramatic fancies. The doctor held open the door of a room and they passed in. On the white bed, bandages round her head, lay the girl. Somehow the whole scene seemed unreal, beautifully staged.

The girl looked at them with large wondering eyes. Sir James spoke:

“Miss Finn, this is your cousin, Mr. Julius P. Hersheimmer.”

A faint flush rose as Julius took her hand. “How do, Cousin Jane?”

“Are you really Uncle Hiram’s son?” Her low, soft voice, with its Western warmth, seemed vaguely familiar to Tommy, but he thrust the impression aside.

“Sure thing.”

“We used to read about Uncle Hiram in the papers,” she went on.

“But I never thought I’d meet you. Mother figured it out that Uncle Hiram would never get over being mad with her.”

“The old man was like that,” admitted Julius. “But I guess the new generation’s sort of different. First thing I thought about, soon as the war was over, was to come along and hunt you up.”

“They’ve been telling me dreadful things—that my memory went, and that there are years I shall never know.”

“You didn’t realize that yourself?”

“Why, no. It seems to me as though it were no time since we were being hustled into those boats. I can see it all now.” She closed her eyes with a shudder.

Julius glanced at Sir James, who nodded him on.

“Now, see here, Jane, there’s something we want to know. There was a man aboard that boat with some mighty important papers on him, and the big guns here have got a notion he passed the goods to you. Is that so?”

She hesitated, glancing at the others.

“Mr. Beresford is commissioned by the British Government to get those papers back,” said Julius promptly. “Sir James Peel Edgerton is an English Member of Parliament, and it’s owing to him we’ve ferreted you out. So you can go right ahead. Did Danvers give you the papers?”

“Yes. He said they’d have a better chance with me, because they would save the women and children first.”

“Just as we thought,” said Sir James.

“He said they were very important—that they might make all the difference to the Allies. But, if it’s all so long ago, and the war’s over, what does it matter now?”

“I guess history repeats itself, Jane. First there was a great hue and cry; then it all died down; and now the whole caboodle’s started over again—for rather different reasons. Then you can hand them over to us right away?”

“But I can’t.”

“What?”

“I haven’t got them.”

“You—haven’t—got them?” Julius punctuated the words.

“No—I hid them.”

“You hid them?”

“Yes. I got uneasy. People seemed to be watching me. It scared me—badly.” She pressed her hand to her head.

“It’s almost the last thing I remember before waking up in the hospital....”

“Go on,” said Sir James quietly. “What do you remember?”

“It was at Holyhead. In the confusion on the quay I slipped away. I took a car and told the man to drive me out of the town. When we got on the open road I saw a path at the side. I told the man to wait. The path led to the cliff, and down to the sea between big yellow gorse bushes—they were like golden flames. I looked round. There wasn’t a soul in sight. But just level with my head there was a hole in the rock. It was quite small—I could only just get my hand in, but it went a long way back. I took the oilskin packet from round my neck and shoved it right in as far as I could. Then I tore off a bit of gorse—My! but it did prick—and plugged the hole so you’d never guess there was a crevice. Then I marked the place carefully in my own mind. There was a queer boulder in the path there—like a dog sitting up begging. Then I went back. I caught the train. I was a bit ashamed of myself for fancying things maybe, but by and by I saw the man opposite wink at a woman next to me, and I felt scared again, and was glad the papers were safe. I went out into the corridor to get a little air. The woman called me back, said I’d dropped something, and when I stooped, something seemed to hit me—here.” She touched the back of her head. “I don’t remember anything more until I woke up in the hospital.”

“Thank you, Miss Finn,” said Sir James. “I hope we have not tired you?”

“Oh, that’s all right. My head aches a little, but otherwise I feel fine.”

Julius took her hand again. “So long, Cousin Jane. I’m going to get busy after those papers, but I’ll be back in two shakes, and I’ll tote you up to London and give you a fine time before we go back to the States! So hurry up and get well.”

TOO LATE

In the street they held an informal council. Sir James consulted his watch. “The boat train to Holyhead stops at Chester at 12.14. If you start at once you can catch the connection.”

Tommy looked up, puzzled. “Is there any need to hurry, sir? To-day is only the 24th.”

“I guess it’s always well to get up early in the morning,” said Julius. “We’ll make tracks for the depot right away.”

A little frown settled on Sir James’s brow. “I wish I could come. I am due to speak at two o’clock. It is unfortunate.”

“I guess there’s nothing complicated about this deal,” Julius remarked.

“Just a game of hide-and-seek, that’s all.”

“I hope so,” said Sir James.

“Sure thing. What else could it be?”

“You are still young, Mr. Hersheimmer. At my age you will probably have learnt one lesson. ‘Never underestimate your adversary.’”

The gravity of his tone impressed Tommy, but had little effect upon Julius.

“You think Mr. Brown might come along and take a hand? If he does, I’m ready for him.” He patted his pocket. “Little Willie here travels round with me everywhere.” He produced a murderous-looking automatic, tapped it, and returned it to its home.

“But he won’t be needed this trip. There’s nobody to put Mr. Brown wise.”

“There was nobody to put Mr. Brown wise to the fact that Mrs. Vandemeyer meant to betray him,” said Sir James dryly. “Nevertheless, Mrs. Vandemeyer died without speaking.” He held out his hand. “Good-bye, and good luck. Take no unnecessary risks once the papers are in your hands. If there is any reason to believe that you have been shadowed, destroy them at once. The game is in your hands now.”

Ten minutes later they were in a first-class carriage en route for Chester. Silence held them for some time; then Julius said unexpectedly:

“Say, did you ever make a darned fool of yourself over a girl’s face?”

Tommy searched his mind. “Can’t say I have. Why?”

“Because for the last two months I’ve been making a sentimental idiot of myself over Jane! First moment I clapped eyes on her photograph my heart did all the usual stunts you read about in novels. I guess I’m ashamed to admit it, but I came over here determined to find her and fix it all up, and take her back as Mrs. Julius P. Hersheimmer!”

“Oh!” said Tommy, amazed.

Julius uncrossed his legs brusquely and continued: “Just shows what an almighty fool a man can make of himself! One look at the girl in the flesh, and I was cured! No disparagement to Jane. She’s a real nice girl, and some fellow will fall in love with her right away. But there was something about that photo—” He shook his head and sighed. “I guess romance is a mighty queer thing!”

“It must be,” said Tommy coldly, “if you can come over here in love with one girl, and propose to another within a fortnight.”

Julius coloured. “Well, you see, I’d got kind of tired thinking I’d never find Jane—and then—oh, well, the French are much more sensible. They keep romance and marriage apart——”

Tommy flushed. “Well, I’m damned! If that’s——”

Julius hastened to interrupt. “Say now, don’t be hasty. I don’t mean what you mean. I take it Americans have a higher opinion of morality than you have even. What I meant was that the French set about marriage in a businesslike way—find two people who are suited to one another, look after the money affairs, and see the whole thing practically, and in a businesslike spirit.”

“If you ask me,” said Tommy hotly, “we’re all too damned businesslike nowadays.”

“Cool down, son,” said Julius; and prudently said no more.

By Holyhead the cheerful grin had returned to Tommy’s face. With a road map and brief consultation they hired a taxi and drove out towards Treaddur Bay, made the man go slowly, and watched for the path. Not long after leaving the town Tommy stopped the car, asked carelessly if the path led to the sea, and, hearing it did, paid off the driver.

They turned to the narrow path between swelling hedges of golden gorse. Julius led; twice Tommy looked back uneasily.

“What is it?” asked Julius.

“I don’t know. I keep fancying there’s some one following us.”

“Can’t be. We’d see him.”

Nevertheless Tommy’s sense of the enemy’s omniscience deepened.

“I rather wish that fellow would come along,” said Julius. He patted his pocket. “Little William here is just aching for exercise!”

“Do you always carry it—him—with you?” asked Tommy, impressed.

“Most always. You never know what might turn up.”

The path ran along the side of the cliff. Suddenly Julius halted so abruptly that Tommy cannoned into him.

“What’s up?”

“Look there. If that doesn’t beat the band!”

Half obstructing the path stood a huge boulder, fancifully like a begging terrier.

“Well,” said Tommy, refusing to be thrilled, “it’s what we expected to see.”

“Sure we expected it,” said Julius sadly. “But it kind of rattles me all the same to see it sitting there just where we expected to find it!”

“Push on. What about the hole?”

They scanned the cliff. “The gorse won’t be there after all these years,” said Tommy idiotically.

“I guess you’re right,” said Julius solemnly.

Tommy pointed with a shaking hand. “What about that crevice there?”

“That’s it—for sure,” breathed Julius.

“When I was in France,” said Tommy, “whenever my batman failed to call me, he said he had come over queer. I never believed it. But whether he felt it or not, there is such a sensation. I’ve got it now! Badly!” He glared at the rock. “Damn it! It’s impossible! Five years! Bird’s-nesting boys, picnic parties, thousands of people passing! It can’t be there! It’s a hundred to one against it! It’s against all reason!”

Indeed, he felt it to be impossible—more, perhaps, because he could not believe in his own success where so many others had failed. The thing was too easy, therefore it could not be. The hole would be empty.

Julius’s smile widened. “I guess you’re rattled now all right. Well, here goes!” He thrust his hand into the crevice, grimaced. “It’s a tight fit. Jane’s hand must be smaller. I don’t feel anything—no—say, what’s this? Gee whiz!” He waved aloft a small discoloured packet. “It’s the goods all right. Sewn up in oilskin. Hold it while I get my penknife.”

The unbelievable had happened. Tommy held the packet tenderly. “It’s queer,” he murmured.

“You’d think the stitches would have rotted. They look just as good as new.”

They cut the stitches, ripped away the oilskin. Inside was a small folded sheet. They unfolded it with trembling fingers. The sheet was blank.

“A dummy?” hazarded Julius. “Was Danvers just a decoy?”

Tommy’s face cleared. “I’ve got it! Sympathetic ink!”

“Worth trying. Heat usually does the trick.”

They kindled a little fire of twigs and leaves. Tommy held the paper near the glow. The paper curled; nothing more. Suddenly Julius gripped his arm. Faint brown characters were appearing.

“Gee whiz! You’ve got it! Say, that idea of yours was great.”

Tommy held steady until the heat had done its work, then withdrew it—only to utter a cry.

Across the sheet, in neat brown printing, ran the words: WITH THE COMPLIMENTS OF MR. BROWN.

They stared at each other stupidly. Somehow, inexplicably, Mr. Brown had forestalled them.

“It accounts for the stitches being new,” said Tommy dully. “We might have guessed....”

“Never mind the darned stitches,” cried Julius. “How did he get ahead of us? We hustled all we knew. It’s downright impossible. And how did he know? Was there a dictaphone in Jane’s room? Then one of the nurses was a crook and listened at the door. How’s that?”

“No one could have known beforehand that she was going to be in that house—much less that room,” objected Tommy. “He may have found out months ago and removed the papers then—No, that won’t wash! They’d have been published at once.”

“Sure thing they would! No, some one’s got ahead of us to-day by an hour or so. But how they did it gets my goat.”

“I wish that chap Peel Edgerton had been with us,” said Tommy.

“Why? The mischief was done when we came.”

Tommy could not explain the illogical sense that Sir James’s presence would somehow have averted the catastrophe. He pulled himself together. “It’s no good arguing. The game’s up. There’s only one thing for me to do: get back to London and warn Mr. Carter. It’s only a matter of hours now before the blow falls.”

He took the midnight mail. Julius elected to stay the night at Holyhead. Half an hour after arrival, Tommy stood before his chief.

“I’ve come to report, sir. I’ve failed—failed badly.”

“You mean that the treaty——?”

“Is in the hands of Mr. Brown, sir.”

“Ah!” said Mr. Carter quietly. His face did not change, but Tommy caught a flicker of despair in his eyes. “Well, we mustn’t sag at the knees, I suppose. I’m glad to know definitely. We must do what we can. Don’t take it to heart, lad,” he added kindly.

“You did your best. You were up against one of the biggest brains of the century. And you came very near success. Remember that.”

Through Tommy’s mind flashed the assurance: “It’s hopeless, and he knows it’s hopeless!”

“I blame myself,” said Mr. Carter. “I have been blaming myself ever since I heard this other news.”

A new fear gripped Tommy. “Is there—something more, sir?”

“I’m afraid so.” He handed him a typed sheet.

“Tuppence——?” faltered Tommy.

“Read for yourself.”

The typed words danced: the description of a green toque, a coat with a handkerchief in the pocket marked P.L.C. Mr. Carter answered his look: “Washed up on the Yorkshire coast—near Ebury. I’m afraid—it looks very much like foul play.”

“My God!” gasped Tommy. “Tuppence! Those devils—I’ll never rest till I’ve got even with them! I’ll hunt them down! I’ll——”

“I know what you feel like, my poor boy,” said Mr. Carter gently. “But it’s no good. You’ll waste your strength uselessly. It may sound harsh, but my advice is: Cut your losses. Time’s merciful. You’ll forget.”

“Forget Tuppence? Never!”

“So you think now. Well, it won’t bear thinking of—that brave little girl! I’m confoundedly sorry.”

“I’m taking up your time, sir,” said Tommy, making an effort. “There’s no need for you to blame yourself. I dare say we were a couple of young fools. You warned us all right. But I wish to God I’d been the one to get it in the neck. Good-bye, sir.”

Back at the Ritz, he packed mechanically, his thoughts far away. He was still bewildered by the introduction of tragedy into his cheerful commonplace existence. What fun they had had, he and Tuppence! And now—oh, it couldn’t be true! Tuppence—dead! Little Tuppence, brimming over with life! It was a horrible dream.

They brought him a note: a few kind words of sympathy from Peel Edgerton, who had read the headline (EX-V.A.D. FEARED DROWNED). The letter ended with the offer of a post on a ranch in the Argentine.

“Kind old beggar,” muttered Tommy, flinging it aside.

The door burst open; Julius, with a newspaper.

“Say, what’s all this? They seem to have got some fool idea about Tuppence.”

“It’s true,” said Tommy quietly.

“You mean they’ve done her in?”

Tommy nodded. “I suppose when they got the treaty she wasn’t any good to them any longer, and they were afraid to let her go.”

“Well, I’m darned!” said Julius brokenly. “Little Tuppence. She sure was the pluckiest little girl——”

Something cracked in Tommy’s brain. He sprang up. “Oh, get out! You don’t really care, damn you! You asked her to marry you in your rotten cold-blooded way, but I loved her. I’d have given the soul out of my body to save her. I’d have stood by and let her marry you, because you could have given her the sort of time she ought to have had, and I was only a poor devil without a penny. But it wouldn’t have been because I didn’t care!”

“See here,” began Julius temperately.

“Oh, go to the devil! Don’t come here talking about ‘little Tuppence.’ Go and look after your cousin. Tuppence is my girl! I’ve always loved her—from the time we played together as kids. I shall never forget when I was in hospital, and she came in in that ridiculous cap and apron! It was like a miracle to see the girl I loved turn up in a nurse’s kit——”

“A nurse’s kit!” Julius swung round. “Gee whiz! I must be going to Colney Hatch! I could swear I’ve seen Jane in a nurse’s cap too. And that’s plumb impossible! No—by gum, I’ve got it! It was her I saw talking to Whittington at that nursing home in Bournemouth. She wasn’t a patient there! She was a nurse!”

“I dare say,” said Tommy angrily. “She’s probably been in with them from the start. I shouldn’t wonder if she stole those papers from Danvers to begin with.”

“I’m darned if she did!” shouted Julius. “She’s my cousin, and as patriotic a girl as ever stepped.”

“I don’t care a damn what she is, but get out of here!”

They were on the point of blows, when Julius’s anger dropped away.

“All right, son,” he said quietly, “I’m going. I don’t blame you. It’s mighty lucky you did say it. I’ve been the most almighty blithering darned idiot that it’s possible to imagine. Calm down—I’m going right away now—going to the London and North Western Railway depot, if you want to know.”

“I don’t care a damn where you’re going,” growled Tommy.

As the door closed, he turned back to his suit-case. “That’s the lot,” he muttered, and rang the bell.

“Take my luggage down.”

“Yes, sir. Going away, sir?”

“I’m going to the devil,” said Tommy, regardless of the menial’s feelings.

That functionary, however, merely replied respectfully: “Yes, sir. Shall I call a taxi?”

Tommy nodded. Where was he going? He hadn’t the faintest idea—only a fixed determination to get even with Mr. Brown. He re-read Sir James’s letter and shook his head. Tuppence must be avenged. Still, it was kind.

“Better answer it,” he decided. He went to the writing-table. There were envelopes but no paper. He rang. No one came. Fuming, he remembered there was paper in Julius’s sitting-room. The American had announced his immediate departure; there was no fear of meeting him.

The room was deserted. Tommy crossed to the writing-table and opened the middle drawer. A photograph, carelessly thrust in face upwards, caught his eye. He stood rooted; then took it out, shut the drawer, and sat down, staring.

What on earth was a photograph of the French girl Annette doing in Julius Hersheimmer’s writing-table?

The Prime Minister’s fingers tapped the desk; his face was worn. “Do you really mean things are not so desperate after all?”

“So this lad seems to think,” said Mr. Carter, handing him a sprawling boyish letter.

“DEAR MR. CARTER,

“Something’s turned up that has given me a jar. Of course I may be an awful ass, but I don’t think so. If I’m right, that girl at Manchester was just a plant. Sham packet and all—prearranged to make us think the game was up; therefore we must have been pretty hot on the scent.

“I think I know who the real Jane Finn is, and I’ve even got an idea where the papers are. That last’s only a guess, but I’ve a sort of feeling it’ll turn out right. Anyhow, I enclose it in a sealed envelope for what it’s worth. Please don’t open it till the very last moment—midnight on the 28th—you’ll understand why in a minute.

“You see, I’ve figured it out that those things of Tuppence’s are a plant too, and she’s no more drowned than I am. As a last chance they’ll let Jane Finn escape, hoping she’s been shamming this memory stunt, and that once she thinks she’s free she’ll go right away to the cache. Of course it’s an awful risk for them, because she knows all about them—but they’re desperate for that treaty. But if they know the papers have been recovered by us, neither of those two girls’ lives will be worth an hour’s purchase. I must try and get hold of Tuppence before Jane escapes.

“I want a repeat of that telegram sent to Tuppence at the Ritz. Sir James Peel Edgerton said you’d manage that for me. He’s frightfully clever.

“One last thing—please have that house in Soho watched day and night.

“Yours, etc., THOMAS BERESFORD.”

“The enclosure?”

“In the vaults of the Bank,” said Carter. “I’m taking no chances.”

“Would it not be better to open it now?” suggested the Prime Minister. “Secure the document at once, and keep it secret?”

“Can we keep it secret?” Carter snapped his fingers.

“Spies all round us. Once it’s known, I wouldn’t give that—for the lives of those girls. The boy trusted me; I shan’t let him down.”

He went on: “Outwardly, he’s an ordinary, clean‑limbed, rather block‑headed young Englishman—slow. But impossible to lead astray through imagination—he hasn’t any. He worries things out, and once he’s got hold of anything he doesn’t let go. The little lady’s quite different. More intuition and less common sense. They make a pretty pair: pace and stamina.”

“He seems confident,” mused the Prime Minister.

“Yes, and that’s what gives me hope. He’s the kind of diffident youth who would have to be _very_ sure before he ventured an opinion at all.”

“And it is this boy who will defeat the master criminal of our time?” murmured the Prime Minister.

“This—boy. But I sometimes fancy I see a shadow behind.”

“Peel Edgerton?” The Prime Minister was surprised.

“Yes. I see his hand in this.” He struck the letter. “If anyone runs Mr. Brown to earth, Peel Edgerton will. He’s on the case now, and doesn’t want it known. He sent me a cutting from an American paper—man’s body found near the docks in New York about three weeks ago; young fellow, thirty‑five, poorly dressed, face badly disfigured—never identified. He asked for any information.”

“And you fancy the matters are connected?”

“Somehow I do.”

Sir James arrived. A half whimsical thought flashed across the Premier’s mind. “My successor, perhaps!” To Carter’s direct question he said smilingly: “He rang me up. Thanked me for a letter—I’d offered him a job. Then he reminded me that Manchester bogus telegram. I asked if anything untoward had occurred. He said it had: in a drawer in Mr. Hersheimmer’s room he had discovered a photograph.”

“I asked him if the photograph bore the name and address of a Californian photographer. He replied: ‘You’re on to it, sir. It had.’”

“The French girl, Annette, who saved his life,” Sir James added.

“I asked what he had done with it. He replied: put it back. That was good. From the moment the Manchester girl was proved a plant, everything was altered. Young Beresford saw that without my telling him. But he couldn’t trust his judgment on Miss Cowley; did I think she was alive? Weighing the evidence, I told him there was a very decided chance in favour of it. That brought us to the telegram. I advised him to apply to you for a copy of the original wire. Words might have been erased and altered with the express intention of sending searchers on a false trail.”

Carter produced a paper and read: “Come at once, Astley Priors, Gatehouse, Kent. Great developments—TOMMY.”

“Very simple and very ingenious,” said Sir James. “And the one clue they overlooked was the page‑boy’s statement that Miss Cowley drove to Charing Cross. They were so sure of themselves they took it for granted he was mistaken.”

“Then young Beresford is now—?”

“At Gatehouse, Kent, unless I’m much mistaken.”

“I rather wonder you’re not there too.”

“I’m busy on a case,” said Sir James. “Perhaps it would be more correct to say I’m preparing a case.”

“What I don’t understand,” said the Prime Minister suddenly, “is how that photograph came to be in Mr. Hersheimmer’s drawer.”

“Perhaps it never left it,” suggested the lawyer gently.

“But the bogus inspector? Inspector Brown?”

“Ah!” said Sir James thoughtfully. He rose. “I mustn’t keep you. Go on with the affairs of the nation. I must get back to—my case.”

Two days later Julius Hersheimmer came back from Manchester. A note lay on his table:

“DEAR HERSHEIMMER,

“Sorry I lost my temper. In case I don’t see you again, good‑bye. I’ve been offered a job in the Argentine, and might as well take it.

“Yours, TOMMY BERESFORD.”

A peculiar smile lingered on Julius’s face. He threw the letter into the basket.

“The darned fool!” he murmured.

After ringing up Sir James, Tommy made straight for South Audley Mansions. He found Albert at his post and introduced himself as a friend of Tuppence’s. Albert’s eyes brightened.

“Things has been very quiet here lately,” he said wistfully.

“Hope the young lady’s keeping well, sir?”

“That’s just the point, Albert. She’s disappeared.”

“You don’t mean as the crooks have got her? In the Underworld?”

“No, dash it, in this world!”

“It’s a h’expression, sir. At the pictures the crooks always have a restoorant in the Underworld. But do you think as they’ve done her in?”

“I hope not. By the way, have you an aunt, a cousin, a grandmother—any suitable female relation likely to kick the bucket?”

A delighted grin spread over Albert’s countenance. “I’m on, sir. My poor aunt what lives in the country has been mortal bad a long time, and she’s asking for me with her dying breath.”

“Good. Can you report that properly and meet me at Charing Cross in an hour?”

“I’ll be there, sir.”

Together they took rooms at the inn in Gatehouse. Albert, indefatigable, cemented an alliance with the greengrocer’s boy and ingratiated himself with the cook at Malthouse. Astley Priors, he learned, belonged to a Dr. Adams—retired, “but he took a few private patients”—here the landlord tapped his forehead knowingly—“balmy ones!” A popular figure in the village, free with subscriptions.

Tommy’s doubts pricked him. Could this genial, well‑known figure be a dangerous criminal? No hint of anything sinister. Suppose it was a gigantic mistake? Then he remembered the “private patients.” He inquired quietly whether there was a young lady among them—describing Tuppence. Nobody knew; patients were seldom seen. A guarded description of Annette also failed.

That evening he and Albert crawled, at Albert’s insistence, painfully on their stomachs through the shrubbery, thereby producing a great deal more noise than if they had stood upright. In any case, these precautions were totally unnecessary. Tommy had imagined a possible fierce watchdog. Albert’s fancy ran to a puma, or a tame cobra. But they reached a shrubbery near the house quite unmolested.

The blinds of the dining‑room were up; a large company sat round the table; the port went its round; scraps of county‑cricket talk drifted through the open window. The fair‑bearded spectacled gentleman at the head looked honest and normal. Again the cold chill seized Tommy.

The following day, after more errands kitchen‑ward, Albert brought the first hopeful news: there was a French young lady staying in the house. Tommy thrust doubts aside. To‑day was the 27th. The 29th—“Labour Day”—was at hand; rumours of a general strike were rife; the Government said nothing; Labour leaders were divided while subtle forces urged extremism. With the fatal document in Mr. Brown’s hands, public opinion would swing to the revolutionaries; failing that, the issue was an even chance. Tommy nourished a preposterous dream: unmask Mr. Brown, and the whole organization would crumble; honest men, left to themselves, might patch up an eleventh‑hour peace. “This is a one‑man show,” said Tommy to himself. “The thing to do is to get hold of the man.” Hence the sealed envelope—bait.

That evening, crouched in the drive, he gave a sudden gasp. Against a second‑floor blind a silhouette stood out—a silhouette he would have known anywhere. Tuppence was in that house!

He clutched Albert. “Stay here. When I begin to sing, watch that window.”

He retreated to the drive, and in a deep, unsteady roar began:

I am a Soldier
A jolly British Soldier;
You can see that I’m a Soldier by my feet...

In Tuppence’s hospital days the gramophone had ground it out a hundred times. An unimpeachable butler, with an unimpeachable footman, issued from the front door. With perfect decorum they seized him by either arm and ran him out of the gate. “If you intrude again, I shall send for the police,” said the butler—Whittington—beautifully.

Tommy waited at the inn till Albert returned, triumphant. “While they was a‑running of you out the window opened, and something was chucked. It was wrapped round a letterweight.” On the scrap were three words: “To‑morrow—same time.”

“Good egg!” said Tommy.

“I wrote a message on a piece of paper, wrapped it round a stone, and chucked it through the window,” added Albert, breathless and then crestfallen under Tommy’s groan. “Said we was a‑staying at the inn. If she could get away, to come there and croak like a frog.”

“Your zeal will be the undoing of us, Albert. What did you say?”

He repeated it, abashed.

“She’ll know that’s you,” said Tommy with a sigh of relief. “Your imagination runs away with you, you know. Why, you wouldn’t recognize a frog croaking if you heard it. Cheer up. No harm done. That butler’s an old friend of mine—I bet he knew who I was, though he didn’t let on. It’s not their game to show suspicion. That’s why we’ve found it fairly plain sailing. They don’t want to discourage me altogether. On the other hand, they don’t want to make it too easy. I’m a pawn in their game, Albert, that’s what I am. You see, if the spider lets the fly walk out too easily, the fly might suspect it was a put‑up job. Hence the usefulness of that promising youth, Mr. T. Beresford, who’s blundered in just at the right moment for them. But later, Mr. T. Beresford had better look out!”

He elaborated a plan for the morrow, and retired for the night in some elation, confident they would not interfere up to a point. It was after that Tommy proposed to give them a surprise.

Near midnight a rude‑looking carter, mud‑spattered, demanded him in the bar, and held out a dirty folded note: “Take this to the gentleman at the inn near Astley Priors. He will give you ten shillings.” The handwriting was Tuppence’s. Tommy snatched it, paid.

“DEAR TOMMY,

“I knew it was you last night. Don’t go this evening. They’ll be lying in wait for you. They’re taking us away this morning. I heard something about Wales—Holyhead, I think. I’ll drop this on the road if I get a chance. Annette told me how you’d escaped. Buck up.

“Yours, TWOPENCE.”

“Pack my bag! We’re off!” he shouted to Albert—then, before the boots had ceased thundering upstairs, another cry: “Albert! I’m a damned fool! Unpack that bag!”

He smoothed the note thoughtfully. “Yes, a damned fool,” he said softly.

“But so’s some one else! And at last I know who it is!”

In his suite at Claridge’s, Kramenin reclined and dictated in sibilant Russian. The telephone purred; the secretary announced, “A Mr. Julius P. Hersheimmer.” The name narrowed the Russian’s eyes.

“He declines to state his business. Says it is entirely private and personal.”

“Bring him up.”

Julius entered abruptly. “Monsieur Kramenin? I’ve got some very important business I’d like to talk over, if I can see you alone.”

“My secretary, Monsieur Grieber, from whom I have no secrets.”

“That may be so—but I have. Tell him to scoot. The next room won’t do—I know these ducal suites—and I want this one plumb empty except for you and me. Send him round to a store to buy a penn’orth of peanuts.”

Kramenin yielded. The door closed. With a sudden change of manner, Julius whipped out a big automatic. “Hands up—or I shoot!”

The Russian flung up his hands. Julius took his measure in that instant: abject physical coward.

“This is an outrage! Do you mean to kill me?”

“Not if you keep your voice down—and don’t edge to that bell. That’s better. What do you want? Money?”

“No. I want Jane Finn.”

“I—I never heard of her!”

“You’re a darned liar. Little Willie here is just hopping mad to go off!”

“You wouldn’t dare—”

“Oh, yes, I would, son!” Julius went on mercilessly.

“You will tell me where she is—now.”

“I daren’t.”

“Afraid of whom? Mr. Brown? Ah! There is such a person, then? The mere mention of him scares you stiff!”

“I have seen him. Spoken to him face to face. I did not know it till afterwards. He was one of a crowd. I should not know him again. But I know this—he is a man to fear. He knows everything—and his vengeance is swift. Even I—Kramenin!—would not be exempt.”

“He’ll never know,” said Julius.

“You will hang if you shoot me,” muttered the Russian irresolutely.

“No, stranger, that’s where you’re wrong. You forget the dollars. A big crowd of solicitors will get busy, and they’ll get some high-brow doctors on the job, and the end of it all will be that they’ll say my brain was unhinged. I shall spend a few months in a quiet sanatorium, my mental health will improve, the doctors will declare me sane again, and all will end happily for little Julius. I guess I can bear a few months’ retirement in order to rid the world of you, but don’t you kid yourself I’ll hang for it!”

The Russian believed him. Corrupt himself, he believed implicitly in the power of money.

“I’m going to count five. If I get past four, don’t you worry any about Mr. Brown: maybe he’ll send flowers to the funeral, but you won’t smell them! One—two—three—four—”

“Do not shoot. I will do all you wish.”

“I thought you’d hear sense. Where is the girl?”

“At Gatehouse, in Kent. Astley Priors, the place is called.”

“A prisoner?”

“She is not allowed to leave. The little fool has lost her memory, curse her!”

“That’s been annoying for you. What about the other girl—the one you decoyed away?”

“She’s there too.”

“That’s good,” said Julius. “And a lovely night for the run.”

“What run?”

“Down to Gatehouse. I’m not such a kid as to leave you here to telephone. Into the bedroom—your thick coat—fur‑lined? And you a Socialist! Now we walk out, and don’t forget I can shoot through my coat pocket. One word, or a glance even, and there’ll sure be a strange face in the Sulphur and Brimstone Works!”

They went down, past the servants—Kramenin’s nerve failed him at the last second; the American was a man of his word. In the car Julius spoke over his shoulder to the chauffeur: “This gentleman is a Russian Bolshevik. We don’t want to shoot him, but it may be necessary. To Gatehouse, Kent. Make it an hour. I’m in a hurry.”

For an hour Julius discoursed cheerfully on men he had “shot” in Arizona, ‘Frisco, the Rockies. At the end of it, Kramenin was more dead than alive. “Now you’ll just tell them what I say,” Julius concluded, as the car swept up the drive.

The door opened; the butler stood there. Julius pressed the revolver to the Russian’s ribs. “Now. And be careful.”

Kramenin’s lips were white. “It is I—Kramenin! Bring down the girl at once! There is no time to lose!”

Whittington appeared. “You! What’s up? Surely you know the plan—”

“We have been betrayed!” cried Kramenin, in the time‑honoured phrase that breeds panics. “Plans must be abandoned. We must save our skins. The girl! At once! The other little fool had better come too.”

Whittington hesitated—hardly a moment—then ran in. Agonizing minutes; then two figures, cloaked, were hustled out. The smaller resisted; Whittington shoved her unceremoniously. Julius leaned forward; the light from the open door fell on his face. A man behind Whittington gave a startled exclamation. Concealment was at an end.

“Get a move on!” Julius shouted. The car bounded. A flash—report—the bullet shaved the taller girl by an inch.

“Get down, Jane,” cried Julius. “Flat on the bottom.” He thrust her sharply forward, then, standing, took careful aim and fired. “Hit him,” he announced grimly. “Not killed. Skunks like that take a lot of killing. Are you all right, Tuppence?”

“Of course. Where’s Tommy? And who’s this?” She indicated the shivering Russian.

“Tommy’s making tracks for the Argentine. I guess he thought you’d turned up your toes. Steady through the gate! They’ll telephone—look out for snares. Who’s this? Let me present Monsieur Kramenin. I persuaded him to come on the trip for his health.”

“Curse you!” burst out the Russian suddenly. “They know now that I betrayed them. My life won’t be safe an hour.”

“That’s so,” assented Julius. “I’d advise you to make tracks for Russia right away.”

“Let me go then. I have done what you asked.”

“Not for the pleasure of your company. But sure—hop it.” The car slowed; before it had stopped he had swung himself out, and vanished into the night.

“Jane, you can get up now.”

“How did you ‘persuade’ him?” asked the girl.

Julius tapped the automatic. “Little Willie takes the credit.”

“Splendid!” she cried.

“Annette and I didn’t know what would happen,” said Tuppence. “Old Whittington hurried us off. We thought it was lambs to the slaughter.”

“Annette?” said Julius slowly. “Is that what you call her?”

“It’s her name,” said Tuppence, opening her eyes wide.

“Shucks! She may think it’s her name—her memory’s gone, poor kid—but it’s the one real and original Jane Finn we’ve got here.”

“What?” cried Tuppence.

An angry spurt; a bullet embedded itself in the upholstery behind her head.

“Down with you,” snapped Julius. “An ambush. They got busy quickly. Push her a bit,” he called to the wheel. Three more shots rang out; happily wide. Julius leaned over the back.

“Nothing to shoot at.”

He raised a hand to his cheek. “Only a scratch.”

The tall girl sprang to her feet. “Let me out! It is me they’re after. I’m the one they want. You shall not lose your lives because of me.”

Julius took her by both arms and looked straight at her. She had spoken without a trace of foreign accent. “Sit down, kid,” he said gently.

“I guess there’s nothing wrong with your memory. Been fooling them all the time, eh?”

She nodded—and burst into tears.

“There, there,” said Julius, patting her shoulder. “We’re not going to let you quit.”

“You’re from home,” she sobbed. “Your voice—It makes me home‑sick.”

“Sure I’m from home. I’m your cousin—Julius Hersheimmer. I came over to find you—and a pretty dance you’ve led me.”

The car slackened. “Cross‑roads here, sir,” called the man at the wheel. “I’m not sure of the way.”

The car almost halted. As it did so, a figure climbed suddenly over the back, and plunged head first among them.

“Sorry,” said Tommy, extricating himself.

A babel of exclamations. He answered them, breathless: “In the bushes by the drive. Hung on behind. Couldn’t let you know at that pace. Now then, you girls, get out!”

“Get out?”

“Yes. There’s a station up that road. Train due in three minutes. You’ll catch it if you hurry.”

“What the devil—” began Julius. “Do you think you can fool them by leaving the car?”

“You and I aren’t leaving. Only the girls.”

“You’re crazed, Beresford! You can’t let them go alone. It’ll be the end of it.”

Tommy turned to Tuppence, stern. “Get out at once. Take her with you, and do exactly as I say. No one will harm you. Go straight to Sir James Peel Edgerton. Mr. Carter lives out of town, but you’ll be safe with Sir James.”

“Darn you!” cried Julius. “You’re mad. Jane, you stay.”

With a sudden swift movement Tommy snatched the revolver from Julius’s hand and levelled it. “Now will you believe I’m in earnest? Get out, both of you, and do as I say—or I’ll shoot.”

Tuppence sprang out, dragging the reluctant Jane. “Come on. If Tommy’s sure—he’s sure. Be quick; we’ll miss the train.”

They ran.

Julius’s pent‑up rage burst forth. “What the hell—”

“Dry up!” said Tommy. “I want a few words with you, Mr. Julius Hersheimmer.”

Her arm through Jane’s, dragging her along, Tuppence reached the station. The train drew in as they gained the platform; she thrust Jane into an empty first-class, and they sank breathless on the padded seats.

A man looked in, then passed on. Jane started; her eyes were wide with terror.

“Is he one of them, do you think?” she breathed.

Tuppence shook her head. “No, no. It’s all right. Tommy wouldn’t have told us to do this unless he was sure we’d be all right.”

“But he doesn’t know them as I do! Five years! Five long years!” She shivered.

“Never mind. It’s all over.”

“Is it?”

The train sped through the night. Jane started up. “I thought I saw a face—looking in through the window.”

“There’s nothing. See.” Tuppence let the pane down, then drew Jane’s hand into hers. “Lie back, and don’t think.”

“My cousin didn’t think so. He didn’t want us to do this.”

Tuppence was silent a moment. “I was thinking of something. But I don’t want to tell you—not now. Do what I tell you—lie back and don’t think of anything.”

Jane’s lashes drooped. Tuppence sat bolt upright, a watchful terrier on guard, eyes flicking from window to window. Not that she disbelieved in Tommy, but occasionally she was shaken with doubts as to whether anyone so simple and honest as he was could ever be a match for the fiendish subtlety of the arch-criminal. If once they reached Sir James all would be well. But would they reach him?

At Charing Cross Jane sat up with a start. “Have we arrived?”

“Oh, I thought we’d get to London all right. If there’s going to be any fun, now is when it will begin. Quick. We’ll nip into a taxi.” As they started, a man looked in at the window; Tuppence had a horrible sense of being hemmed in.

“King’s Cross,” she directed, then, low to Jane: “If they think we’re going to Sir James, this will put them off the scent. Now they’ll imagine we’re going to Mr. Carter.”

A block at Holborn—what Tuppence had waited for. “Quick. Open the right-hand door!” They slipped out, took another taxi, and doubled back, this time direct to Carlton House Terrace.

“This ought to do them,” murmured Tuppence with satisfaction. “How that other taxi man will swear! But I’ll send him a postal order to-morrow— What’s this—Oh!”

A grinding bump. Another taxi had collided with them. Tuppence was out in a flash. A policeman approached; she pressed five shillings on the driver, and drew Jane into the crowd.

“It’s only a step or two now.” Trafalgar Square. “It may be my fancy, but I feel as though there was some one behind us.”

“Hurry!” breathed Jane.

At the corner of Carlton House Terrace a large, apparently intoxicated man lurched across their path. “Whither away so fast?” He clutched Jane’s shoulder. Footsteps sounded behind. Tuppence lowered her head and butted him full in the middle. He sat down abruptly on the pavement. They ran on, choking for breath. At Sir James’s door they pulled bell and knocker together. The man hesitated at the foot of the steps; the door opened; they tumbled into the hall.

Sir James came from the library, caught Jane as she swayed, laid her on a leather couch, and gave her brandy. “It’s all right. Don’t be afraid, my child. You’re quite safe.”

He looked quizzically at Tuppence. “So you’re not dead, Miss Tuppence, any more than that Tommy boy of yours was!”

“The Young Adventurers take a lot of killing,” boasted Tuppence.

“So it seems,” said Sir James dryly. “Am I right that the joint venture has ended in success, and that this—” he turned to the girl—“is Miss Jane Finn?”

Jane sat up. “Yes. I am Jane Finn. I have a lot to tell you.”

“When you are stronger——”

“No—now! I shall feel safer when I have told everything.”

“As you please,” said the lawyer, and Jane began, low and steady:

“I came over on the Lusitania to take up a post in a Paris hospital. I was dying to help. When the Lusitania was torpedoed, a man—Mr. Danvers—asked me if I was a patriotic American. He said he carried papers that were life or death to the Allies. He gave them to me. I was to watch for an advertisement in the Times. If it didn’t appear, I was to take them to the American Ambassador.

“At first I had no suspicions. Then, on the boat to Holyhead, I noticed a woman—Mrs. Vandemeyer—who had been ‘chummy’ with me, talking to queer-looking men, and they watched me. I’d seen her near Mr. Danvers when he gave me the packet. I got scared. I ripped open the oilskin, substituted blank paper, sewed it up again. The two real sheets I laid flat between advertisement pages of a magazine, gumming the edges, and stuffed the magazine into my ulster pocket.

“At Holyhead, no matter what I did, I seemed pushed into a carriage with Mrs. Vandemeyer and a nice-looking man and his wife. Just outside London I saw the man hand Mrs. Vandemeyer something—and he winked. ‘I can’t tell you how that wink sort of froze me through and through.’ My only thought was to get out into the corridor as quick as ever I could. I got up, trying to look natural and easy. She cried ‘Now!’—something was flung across my face—and a terrific blow on the head....”

She shuddered, then went on:

“I woke on a dirty bed behind a screen. Mrs. Vandemeyer and a man were talking. They hadn’t found the papers; they were mad. They spoke of—torturing me. I knew I couldn’t stand that. Suddenly I thought of loss of memory. I’d read all about it. I opened my eyes and began babbling in French. Mrs. Vandemeyer came—her face was wicked—but I smiled and asked where I was. The man spoke French too. He scared me worse than she did. He caught my wrist and twisted. I screamed and screamed—but in French. At last he said: ‘That’s not bluff! Anyway, a kid of her age wouldn’t know enough.’ After that, she was sweet as honey. She told me I was her niece, and I was to call her ‘Aunt Rita.’ She showed me the oilskin. I took it, turned it over, said it seemed as if I ought to remember—but couldn’t.

“I was suspicious still, and lay quite quiet for some time. In the end I got up and walked round the room, examining it. I thought that even if anyone was watching me from somewhere, it would seem natural enough under the circumstances. It was a squalid, dirty place. There were no windows. I guessed the door would be locked, but I didn’t try it. On the walls were battered old pictures—scenes from Faust. All of a sudden I sat down on the edge of the table, and put my face in my hands, sobbing out a ‘Mon Dieu! Mon Dieu!’ I’ve got very sharp ears. I distinctly heard the rustle of a dress, and a slight creak. That was enough for me. I was being watched!

“That night, about two, I got up softly. I unhooked one of the pictures—Marguerite with her casket of jewels—damped the brown paper backing, pulled it away, slipped the two stuck-together pages with their precious enclosure between the canvas and the paper, gummed it down again, rehung the picture, and put the magazine back in my pocket. No one would dream it had been tampered with.

“They kept me, questioning for hours together; the strain was awful. They took me back to Ireland over every step, in case I’d hidden the papers en route. I dared appeal to no one. In the end I was sent to a sanatorium at Bournemouth as a special patient. The nurse seemed nice, and I nearly confided in her, but I overheard her in the passage—she was one of them! After that my nerve went. I think I almost hypnotized myself. After a while, I almost forgot that I was really Jane Finn. I was so bent on playing the part of Janet Vandemeyer that my nerves began to play me tricks. I became really ill—for months I sank into a sort of stupor. A sane person shut up in a lunatic asylum often ends by becoming insane, they say. I guess I was like that. I wasn’t even unhappy—just apathetic. The years went on.

“Then suddenly there was a change. Mrs. Vandemeyer came down with a doctor; they questioned me, tried treatments; there was talk of Paris. They didn’t dare risk it. The nurse went instead, and consulted a specialist, pretending to be me. He exposed her loss of memory as fraudulent, but she noted his methods and tried them on me. Perhaps a specialist would have seen through me in a minute; I managed to hold my own.

“One night I was whisked to London—to the house in Soho. Away from the sanatorium I felt different, as though something in me was waking. They sent me in to wait on Mr. Beresford—of course I didn’t know his name then. I was suspicious; I knew there was a small hole high up in the wall. But he looked so honest. On the Sunday a message came; they were disturbed. He was to be killed. I thought I’d have time to snatch the papers—but I was caught. So I screamed that he was escaping, and begged to go back to Marguerite. I shouted the name three times. I knew they’d think I meant Mrs. Vandemeyer, but I hoped it would make Mr. Beresford think of the picture. He’d unhooked one the first day. I was taken away before I could get the papers. They’re still there.”

She sank back, exhausted.

Sir James rose and looked at his watch. “Come,” he said, “we must go at once.”

“To-night?” cried Tuppence.

“To-morrow may be too late,” said Sir James gravely. “Besides, by going to-night we have the chance of capturing that great man and super-criminal—Mr. Brown! You have been followed here—when we leave we shall be followed again, but not molested, for it is Mr. Brown’s plan that we are to lead him. The Soho house is under police supervision night and day. When we enter, Mr. Brown will not draw back—he will risk all on the chance of obtaining the spark to fire his mine. And he fancies the risk not great—since he will enter in the guise of a friend!”

Tuppence flushed, then, impulsively: “There’s something you don’t know—that we haven’t told you.” She hesitated, glanced at Jane. “If I’m wrong—oh, it would be dreadful.”

“You want me to help you out, eh?”

“Yes, please. You know who Mr. Brown is, don’t you?”

“Yes,” said Sir James gravely. “At last I do.”

“At last?” murmured Tuppence. “Oh, but I thought——”

“You thought correctly, Miss Tuppence. I have been morally certain of his identity ever since the night of Mrs. Vandemeyer’s mysterious death.”

“Ah!” breathed Tuppence.

“There are only two solutions. Either the chloral was administered by her own hand—which I reject utterly—or else it was in the brandy you gave her. Only three people touched that brandy—you, I myself, and one other—Mr. Julius Hersheimmer! At first it seemed impossible. As the son of a prominent millionaire, he was well known in America. It seemed impossible that he and Mr. Brown could be one and the same. But you cannot escape from the logic of facts. Remember Mrs. Vandemeyer’s sudden and inexplicable agitation. I gave you a hint. From some words of Mr. Hersheimmer’s at Manchester, I gathered you had understood. Then Mr. Beresford rang me—what I had already suspected—the photograph of Miss Jane Finn had never really been out of Mr. Hersheimmer’s possession——”

Jane sprang to her feet. “What do you mean? What are you trying to suggest? That Mr. Brown is Julius? Julius—my own cousin!”

“No, Miss Finn,” said Sir James unexpectedly. “Not your cousin. The man who calls himself Julius Hersheimmer is no relation to you whatsoever.”

Sir James’s words came like a bomb-shell. He set a clipped paragraph before Jane, and Tuppence read it over her shoulder: a mysterious man, murdered in New York, his body dressed in shabby clothes, his face disfigured.

“As I was saying to Miss Tuppence,” the lawyer resumed quietly, “I set to work to prove the impossible possible. Julius Hersheimmer set out to discover his cousin. On the eve of his departure he was set upon and murdered. Mr. Brown took his place, sailed for England, and none of the real Julius’s friends saw him first—though indeed it would hardly have mattered if they had, the impersonation was so perfect. Since then he has been hand and glove with those sworn to hunt him down, every secret of theirs known to him. Mrs. Vandemeyer knew his secret; he nearly failed there—only Miss Tuppence’s fortunate change of plan saved him. He took a desperate step, trusting his assumed character. He nearly succeeded—but not quite.”

“I can’t believe it,” murmured Jane. “He seemed so splendid.”

“The real Julius Hersheimmer was a splendid fellow,” returned Sir James. “And Mr. Brown is a consummate actor. But ask Miss Tuppence whether she has had no suspicions.”

Tuppence nodded, unwillingly. “I still don’t understand why, if he’s Mr. Brown, he rescued us.”

“As a last hope,” said Sir James, “Jane Finn was to be allowed to escape—with no suspicion of a put-up job. Young Beresford might be suffered to hover about and, if necessary, communicate with you. Then ‘Julius’ dashes up in melodramatic style—bullets fly, but don’t hit anybody. Afterwards? You drive to Soho and secure the document; if he conducts the search, he ‘finds’ the place already rifled. There are a dozen ways of playing it, but the end is the same. And, I rather fancy, an accident would have happened to both of you. You know an inconvenient amount.”

“Tommy,” said Tuppence softly.

“Yes. When the right moment came to get rid of him, he was too sharp for them. All the same, I’m not easy in my mind about him.”

“Why?”

“Because Julius Hersheimmer is Mr. Brown,” said Sir James dryly. “And it takes more than one man and a revolver to hold up Mr. Brown....”

They could do nothing till they had been to the house in Soho. Sir James slipped a service revolver into his pocket. He would not suggest going without Tuppence; he did suggest that Miss Finn should remain, but Jane shook her head.

“No. Those papers were my trust. I must go through with this to the end.”

They drove to the square. The police were posted, front and back; anyone entering after them would be arrested. A key admitted them. Slowly they mounted the rickety stairs. At the top hung the ragged velvet curtain; Tuppence looked at the tattered velvet with interest. Even now she could almost swear it moved—as though _some one_ was behind it. So strong was the illusion that she almost fancied she could make out the outline of a form.... Supposing Mr. Brown—Julius—was there waiting.... Impossible of course! Yet she almost went back to put the curtain aside and make sure....

Now they were entering the prison room. No place for anyone to hide here, thought Tuppence, with a sigh of relief, then chided herself indignantly. She must not give way to this foolish fancying—this curious insistent feeling that _Mr. Brown was in the house_.... Hark! what was that? A stealthy footstep on the stairs? There _was_ some one in the house! Absurd! She was becoming hysterical.

Jane went straight to the picture of Marguerite. She unhooked it, ripped away the backing. An advertisement sheet fell. Between the frayed inner edges two thin written sheets lay hidden. No dummy—this time the real thing!

“We’ve got it,” breathed Tuppence.

Sir James scrutinized the pages. “Yes,” he said quietly.

“This is the ill-fated draft treaty.” He folded it and slipped it into his pocket-book. Then, looking round the windowless, close-fitting room: “Truly sinister. Whatever took place here would never be heard by the outside world.”

A vague alarm stirred in Tuppence. Suppose some one barred that door? Absurd—the police were all around. She smiled at her foolishness, and looked up to find Sir James’s eyes upon her.

“Quite right, Miss Tuppence. You scent danger. So do I. So does Miss Finn.”

“Yes,” admitted Jane. “It’s absurd—but I can’t help it.”

“You feel—as we all feel— _the presence of Mr. Brown_. Yes”—he checked her involuntary movement—“not a doubt of it— _Mr. Brown is here_....”

“In this house?”

“In this room.... You don’t understand? _I am Mr. Brown_....”

Stupefied, unbelieving, they stared at him. The very lines of his face had changed. It was a different man who stood before them. He smiled a slow, cruel smile.

“Neither of you will leave this room alive! You said just now we had succeeded. I have succeeded. The draft treaty is mine. Shall I tell you how it will be? Sooner or later the police will break in, and they will find three victims of Mr. Brown—three, not two, you understand—but fortunately the third will not be dead, only wounded, and will be able to describe the attack with a wealth of detail! The treaty? It is in the hands of Mr. Brown. No one will think of searching the pockets of Sir James Peel Edgerton!”

He turned to Jane. “You outwitted me. I make my acknowledgments. But you will not do it again.”

There was a faint sound behind him. Intoxicated with success, he did not turn. He slipped his hand into his pocket. “Checkmate to the Young Adventurers,” he said, and slowly raised the big automatic.

As he did so, an iron grip seized him from behind; the revolver was wrenched away.

“I guess you’re caught redhanded with the goods upon you,” said Julius Hersheimmer drawlingly.

Sir James looked from one to the other, longest at Tommy. “You,” he breathed.

“You! I might have known.”

They relaxed their hold a fraction. His left hand—bearing the big signet ring—flashed to his lips.

“‘_Ave, Cæsar! te morituri salutant_,’” he said, still looking at Tommy. Then his face changed; with a long convulsive shudder he crumpled to the floor. An odour of bitter almonds filled the air.

The much-heralded Labour Day had passed like any other day. There had been speeches and processions, but the reign of terror failed to materialize. On Sunday a brief notice recorded the sudden death of Sir James Peel Edgerton, K.C.; Monday’s papers praised his career. The exact manner of his death was never made public.

Tommy had read the situation aright: it had been a one-man show. Deprived of their chief, the organization fell to pieces. Kramenin fled; Astley Priors was abandoned in panic, leaving behind documents that damned the gang. In Soho, from the dead man’s pocket-book, Mr. Carter retrieved the draft treaty; there and then, in the presence of the other three, it was reduced to ashes.

On the evening of the 30th Julius P. Hersheimmer gave a little supper in a private room at the Savoy. “Carte blanche,” he had said—and a millionaire usually gets it. The company was small, the purpose plain: to gather the friends who had brought the thing to a triumphant close. Jane Finn, guest of honour, entered shyly. Tuppence had “toggled her up” royally in a famous dressmaker’s “tiger lily”—golds, reds, and browns, from which rose the pure line of her white throat and the bronze masses of hair crowning the lovely head.

Soon the talk was in full swing, and, with one accord, they demanded a complete explanation.

“You’ve been too darned close,” Julius complained. “The idea of both you and Tuppence casting me for Mr. Brown—say, it tickles me to death!”

“The idea was not original to them,” said Mr. Carter gravely. “It was suggested and the poison instilled by a past-master. The New York paragraph gave him the plan; by means of it he wove a web that nearly enmeshed you fatally.”

Julius nodded. “I never liked him. I always suspected he’d silenced Mrs. Vandemeyer. But not till I heard the order for Tommy’s execution came right on the heels of our Sunday interview with him did I begin to tumble to it.”

“I never suspected at all,” lamented Tuppence. “I thought I was so much cleverer than Tommy—but he’s scored handsomely.”

Tommy flushed and was for silence, but they clamoured.

“There’s nothing to tell,” he began, acutely uncomfortable.

“I was an awful mug—right up to the time I found that photograph of Annette, and realized she was Jane Finn. Then I remembered how persistently she had shouted ‘Marguerite’—and I thought of the pictures. Well, that’s that. Then I went over the whole thing to see where I’d made an ass of myself.

“That business about Mrs. Vandemeyer had worried me when Julius told me. On the face of it, it seemed that he or Sir James must have done the trick. Finding the photograph in the drawer, after that story of how it had been got from him by Inspector Brown, made me suspect Julius. Then I remembered it was Sir James who discovered the false Jane Finn. In the end, I couldn’t make up my mind—and decided to take no chances either way.

“I left a note for Julius, in case he was Mr. Brown, saying I was off to the Argentine, and I dropped Sir James’s letter with the offer of the job by the desk so he’d see it was a genuine stunt. Then I wrote to Mr. Carter and rang up Sir James. Taking him into my confidence would be best either way, so I told him everything—except where I believed the papers to be hidden. The way he helped me get on Tuppence’s and Annette’s track almost disarmed me, but not quite. I kept my mind open between the two. And then I got a bogus note from Tuppence—and I knew!”

“How?” demanded several voices.

Tommy produced the note and passed it round. “It’s her handwriting right enough, but I knew it wasn’t from her because of the signature. She’d never spell her name ‘Twopence.’ Anyone who’d never seen it written might quite easily do so. Julius had seen it—he’d shown me a note of hers once—but Sir James hadn’t. After that, everything was plain sailing. I sent Albert post-haste to Mr. Carter. I pretended to go away, but doubled back. When Julius came bursting up in his car, I felt it wasn’t part of Mr. Brown’s plan, and there’d be trouble. Unless Sir James was actually caught in the act, I knew Mr. Carter would never believe it on my bare word—”

“I didn’t,” interposed Mr. Carter ruefully.

“That’s why I sent the girls to Sir James. I was sure they’d fetch up at the Soho house sooner or later. I threatened Julius with the revolver because I wanted Tuppence to repeat that to Sir James, so he wouldn’t worry about us. The moment the girls were out of sight I told Julius to drive like blazes for London, and as we went I told him the whole story. We got to Soho in plenty of time and met Mr. Carter outside. After arranging things we went in and hid behind the curtain in the recess. The police had orders to say, if asked, that no one had gone in. That’s all.”

“In the end,” said Julius, “you’re all wrong about that photograph. It was taken from me—but I found it again.”

“Where?” cried Tuppence.

“In the little wall safe in Mrs. Vandemeyer’s bedroom.”

“I knew you found something,” said Tuppence reproachfully. “That’s what started me suspecting you. Why didn’t you say?”

“I guess I was a mite suspicious too. It had been got away from me once, and I wasn’t going to let on I’d got it till a photographer had made a dozen copies!”

Mr. Carter took from his pocket a small shabby brown book. “Beresford has just said I would not have believed Sir James guilty unless he was caught in the act. True. Not until I read this could I bring myself to credit the amazing truth. This will pass into Scotland Yard’s possession, but never be exhibited. To you, who know, I will read certain passages.”

He turned the thin pages and read, the dry official voice oddly moving:

“... It is madness to keep this book.... But I have never shrunk from risks. I feel an urgent need for self-expression.... The book will only be taken from my dead body....

“... From an early age I realized that I had exceptional abilities. Only a fool underestimates his capabilities. My brain power was greatly above the average.... My appearance was the only thing against me—quiet, insignificant, utterly nondescript....

“... When I was a boy I heard a famous murder trial.... Then I studied the criminal in the dock.... The man was a fool--he had been incredibly, unbelievably stupid.... I felt an immeasurable contempt for him.... Then it occurred to me that the criminal standard was a low one. Wastrels and failures drift into crime.... Strange that men of brains have never realized its opportunities.... What a magnificent field—what unlimited possibilities!...

“... I read standard works on crime and criminals.... Degeneracy, disease--never the deliberate embracing of a career by a far-seeing man....

“... Supposing I became Prime Minister? What then? Hampered at every turn—fettered by the democratic system! No—the power I dreamed of was absolute! An autocrat! A dictator! Such power can only be obtained by working outside the law. To play on the weaknesses of men and nations—to control a vast organization—and overthrow the existing order, and rule! The thought intoxicated me....

“... I must lead two lives.... I cultivated a personality. If I had chosen to be an actor, I should have been the greatest actor living! No disguises—no false beards! Personality! I put it on like a glove! When I shed it, I was myself, quiet, unobtrusive, a man like every other man. I called myself Mr. Brown. There are hundreds of men called Brown—hundreds looking just like me....

“... I have been reading a life of Napoleon. He and I have much in common....

“... I make a practice of defending criminals. A man should look after his own people....

“... Once or twice I have felt afraid.... ‘A great many men are mad, and no one knows it. They do not know it themselves.’... I did not like his glance....

“... The war disturbs me.... They won the war.... It disturbs me....

“... All goes well. The loss of memory is vexing. It cannot be a fake. No girl could deceive me!...

“... The 29th.... That is very soon....

“... By inducing the girl to come to me of her own accord, I have disarmed her. But she has intuitive flashes that might be dangerous.... I can do nothing with the American. He suspects and dislikes me.... Sometimes I fear I have underestimated the other boy. He is not clever, but it is hard to blind his eyes to facts....”

Mr. Carter shut the book. “A great man,” he said.

“Genius, or insanity—who can say?”

He rose. “I will give you a toast. The Joint Venture that has justified itself by success!” It was drunk with acclamation. “And now—we’ll ask Miss Jane Finn to tell the story that only Miss Tuppence has heard so far. But first—her health: one of the bravest of America’s daughters, to whom is due the thanks of two great countries!”

AND AFTER

“THAT was a mighty good toast, Jane,” said Mr. Hersheimmer, as he and
his cousin were being driven back in the Rolls-Royce to the _Ritz_.

“The one to the joint venture?”

“No--the one to you. There isn’t another girl in the world who could
have carried it through as you did. You were just wonderful!”

Jane shook her head.

“I don’t feel wonderful. At heart I’m just tired and lonesome--and
longing for my own country.”

“That brings me to something I wanted to say. Jane--I want you to
marry me! Don’t get scared and say no at once. You can’t love me right
away, of course, that’s impossible. But I’ve loved you from the very
moment I set eyes on your photo--and now I’ve seen you I’m simply crazy
about you! If you’ll only marry me, I won’t worry you any--you shall
take your own time. Maybe you’ll never come to love me, and if that’s
the case I’ll manage to set you free. But I want the right to look after
you, and take care of you.”

“That’s what I want,” said the girl wistfully. “Some one who’ll be good
to me. Oh, you don’t know how lonesome I feel!”

“Sure thing I do. Then I guess that’s all fixed up, and I’ll see the
archbishop about a special license to-morrow morning.”

“Oh, Julius!”

“Well, I don’t want to hustle you any, Jane. Don’t be scared--I shan’t
expect you to love me all at once.”

But a small hand was slipped into his.

“I love you now, Julius,” said Jane Finn. “I loved you that first moment
in the car when the bullet grazed your cheek....”

Five minutes later Jane murmured softly:

“I don’t know London very well, Julius, but is it such a very long way
from the _Savoy_ to the _Ritz?_”

“It depends how you go,” explained Julius unblushingly. “We’re going by
way of Regent’s Park!”

“Oh, Julius--what will the chauffeur think?”

“At the wages I pay him, he knows better than to do any independent
thinking. Why, Jane, the only reason I had the supper at the _Savoy_ was
so that I could drive you home. I didn’t see how I was ever going to
get hold of you alone. You and Tuppence have been sticking together
like Siamese twins. I guess another day of it would have driven me and
Beresford stark staring mad!”

“Oh. Is he----?”

“Of course he is. Head over ears.”

“I thought so,” said Jane thoughtfully.

“Why?”

“From all the things Tuppence didn’t say!”

“There you have me beat,” said Mr. Hersheimmer. But Jane only laughed.

In the meantime, the Young Adventurers were sitting bolt upright,
very stiff and ill at ease, in a taxi which, with a singular lack of
originality, was also returning to the _Ritz_ via Regent’s Park.

A terrible constraint seemed to have settled down between them. Without
quite knowing what had happened, everything seemed changed. They were
tongue-tied--paralysed. All the old _camaraderie_ was gone.

Tuppence could think of nothing to say.

Tommy was equally afflicted.

They sat very straight and forbore to look at each other.

At last Tuppence made a desperate effort.

“Rather fun, wasn’t it?”

“Rather.”

Another silence.

“I like Julius,” essayed Tuppence again.

Tommy was suddenly galvanized into life.

“You’re not going to marry him, do you hear?” he said dictatorially.

“I
forbid it.”

“Oh!” said Tuppence meekly.

“Absolutely, you understand.”

“He doesn’t want to marry me--he really only asked me out of kindness.”

“That’s not very likely,” scoffed Tommy.

“It’s quite true. He’s head over ears in love with Jane. I expect he’s
proposing to her now.”

“She’ll do for him very nicely,” said Tommy condescendingly.

“Don’t you think she’s the most lovely creature you’ve ever seen?”

“Oh, I dare say.”

“But I suppose you prefer sterling worth,” said Tuppence demurely.

“I--oh, dash it all, Tuppence, you know!”

“I like your uncle, Tommy,” said Tuppence, hastily creating a diversion.
“By the way, what are you going to do, accept Mr. Carter’s offer of
a Government job, or accept Julius’s invitation and take a richly
remunerated post in America?”

“I shall stick to the old ship, I think, though it’s awfully good of
Hersheimmer. But I feel you’d be more at home in London.”

“I don’t see where I come in.”

“I do,” said Tommy positively.

Tuppence stole a glance at him sideways.

“There’s the money, too,” she observed thoughtfully.

“What money?”

“We’re going to get a cheque each. Mr. Carter told me so.”

“Did you ask how much?” inquired Tommy sarcastically.

“Yes,” said Tuppence triumphantly. “But I shan’t tell you.”

“Tuppence, you are the limit!”

“It has been fun, hasn’t it, Tommy? I do hope we shall have lots more
adventures.”

“You’re insatiable, Tuppence. I’ve had quite enough adventures for the
present.”

“Well, shopping is almost as good,” said Tuppence dreamily. “Think of
buying old furniture, and bright carpets, and futurist silk curtains,
and a polished dining-table, and a divan with lots of cushions.”

“Hold hard,” said Tommy. “What’s all this for?”

“Possibly a house--but I think a flat.”

“Whose flat?”

“You think I mind saying it, but I don’t in the least! _Ours_, so
there!”

“You darling!” cried Tommy, his arms tightly round her. “I was
determined to make you say it. I owe you something for the relentless
way you’ve squashed me whenever I’ve tried to be sentimental.”

Tuppence raised her face to his. The taxi proceeded on its course round
the north side of Regent’s Park.

“You haven’t really proposed now,” pointed out Tuppence. “Not what our
grandmothers would call a proposal. But after listening to a rotten one
like Julius’s, I’m inclined to let you off.”

“You won’t be able to get out of marrying me, so don’t you think it.”

“What fun it will be,” responded Tuppence. “Marriage is called all sorts
of things, a haven, and a refuge, and a crowning glory, and a state of
bondage, and lots more. But do you know what I think it is?”

“What?”

“A sport!”

“And a damned good sport too,” said Tommy.

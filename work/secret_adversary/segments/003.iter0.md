MR. WHITTINGTON’S OFFER

Tuppence turned sharply. Whatever retort had trembled on her tongue died, for the man’s appearance did not fit her first and most natural assumption. He said quickly: “I can assure you I mean no disrespect.”

She believed him without liking or trusting him. A big, clean-shaven man with a heavy jowl; small, cunning eyes that shifted under her direct gaze.

“Well, what is it?” she asked.

“I happened to overhear part of your conversation with the young gentleman in Lyons’.”

“Well—what of it?”

“Nothing—except that I think I may be of some use to you.”

“You followed me here?”

“I took that liberty.”

He handed her a card with a bow. She read: “Mr. Edward Whittington.” Below, “Esthonia Glassware Co.” and a city address.

“If you will call upon me to-morrow morning at eleven o’clock, I will lay the details of my proposition before you.”

“At eleven o’clock?” she said doubtfully.

“At eleven o’clock.”

She made up her mind. “Very well. I’ll be there.”

He raised his hat with a flourish and walked away. Tuppence watched him go, then gave a little shake, like a terrier. “The adventures have begun,” she murmured. “What does he want me to do, I wonder? There’s something about you, Mr. Whittington, that I don’t like at all. But, on the other hand, I’m not the least bit afraid of you. And as I’ve said before, and shall doubtless say again, little Tuppence can look after herself, thank you!”

She turned into a post office, pondered a telegraph form, and, spurred by economy, decided to risk ninepence. Disdaining the spiky pen and thick, black treacle, she pulled out Tommy’s pencil and wrote rapidly: “Don’t put in advertisement. Will explain to-morrow.” Addressed to Tommy at his club.

“It may catch him. Anyway, it’s worth trying.” She bought three penny-worth of new buns, and that night in her tiny cubicle munched and reflected. Esthonia Glassware? What earthly need could it have for her? Excitement tingled; the morrow held possibilities. When she slept, she dreamed Mr. Whittington set her to wash Esthonia glass that looked remarkably like hospital plates.

Five minutes to eleven found her at the block of buildings. To arrive before time would look over-eager; on the stroke she plunged in. The Esthonia Glassware Co. was on the top floor; there was a lift, but she walked. Slightly out of breath, she knocked at the ground-glass door.

A middle-aged clerk rose from his high stool. “I have an appointment with Mr. Whittington.”

“Will you come this way, please.” He opened a door marked Private.

Whittington sat behind a large paper-littered desk. Sleek prosperity and a shifty eye—Tuppence’s first judgment held.

“So you’ve turned up all right? That’s good. Sit down, will you?”

She looked particularly small and demure, downcast eyes, while he rustled among papers. Then he leaned forward. “Now, my dear young lady, let us come to business. You want work? Well, I have work to offer you. What should you say now to £100 down, and all expenses paid?” He leaned back, thumbs in his waistcoat.

“And the nature of the work?” said Tuppence warily.

“Nominal—purely nominal. A pleasant trip, that is all.”

“Where to?”

“Paris.”

“Oh!” thought Tuppence. “Of course, if father heard that he would have a fit! But somehow I don’t see Mr. Whittington in the role of the gay deceiver.”

“Yes,” he purred. “To put the clock back a few years—and re-enter one of those charming _pensionnats de jeunes filles_ with which Paris abounds——”

“A _pensionnat?_”

“Exactly. Madame Colombier’s in the Avenue de Neuilly.”

Tuppence knew it well—select, impeccable. She was more puzzled than ever. “You want me to go to Madame Colombier’s? For how long?”

“Possibly three months.”

“And that is all? There are no other conditions?”

“None whatever. You would, of course, go in the character of my ward, and you would hold no communication with your friends. I should have to request absolute secrecy for the time being. By the way, you are English, are you not?”

“Yes.”

“Yet you speak with a slight American accent?”

“My great pal in hospital was a little American girl. I dare say I picked it up. I can soon get out of it again.”

“On the contrary, it might be simpler for you to pass as an American. Details about your past life in England might be more difficult to sustain. Yes, that would be decidedly better. Then——”

“One moment, Mr. Whittington! You seem to be taking my consent for granted.”

“Surely you are not thinking of refusing? Madame Colombier’s is most high-class and orthodox. And the terms are most liberal.”

“Exactly. That’s just it. The terms are almost too liberal, Mr. Whittington. I cannot see any way in which I can be worth that amount of money to you.”

“No?” he said softly. “Well, I will tell you. I could doubtless obtain some one else for very much less. What I am willing to pay for is a young lady with sufficient intelligence and presence of mind to sustain her part well, and also one who will have sufficient discretion not to ask too many questions.”

Tuppence smiled. He had scored. “There’s another thing. So far there has been no mention of Mr. Beresford. Where does he come in?”

“Mr. Beresford?”

“My partner. You saw us together yesterday.”

“Ah, yes. But I’m afraid we shan’t require his services.”

“Then it’s off!” Tuppence rose. “It’s both or neither. Sorry—but that’s how it is. Good morning, Mr. Whittington.”

“Wait a minute. Let us see if something can’t be managed. Sit down again, Miss——”

Tuppence’s conscience gave her a passing twinge as she remembered the archdeacon. She seized on the first name that came. “Jane Finn,” she said hastily—and then paused open-mouthed at the effect of those two simple words.

His geniality vanished. His face went purple; veins stood out. Behind the rage lurked incredulous dismay. He leaned forward and hissed: “So that’s your little game, is it?”

Tuppence, utterly taken aback, kept her head.

“Been playing with me, have you—like a cat and mouse? Knew all the time what I wanted you for, but kept up the comedy. Is that it, eh?” The red ebbed. He eyed her keenly. “Who’s been blabbing? Rita?”

“No,” said Tuppence truthfully. “Rita knows nothing about me.”

“How much do you know?” he shot out.

“Very little indeed,” said Tuppence—and noted his uneasiness grow.

“Anyway, you knew enough to come in here and plump out that name.”

“It might be my own name,” she pointed out.

“It’s likely, isn’t it—two girls with a name like that?”

“Or I might just have hit upon it by chance.”

He brought his fist down. “Quit fooling! How much do you know? And how much do you want?”

The last words took Tuppence’s fancy mightily. She sat up, smiling. “My dear Mr. Whittington, let us by all means lay our cards upon the table. And pray do not be so angry. You heard me say yesterday that I proposed to live by my wits. It seems I have now proved I have some wits to live by! I admit I have knowledge of a certain name, but perhaps my knowledge ends there.”

“Yes—and perhaps it doesn’t. So we come to the usual question—how much?”

“Suppose we say a little something down, and a fuller discussion later?”

“Blackmail, eh?”

“Oh no! Shall we say payment of services in advance? I’m so very fond of money!”

“You’re about the limit,” he growled, half admiring. “Took me in all right. Thought you were a meek little kid with just enough brains for my purpose.” He broke off. “All the same, some one’s been talking. You say it isn’t Rita. Was it——? Oh, come in.”

The clerk entered with a telephone message. Whittington read, frowned. “That’ll do, Brown.” The door shut.

“Come to-morrow at the same time. I’m busy now. Here’s fifty to go on with.” He pushed notes across.

Tuppence counted them, slipped them into her bag, and rose. “Good morning, Mr. Whittington. At least, au revoir, I should say.”

“Exactly. Au revoir!” Almost genial again. “Au revoir, my clever and charming young lady.”

Tuppence sped downstairs, wild elation possessing her. A clock showed five minutes to twelve. “Let’s give Tommy a surprise!” She hailed a taxi.

At the tube station entrance Tommy hurried forward, eyes widening. Tuppence smiled affectionately. “Pay the thing, will you, old bean? I’ve got nothing smaller than a five-pound note!”

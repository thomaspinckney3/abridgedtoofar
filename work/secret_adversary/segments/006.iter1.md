“Well,” said Tuppence, recovering herself, “it really seems as though it were meant to be.”

“I know what you mean,” said Carter. “I’m superstitious myself. Luck, and all that. But I warn you the pace may be forced. When you saw Whittington, they had time before them. I have information that the big coup was planned for early in the new year. The Government contemplates legislative action to deal effectually with the strike menace. They’ll get wind of it, and that may bring things to a head. The less time they have to mature their plans the better. Don’t be cast down if you fail. It’s not an easy proposition.”

Tuppence grew businesslike. “What exactly can we count upon you for, Mr. Carter?”

“Funds within reason, detailed information on any point, and no official recognition. If you get into trouble with the police, I can’t officially help you. You’re on your own.”

“I quite understand. I’ll write out a list of things I want to know. Now—about money—”

“Do you want to say how much?”

“Not exactly. We’ve got plenty to go with for the present, but when we want more—”

“It will be waiting for you.”

“Yes, but—you know one really has the devil of a time getting anything out of it! And if we have to fill up a blue form and send it in, and then, after three months, they send us a green one, and so on—well, that won’t be much use, will it?”

Mr. Carter laughed outright.

“Don’t worry, Miss Tuppence. You will send a personal demand to me here, and the money, in notes, shall be sent by return of post. As to salary—shall we say three hundred a year? And an equal sum for Mr. Beresford.”

Tuppence beamed. “How lovely. You are kind. I do love money! I’ll keep beautiful accounts—debit and credit, and the balance on the right side, and a red line with the totals the same at the bottom. I really know how to do it when I think.”

“I’m sure you do. Good-bye, and good luck.”

A minute later they were on the steps of 27 Carshalton Terrace, heads in a whirl.

“Tommy! Tell me at once—who is ‘Mr. Carter’?” He murmured a name. “Oh!” said Tuppence, impressed. “I like him, don’t you? He looks so awfully tired and bored, and yet you feel that underneath he’s just like steel, all keen and flashing. Oh!” She gave a skip. “Pinch me, Tommy, do pinch me. I can’t believe it’s real!”

Mr. Beresford obliged.

“Ow! That’s enough! Yes, we’re not dreaming. We’ve got a job!”

“The joint venture has really begun,” said Tommy. “Julius P. Hersheimmer!”

“We never told Mr. Carter about hearing from him,” said Tommy. “Come on—we’d better take a taxi.”

“All expenses paid,” said Tuppence luxuriously. “I’m sure blackmailers never arrive in buses.”

“We’ve ceased being blackmailers,” Tommy pointed out.

“I’m not sure I have,” said Tuppence darkly.

Shown at once to Mr. Hersheimmer’s suite, they heard an impatient “Come in.” Julius P. Hersheimmer was younger than they had imagined, squarely built, pugnacious but pleasant, unmistakably American.

“Get my note? Sit down and tell me right away all you know about my cousin.”

“Your cousin?”

“Sure thing. Jane Finn. My father and her mother were brother and sister.”

“Oh!” cried Tuppence. “Then you know where she is?”

“No!” He brought his fist down. “I’m darned if I do! Don’t you?”

“We advertised to receive information, not to give it,” said Tuppence severely.

“I can read,” said Julius dryly. “But I thought maybe it was her back history you were after, and that you’d know where she was now?”

Tommy explained that they were “private inquiry agents,” commissioned to find her, and would be glad of any details.

“Put me wise,” said Julius; and, when Tommy had given him a guarded outline, he nodded. “That’s all right. I was just a mite hasty. London gets my goat! I only know little old New York. Trot out your questions.”

Tuppence plunged: “When did you last see your—cousin?”

“Never seen her,” said Julius. He elaborated with American directness. His father had quarrelled with Aunt Jane when she married a poor school-teacher out West; they had never corresponded. The old man did pile it up. “He went into oil, and he went into steel, and he played a bit with railroads, and I can tell you he made Wall Street sit up!” Then he died, and Julius got the dollars—and a conscience. He hired a man to hunt Aunt Jane down. Result: Aunt and Amos Finn were dead, but there was a daughter—Jane—torpedoed in the Lusitania on her way to Paris. Saved, but not heard of over this side. “I guessed they weren’t hustling any, so I thought I’d come along over, and speed things up. I phoned Scotland Yard and the Admiralty first thing. The Admiralty rather choked me off, but Scotland Yard were very civil—said they would make inquiries, even sent a man round this morning to get her photograph. I’m off to Paris to-morrow, just to see what the Prefecture is doing. I guess if I go to and fro hustling them, they ought to get busy!”

“Then we can work together,” said Tuppence. Lunch was proposed and accepted. Oysters had given place to sole when a card was brought in. “A Scotland Yard inspector again,” grumbled Julius. “Another man this time. What does he expect I can tell him that I didn’t tell the first chap? I hope they haven’t lost that photograph. The Western photographer’s place was burned down—this is the only copy.”

A sudden dread swept over Tuppence. “You—you don’t know the name of the man who came this morning?”

“Yes, I do. No, I don’t—half a second—it was on his card. Oh, I know! Inspector Brown. Quiet, unassuming sort of chap.”

A veil might with profit be drawn over the next half-hour. Suffice it to say that no such person as “Inspector Brown” was known to Scotland Yard, and the photograph of Jane Finn, which would have been of the utmost value, was lost beyond recovery. Once again “Mr. Brown” had triumphed.

The reverse brought Julius Hersheimmer and the Young Adventurers into sudden intimacy. Barriers down, Tommy and Tuppence told him the whole story; Julius declared himself “tickled to death.” He turned to Tuppence at the close of the narration. “I’ve always had a kind of idea that English girls were just a mite moss-grown. Old-fashioned and sweet, you know, but scared to move round without a footman or a maiden aunt. I guess I’m a bit behind the times!”

At Tuppence’s suggestion that they keep in touch with Jane’s only relation, they installed themselves forthwith at the Ritz. “Put like that,” she added to Tommy, “nobody could boggle at the expense!” Nobody did.

“And now—to work!” she announced next morning.

“My union, Tuppence, my union! It does not permit me to work before 11 a.m.”

“Tommy, you’ll have something thrown at you. It is absolutely essential that we should without delay map out a plan of campaign.”

Tommy laid his paper finally aside. “There’s something of the simplicity of the truly great mind about you, Tuppence. Fire ahead. I’m listening.”

“To begin with—what have we to go upon?”

“Absolutely nothing,” said Tommy cheerily.

“Wrong. We have two distinct clues.”

“What are they?”

“First, we know one of the gang.”

“Whittington?”

“Yes. I’d recognize him anywhere.”

“Hum. I don’t call that much of a clue. You don’t know where to look for him, and it’s about a thousand to one against your running against him by accident.”

“I’m not so sure. Once coincidences start happening they go on happening in the most extraordinary way. Still, we can’t rely on that. But there are places in London where simply everyone is bound to turn up sooner or later—Piccadilly Circus, for instance. One of my ideas was to take up my stand there every day with a tray of flags.”

“What about meals?” inquired the practical Tommy.

“How like a man! What does mere food matter?”

“That’s all very well. You’ve just had a thundering good breakfast. No one’s got a better appetite than you have, Tuppence, and by tea-time you’d be eating the flags, pins and all. But honestly, I don’t think much of the idea. Whittington mayn’t be in London at all.”

“That’s true. Anyway, clue No. 2 is more promising. We’ve a Christian name—Rita. Whittington mentioned it that day. Danvers was shadowed on the way over, and it’s more likely to have been a woman, and a good-looking one. Obviously she was saved—if she wasn’t, how would they have known Jane had the papers? Now there’s just a chance that this woman may have been ‘Rita.’ If so, we’ve got to hunt through the survivors of the Lusitania till we find her.”

“Then the first thing is to get a list.”

“I wrote to Mr. Carter. I got his reply this morning, and among other things it encloses the official statement of those saved from the Lusitania.”

“But very few Christian names. Nearly all Mrs. or Miss.”

“That complicates matters.”

“We’ll just get down to it. London first. Note the addresses of any women in or near town while I put on my hat.”

They set out for the first address, The Laurels. At the gate, as Tuppence was about to ring, Tommy checked her. “What are you going to say?”

“What am I going to say? Oh dear, I don’t know. It’s very awkward.”

“I thought as much,” said Tommy with satisfaction. “How like a woman! No foresight! Now just stand aside, and see how easily the mere male deals with the situation.” He pressed the bell, produced notebook and pencil, and, as a slatternly servant appeared, rattled off his glib formula from the “Borough Council” about the new voting register; “Mrs. Edgar Keith—Christian name?” “Eleanor Jane.” “Thank you.” He rejoined Tuppence.

“I don’t mind admitting that for once you’ve scored handsomely. I should never have thought of that.”

They repeated the wheeze ad lib; a couple of fruitless calls later—Tommy’s impersonation had supplied many a Christian name and slammed many a door—they lunched briefly, and Tuppence read from the notebook: “Mrs. Vandemeyer, 20 South Audley Mansions.”

“Then the Mayfair lady is clearly indicated,” said Tommy. “And stifle this longing for vulgar sensation, Tuppence. It’s a wonder Mr. Brown has not ere now done us to death.”

“Still, it is queer he hasn’t wreaked vengeance upon us. We pass on our way unscathed,” she returned, and rose.

South Audley Mansions rose imposing just off Park Lane. No. 20’s door was opened by an elderly woman of the housekeeper type. Tommy delivered his glib formula.

“Christian name?”

“Margaret.”

Tommy spelt it; the woman corrected him: “No—g u e.”

“Oh, Marguerite—the French way. We had her down as ‘Rita’ Vandemeyer—”

“She’s mostly called that, sir, but Marguerite’s her name.”

“Thank you. That’s all.”

Hardly containing himself, Tommy rejoined Tuppence. “You heard?”

“Yes. Oh, Tommy!”

“I know, old thing.”

“It’s—it’s so lovely to think of things—and then for them really to happen!” cried Tuppence enthusiastically.

Her hand still in his, they reached the entrance hall. Voices and footsteps sounded from above. Suddenly Tuppence dragged him into the shadow by the lift.

“Hush!”

Two men descended and passed out. Tuppence’s fingers tightened. “Quick—follow them. I daren’t. He might recognize me. I don’t know who the other man is, but the bigger of the two was Whittington.”

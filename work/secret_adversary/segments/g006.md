## Chapter 1

A week of fruitless searching around the empty Moat House — Tuppence's brooch found among the leaves the only trace — convinces Tommy and Julius that the telegram was a plant and that they must turn to the professionals; Julius resolves to go to Scotland Yard, while Tommy stays behind in case anything breaks.

It was Sir James Peel Edgerton, however, who reached them first, with a wire summoning them both to Manchester. Julius had got in half an hour earlier and led the way out of the station as Tommy arrived.

"He's booked rooms for us, and will be round to dine at eight."

"What made you think he'd ceased to take any interest in the case?" Tommy asked curiously.

"What he said," Julius replied dryly. "The old bird's as close as an oyster. Wouldn't commit himself till he was sure he could deliver the goods."

Tommy shook his head, unconvinced.

Sir James arrived punctually. "I have heard so much about you from Miss Tuppence," he said, shaking Tommy's hand warmly, "that it really seems as though I already know you quite well."

Tommy felt at once the magnetism of the lawyer's personality. He was reminded of Mr. Carter — the same keen-edged quality of mind beneath the professional reserve. He was conscious, too, of being read through and through, while learning nothing in return. Sir James took in everything, but gave out only what he chose.

Julius broke into eager questions. How had Sir James tracked the girl? Why had he kept silent?

The lawyer stroked his chin and smiled. "Just so, just so. Well, she's found. That's the great thing, isn't it?"

"Sure it is. But just how did you strike her trail? We thought you'd quit for good."

"Did you really? H'm, dear me." He shot a lightning glance. "Well, I don't know that I should go so far as to say that."

"But where is she?"

"The young lady was knocked down in a street accident, and has sustained slight injuries to the head. She was taken to the infirmary, and on recovering consciousness gave her name as Jane Finn. I arranged for her to be removed to the house of a friend, and wired at once for you. She has not spoken since."

"Her memory's come back?" cried Julius.

"Undoubtedly, since she was able to give her real name."

"And you just happened to be on the spot," said Tommy. "Seems quite like a fairy tale."

"Coincidences are curious things," said Sir James dryly.

But Tommy was now certain of what he had only suspected. Sir James had not abandoned the case at all; by some means of his own, he had run the missing girl to earth.

"After dinner," Julius announced, "I'll go right away and see Jane."

"Quite impossible. They would not allow visitors at this hour. To-morrow morning at ten."

"All the same, I reckon I'll go round to-night and ginger them up."

"It will be quite useless, Mr. Hersheimmer."

The words came out like the crack of a pistol. For a moment hostility flared between the two men; then Julius lowered his eyes. "For the moment, I reckon you're the boss."

Sir James turned smoothly to Tommy. He had heard, he said, that his friends had been anxious. Helped by questions, Tommy gave an abbreviated account of his adventures.

"You got yourself out of a tight place very well," Sir James said gravely. "I congratulate you."

"I couldn't have got away but for the girl, sir."

"It was lucky for you she happened to take a fancy to you. There's no doubt about her being one of the gang?"

"I'm afraid not. She went back to them when she could have got away."

Sir James nodded. "Mrs. Vandemeyer always signed herself Rita. Curious that the girl was crying out to her at the moment Vandemeyer was either dead or dying. There are one or two points that strike me as obscure. The house was raided?"

"They'd all cleared out. Not a clue left behind."

"I wonder——" The lawyer tapped the table. "And since then? What have you been doing?"

For a moment Tommy stared. Then it dawned on him — Sir James didn't know.

"I forgot you didn't know about Tuppence."

The lawyer laid down his knife and fork sharply. "Has anything happened to Miss Tuppence?"

"She's disappeared," said Julius. "A week ago."

Between them they gave the history of the futile search. Sir James went at once to the root of the matter.

"A wire signed with your name? Their kidnapping of Miss Tuppence is the counter-move to your escape. They could seal your lips with a threat of what might happen to her."

"That's just what I thought, sir."

"Not bad. The curious thing is that they certainly did not know anything about you when they first held you prisoner. Some one put them wise — and not earlier than Sunday afternoon."

"That almighty omniscient Mr. Brown, of course!" There was a note of derision in Julius's voice.

"You don't believe in Mr. Brown?"

"No, sir. I reckon he's a figurehead — a bogy name to frighten the children. The real head is that Russian, Kramenin."

"I disagree. Mr. Brown exists." Sir James turned to Tommy. "We'll deal with Miss Jane Finn first. Afterwards, we'll set to work to rescue Miss Tuppence. She's in no immediate danger so long as they don't know we've got Jane Finn, and that her memory has returned. We must keep that dark at all costs."

At ten the next morning they were shown upstairs. Tommy's heart beat sensibly faster. Jane Finn at last! The long-sought, the elusive Jane Finn — the girl who held the future of England in her hands. If only Tuppence could have been at his side.

On the white bed, bandages round her head, lay the girl. The whole scene seemed unreal — so exactly what one expected that it gave the effect of being beautifully staged.

"Miss Finn," said Sir James, "this is your cousin, Mr. Julius P. Hersheimmer."

A faint flush flitted over the girl's face. "Are you really Uncle Hiram's son?"

Her voice, with its slight Western warmth, had an almost thrilling quality. It seemed vaguely familiar to Tommy, but he thrust the impression aside as impossible.

"Sure thing," said Julius, with a tremor in his voice.

"They've been telling me dreadful things — that my memory went, and that there are years lost out of my life. It seems no time since we were being hustled into those boats."

"Don't worry. Now, Jane — there was a man on that boat with some mighty important papers. The big guns in this country have a notion he passed them to you."

"Yes. He said they'd have a better chance with me, because they would save the women and children first. He said they might make all the difference to the Allies. Then you can hand them over right away?"

"But I haven't got them. I hid them. I got uneasy. People seemed to be watching me."

"Go on," said Sir James in his quiet penetrating tones. "What do you remember?"

"It was at Holyhead. In the confusion on the quay I slipped away. I took a car, told the man to drive me out of the town. The path led to the cliff, and down to the sea between big yellow gorse bushes — they were like golden flames. Just level with my head was a hole in the rock — small, but it went a long way back. I took the oilskin packet from round my neck and shoved it right in. Then I tore off a bit of gorse and plugged the hole. I marked the place in my own mind. There was a queer boulder in the path just there — for all the world like a dog sitting up begging. I went back and caught the train. By and by, I saw the man opposite me wink at the woman next to me, and was glad the papers were safe. I went out in the corridor. The woman called me back, said I'd dropped something, and when I stooped, something seemed to hit me — here." She placed her hand to the back of her head. "I don't remember anything more until I woke up in the hospital."

"Thank you, Miss Finn," said Sir James.

Julius took her hand. "So long, Cousin Jane. I'm going to get busy after those papers."

## Chapter 2

In the street they held an informal council. Sir James drew out his watch.

"The boat train to Holyhead stops at Chester at 12.14. If you start at once you can catch the connection. I am due to speak at a meeting at two o'clock — unfortunate."

"Nothing complicated about this deal," Julius remarked. "Just a game of hide-and-seek."

"You are still young, Mr. Hersheimmer. At my age you will probably have learnt one lesson. 'Never underestimate your adversary.'"

"You think Mr. Brown might come along? I'm ready for him." Julius slapped his pocket and produced a murderous-looking automatic. "Little Willie travels round with me everywhere. But there's nobody to put Mr. Brown wise."

"There was nobody to put Mr. Brown wise to the fact that Mrs. Vandemeyer meant to betray him. Nevertheless, *Mrs. Vandemeyer died without speaking*."

Julius was silenced.

"Take no unnecessary risks once the papers are in your hands. If there is any reason to believe you have been shadowed, destroy them at once. The game is in your hands now."

For a long time on the train neither spoke. When at length Julius broke the silence, it was with a totally unexpected remark.

"Say — did you ever make a darned fool of yourself over a girl's face?"

"Can't say I have. Why?"

"Because for the last two months I've been making a sentimental idiot of myself over Jane. First moment I clapped eyes on her photograph my heart did all the usual stunts. I came over here determined to find her and take her back as Mrs. Julius P. Hersheimmer."

"Oh!" said Tommy, amazed.

"Just shows what an almighty fool a man can make of himself. One look at the girl in the flesh, and I was cured. No disparagement to Jane, mind you. She's a real nice girl. But she's not like her photo one bit."

"I thought her a very good-looking girl."

"Sure she is. But there was something about that photo——" Julius shook his head. "I guess romance is a mighty queer thing."

"It must be," said Tommy coldly, "if you can come over here in love with one girl, and propose to another within a fortnight."

Julius looked discomposed. "Well, I'd got a tired feeling I'd never find Jane. The French set about marriage in a businesslike way——"

"If you ask me," said Tommy, "we're all too damned businesslike nowadays."

"Cool down, son."

Tommy had cooled by the time they reached Holyhead. They hired a taxi and drove out toward Treaddur Bay, watching narrowly for the path. When Tommy spotted it, he stopped the car promptly, asked casually if it led down to the sea, and paid off the man. They watched the taxi out of sight, then turned to the narrow path.

"It's the right one, I suppose?"

"Sure it is. Look at the gorse."

The hedges of golden blossom bordered the path on either side. They went down in single file, Julius leading. Twice Tommy turned uneasily.

"What is it?"

"I've got the wind up. Keep fancying there's some one following us."

"Can't be. We'd see him."

The path ran along the side of the cliff, parallel to the sea. Suddenly Julius came to such an abrupt halt that Tommy cannoned into him. Standing out half obstructing the path was a huge boulder bearing a fanciful resemblance to a "begging" terrier.

"It's what we expected to see, isn't it?" said Tommy.

"Sure we expected it — but it kind of rattles me to see it sitting there just where we expected!"

Tommy pointed with a shaking hand. "What about that crevice there?"

"That's it — for sure."

"Damn it!" Tommy cried. "Five years! Bird's-nesting boys, picnic parties, thousands of people passing! It can't be there!" The thing was too easy, therefore it could not be.

"I guess you're rattled now all right," Julius drawled. "Well, here goes!" He thrust his hand into the crevice. "Tight fit. Don't feel anything — no — say, what's this? Gee whiz!" And with a flourish he waved aloft a small discoloured packet sewn up in oilskin. "Hold it while I get my penknife."

Tommy held the precious packet tenderly. They had succeeded.

"It's queer," he murmured. "You'd think the stitches would have rotted. They look just as good as new."

They cut them and ripped away the oilskin. Inside was a small folded sheet of paper. With trembling fingers they unfolded it.

The sheet was blank.

"A dummy?" hazarded Julius.

Tommy's face cleared. "I've got it! *Sympathetic ink!* Heat usually does the trick. Get some sticks."

In a few minutes a little fire of twigs was blazing. Tommy held the sheet near the glow. The paper curled. Then Julius grasped his arm and pointed to where characters were appearing in faint brown.

"Gee whiz! You've got it!"

Tommy held the paper longer until he judged the heat had done its work. Then he uttered a cry.

Across the sheet in neat brown printing ran the words: WITH THE COMPLIMENTS OF MR. BROWN.

## Chapter 3

For a moment they stood staring at each other, dazed.

"How in tarnation did he get ahead of us?" Julius ended.

"It accounts for the stitches being new. We might have guessed."

"It's downright impossible for anyone to get here quicker than we did. And how did he know? Was there a dictaphone in Jane's room?"

"No one could have known beforehand that she'd be in that house — much less that particular room."

"Then one of the nurses listened at the door. The game's up. We've failed."

"There's only one thing for me to do," Tommy said. "Get back to London. Mr. Carter must be warned."

Half an hour after his arrival, haggard and pale, Tommy stood before his chief.

"I've come to report. I've failed."

"You mean the treaty——"

"Is in the hands of Mr. Brown, sir."

"Ah!" said Mr. Carter quietly. The expression on his face did not change, but Tommy caught the flicker of despair in his eyes.

"Don't take it to heart, lad. You did your best. You were up against one of the biggest brains of the century, and you came very near success."

"Thank you, sir."

"I have been blaming myself ever since I heard this other news."

A new fear gripped Tommy's heart. "Is there — something more, sir?"

Mr. Carter stretched out his hand to a sheet on the table. "Read for yourself."

The typewritten words danced before Tommy's eyes — the description of a green toque, a coat with a handkerchief in the pocket marked P.L.C.

"Tuppence——?"

"Washed up on the Yorkshire coast. I'm afraid — it looks very much like foul play."

"My God!" gasped Tommy. "*Tuppence!* Those devils — I'll never rest till I've got even with them!"

"I know what you feel, my poor boy. But it's no good. Cut your losses. Time's merciful. You'll forget."

"Forget Tuppence? Never!"

"That brave little girl. I'm confoundedly sorry."

Back at the *Ritz*, Tommy packed mechanically. *Tuppence — dead!* Little Tuppence, brimming over with life. It was a dream — it couldn't be true.

A note came: a few kind words of sympathy from Peel Edgerton, who had read the news. The letter ended with the offer of a post on a ranch in the Argentine.

The door opened, and Julius burst in with his usual violence, an open paper in his hand.

"Say — they seem to have got some fool idea about Tuppence."

"It's true."

"You mean they've done her in?"

Tommy nodded. "When they got the treaty she wasn't any good to them, and they were afraid to let her go."

"Well, I'm darned! Little Tuppence. She sure was the pluckiest——"

Something cracked in Tommy's brain. He rose to his feet.

"Oh, get out! You don't really care, damn you! You asked her to marry you in your rotten cold-blooded way, but I *loved* her. I'd have given the soul out of my body to save her from harm. I'd have stood by without a word and let her marry you, because you could have given her the sort of time she ought to have had, and I was only a poor devil without a penny. But it wouldn't have been because I didn't care!"

"See here——" Julius began.

"Oh, go to the devil! Tuppence is my girl! I've always loved her, from the time we played together as kids. I shall never forget when I was in hospital, and she came in in that ridiculous cap and apron — like a miracle to see the girl I loved turn up in a nurse's kit——"

"A nurse's kit!" Julius shouted. "Gee whiz! I could swear I've seen Jane in a nurse's cap too. By gum, I've got it! It was her I saw talking to Whittington at that nursing home in Bournemouth. She wasn't a patient there! She was a nurse!"

"I dare say she's been in with them from the start. I shouldn't wonder if she stole those papers from Danvers to begin with."

"I'm darned if she did! She's my cousin, and as patriotic a girl as ever stepped."

"I don't care a damn what she is — get out of here!"

The young men were on the point of blows. Then, with magical abruptness, Julius's anger abated.

"All right, son. I'm going. It's mighty lucky you did say it. I've been the most almighty blithering darned idiot. I'm going right away — to the London and North Western Railway depot, if you want to know."

"I don't care a damn where you're going."

When the door closed, Tommy returned to his suit-case. He re-read Sir James's letter and shook his head. Tuppence must be avenged.

"Better answer it." He went to the writing-table — innumerable envelopes and no paper. He remembered there was a supply in Julius's sitting-room. Julius had announced his immediate departure; there was no fear of running into him. He was beginning to be ashamed of what he had said. He'd apologize if he found him there.

But the room was deserted. Tommy walked to the writing-table and opened the middle drawer. A photograph, carelessly thrust in face upwards, caught his eye. For a moment he stood rooted to the ground. Then he took it out, walked slowly to an arm-chair, and sat down still staring at the photograph in his hand.

What on earth was a photograph of the French girl Annette doing in Julius Hersheimmer's writing-table?

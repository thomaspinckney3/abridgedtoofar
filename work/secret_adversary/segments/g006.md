## Chapter 1

“My train got in half an hour ago,” said Julius as they left the station. “I reckoned you’d come by this before I left London, and wired accordingly to Sir James. He’s booked rooms for us, and will be round to dine at eight.”

“What made you think he’d ceased to take any interest in the case?” asked Tommy.

“What he said,” replied Julius dryly. “The old bird’s as close as an oyster!”

Tommy shook his head, unconvinced.

Sir James arrived punctually. Julius introduced Tommy, and the great lawyer’s handclasp was warm.

“I am delighted to make your acquaintance, Mr. Beresford. I have heard so much about you from Miss Tuppence that it really seems as though I already know you quite well.”

Tommy, scanning him, felt again that keen, sheathed rapier-mind he had noted in Mr. Carter. Sir James’s scrutiny, swift and complete, gave nothing back.

Julius broke out at once: how had he tracked the girl, why the secrecy, why let them think he’d quit? Sir James stroked his chin, smiling.

“Just so, just so. Well, she’s found. And that’s the great thing, isn’t it?”

“But where is she?” demanded Julius. “I thought you’d bring her along.”

“That would hardly be possible,” said Sir James gravely. “The young lady was knocked down in a street accident and sustained slight injuries to the head. She was taken to the infirmary, and on recovering consciousness gave her name as Jane Finn. When—ah!—I heard that, I arranged for her to be removed to the house of a doctor—a friend of mine—and wired at once for you. She relapsed into unconsciousness and has not spoken since.”

“She’s not seriously hurt?”

“Oh, a bruise and a cut or two; really, absurdly slight injuries to have produced such a condition. Her state is probably to be attributed to the mental shock consequent on recovering her memory.”

“It’s come back?” cried Julius.

“Undoubtedly, since she was able to give her real name.”

“After dinner,” Julius announced, “I shall go right away and see Jane.”

“That will be impossible, I fear. It is very unlikely they would allow her to see visitors at this time of night. I should suggest to-morrow morning about ten o’clock.”

For a moment the two masterful natures clashed; then Julius gave way. Sir James turned to Tommy.

“The last I heard of you was that your friends were in grave anxiety on your behalf. Miss Tuppence was inclined to think you had got into difficulties.”

“I had, sir,” said Tommy cheerfully; and, helped by Sir James’s questions, he sketched the tale of his capture and escape. The lawyer’s interest sharpened at the mention of the girl who had aided him.

“There’s no doubt about her being one of the gang, I suppose?”

“I’m afraid not, sir. I thought perhaps they were keeping her by force, but the way she acted didn’t fit. She went back when she could have got away. She kept crying out to be taken to Marguerite.”

“She always signed herself Rita Vandemeyer,” mused Sir James. “Still, the girl may have used her full name. Curious that, at the very moment she cried out, Mrs. Vandemeyer was either dead or dying! There are one or two points that strike me as obscure—their sudden change of attitude towards yourself, for instance. By the way, the house was raided?”

“Yes, sir, but they’d all cleared out. Not a clue left.”

“I wonder——” He tapped the table. “I wish I had been there to go over the house.”

He paused. “And since then? What have you been doing?”

“I forgot that you didn’t know about Tuppence,” said Tommy slowly; and the sickening anxiety swept over him anew.

Sir James laid down his knife and fork sharply. “Has anything happened to Miss Tuppence?”

“She’s disappeared,” said Julius. Swiftly, between them, they gave the history of the week.

“A wire signed with your name?” Sir James’s questions were like pistol-shots. “They knew enough of you both for that. They weren’t sure how much you had learnt. Their kidnapping of Miss Tuppence is the counter-move to your escape. If necessary they could seal your lips with a threat of what might happen to her.”

“That’s just what I thought, sir,” said Tommy.

“You had worked that out? Not bad—no, not at all bad. The curious thing is they certainly did not know anything about you at first. You are sure you did not disclose your identity?”

Tommy shook his head.

“That’s so,” said Julius. “Therefore some one put them wise—and not earlier than Sunday afternoon.”

“Yes, but who?”

“That almighty omniscient Mr. Brown, of course!”

Sir James looked up sharply. “You don’t believe in Mr. Brown, Mr. Hersheimmer?”

“No, sir, I do not. I reckon he’s a figurehead—a bogy name to frighten the children with. The real head is that Russian chap Kramenin. Whittington’s probably head of the English branch.”

“I disagree with you,” said Sir James shortly. “Mr. Brown exists.” He turned to Tommy. “Did you notice where that wire was handed in?”

“No, sir.”

“H’m. Got it with you?”

“It’s upstairs in my kit.”

“I’d like to have a look at it some time. You’ve wasted a week—” Tommy hung his head—“a day or so more is immaterial. We’ll deal with Miss Jane Finn first. Afterwards we’ll set to work to rescue Miss Tuppence from bondage. I don’t think she’s in any immediate danger. That is, so long as they don’t know that we’ve got Jane Finn, and that her memory has returned. We must keep that dark at all costs. You understand?”

They assented. Arrangements were made for the morning. At ten o’clock they met Sir James on the doorstep and were introduced to the doctor.

“How’s the patient?”

“Going on well,” said the doctor. “Evidently no idea of the flight of time. Asked this morning how many had been saved from the Lusitania.”

“May we go up?”

“Certainly.”

Tommy’s heart beat faster. Jane Finn at last—the long-sought girl with England’s future in her hands! He thrust the thought of Tuppence aside and followed upstairs.

On the white bed, bandages round her head, lay the girl. The scene seemed unreal, beautifully staged. She looked at them with large wondering eyes. Sir James spoke:

“Miss Finn, this is your cousin, Mr. Julius P. Hersheimmer.”

A faint flush rose as Julius took her hand. “How do, Cousin Jane?”

“Are you really Uncle Hiram’s son?” Her low, soft voice, with its Western warmth, seemed vaguely familiar to Tommy, but he thrust the impression aside.

“Sure thing.”

“We used to read about Uncle Hiram in the papers,” she went on. “But I never thought I’d meet you. Mother figured it out that Uncle Hiram would never get over being mad with her.”

“The old man was like that,” admitted Julius. “But I guess the new generation’s sort of different. First thing I thought about, soon as the war was over, was to come along and hunt you up.”

“They’ve been telling me dreadful things—that my memory went, and that there are years I shall never know.”

“You didn’t realize that yourself?”

“Why, no. It seems to me as though it were no time since we were being hustled into those boats. I can see it all now.” She closed her eyes with a shudder.

Julius glanced at Sir James, who nodded him on.

“Now, see here, Jane, there’s something we want to know. There was a man aboard that boat with some mighty important papers on him, and the big guns here have got a notion he passed the goods to you. Is that so?”

She hesitated, glancing at the others.

“Mr. Beresford is commissioned by the British Government to get those papers back,” said Julius promptly. “Sir James Peel Edgerton is an English Member of Parliament, and it’s owing to him we’ve ferreted you out. So you can go right ahead. Did Danvers give you the papers?”

“Yes. He said they’d have a better chance with me, because they would save the women and children first.”

“Just as we thought,” said Sir James.

“He said they were very important—that they might make all the difference to the Allies. But, if it’s all so long ago, and the war’s over, what does it matter now?”

“I guess history repeats itself, Jane. First there was a great hue and cry; then it all died down; and now the whole caboodle’s started over again—for rather different reasons. Then you can hand them over to us right away?”

“But I can’t.”

“What?”

“I haven’t got them.”

“You—haven’t—got them?” Julius punctuated the words.

“No—I hid them.”

“You hid them?”

“Yes. I got uneasy. People seemed to be watching me. It scared me—badly.” She pressed her hand to her head. “It’s almost the last thing I remember before waking up in the hospital....”

“Go on,” said Sir James quietly. “What do you remember?”

“It was at Holyhead. In the confusion on the quay I slipped away. I took a car and told the man to drive me out of the town. When we got on the open road I saw a path at the side. I told the man to wait. The path led to the cliff, and down to the sea between big yellow gorse bushes—they were like golden flames. I looked round. There wasn’t a soul in sight. But just level with my head there was a hole in the rock. It was quite small—I could only just get my hand in, but it went a long way back. I took the oilskin packet from round my neck and shoved it right in as far as I could. Then I tore off a bit of gorse—My! but it did prick—and plugged the hole so you’d never guess there was a crevice. Then I marked the place carefully in my own mind. There was a queer boulder in the path there—like a dog sitting up begging. Then I went back. I caught the train. I was a bit ashamed of myself for fancying things maybe, but by and by I saw the man opposite wink at a woman next to me, and I felt scared again, and was glad the papers were safe. I went out into the corridor to get a little air. The woman called me back, said I’d dropped something, and when I stooped, something seemed to hit me—here.” She touched the back of her head. “I don’t remember anything more until I woke up in the hospital.”

“Thank you, Miss Finn,” said Sir James. “I hope we have not tired you?”

“Oh, that’s all right. My head aches a little, but otherwise I feel fine.”

Julius took her hand again. “So long, Cousin Jane. I’m going to get busy after those papers, but I’ll be back in two shakes of a dog’s tail, and I’ll tote you up to London and give you the time of your young life before we go back to the States! I mean it—so hurry up and get well.”

## Chapter 2

In the street they held an informal council. Sir James consulted his watch. “The boat train to Holyhead stops at Chester at 12.14. If you start at once you can catch the connection.”

Tommy looked up, puzzled. “Is there any need to hurry, sir? To-day is only the 24th.”

“I guess it’s always well to get up early in the morning,” said Julius.

Sir James frowned. “I wish I could come. I am due to speak at two o’clock. It is unfortunate.” He added gravely: “You are still young, Mr. Hersheimmer. At my age you will probably have learnt one lesson. ‘Never underestimate your adversary.’”

Julius patted his pocket. “You think Mr. Brown might come along and take a hand? If he does, I’m ready for him. Little Willie here travels round with me everywhere.” He flashed a murderous-looking automatic. “But he won’t be needed this trip. There’s nobody to put Mr. Brown wise.”

“There was nobody to put Mr. Brown wise to the fact that Mrs. Vandemeyer meant to betray him,” said Sir James dryly. “Nevertheless, Mrs. Vandemeyer died without speaking.” He held out his hand. “Good-bye, and good luck. Take no unnecessary risks once the papers are in your hands. If there is any reason to believe that you have been shadowed, destroy them at once. The game is in your hands now.”

Ten minutes later they were in a first-class carriage en route for Chester. Silence held them for some time; then Julius said unexpectedly:

“Say, did you ever make a darned fool of yourself over a girl’s face?”

“Can’t say I have,” said Tommy. “Why?”

“Because for the last two months I’ve been making a sentimental idiot of myself over Jane! First moment I clapped eyes on her photograph my heart did all the usual stunts you read about in novels. I came over here determined to find her and fix it all up, and take her back as Mrs. Julius P. Hersheimmer!”

“Oh!” said Tommy, amazed.

“One look at the girl in the flesh, and I was cured! No disparagement to Jane. She’s a real nice girl, and some fellow will fall in love with her right away. But there was something about that photo—” He shook his head and sighed. “I guess romance is a mighty queer thing!”

“It must be,” said Tommy coldly, “if you can come over here in love with one girl, and propose to another within a fortnight.”

Julius coloured. “Well, you see, I’d got kind of tired thinking I’d never find Jane—and then—oh, well, the French are much more sensible. They keep romance and marriage apart——”

“If you ask me,” said Tommy hotly, “we’re all too damned businesslike nowadays.”

“Cool down, son,” said Julius; and prudently said no more.

By Holyhead the cheerful grin had returned to Tommy’s face. With a road map and brief consultation they hired a taxi and drove out towards Treaddur Bay. They made the man go slowly and watched for the path. Not long after leaving the town Tommy stopped the car, asked carelessly if the path led to the sea, and, hearing it did, paid off the driver.

They turned to the narrow path between swelling hedges of golden gorse. Julius led; twice Tommy looked back uneasily.

“What is it?” asked Julius.

“I don’t know. I keep fancying there’s some one following us.”

“Can’t be. We’d see him.”

Nevertheless Tommy’s sense of the enemy’s omniscience deepened.

“I rather wish that fellow would come along,” said Julius. He patted his pocket. “Little William here is just aching for exercise!”

“Do you always carry it—him—with you?” asked Tommy, impressed.

“Most always. You never know what might turn up.”

The path ran along the side of the cliff. Suddenly Julius halted so abruptly that Tommy cannoned into him.

“What’s up?”

“Look there. If that doesn’t beat the band!”

Half obstructing the path stood a huge boulder, fancifully like a begging terrier.

“Well,” said Tommy, refusing to be thrilled, “it’s what we expected to see.”

“Sure we expected it,” said Julius sadly. “But it kind of rattles me all the same to see it sitting there just where we expected to find it!”

“Push on. What about the hole?”

They scanned the cliff. “The gorse won’t be there after all these years,” said Tommy idiotically.

“I guess you’re right,” said Julius solemnly.

Tommy pointed with a shaking hand. “What about that crevice there?”

“That’s it—for sure,” breathed Julius.

“When I was in France,” said Tommy, “whenever my batman failed to call me, he said he had come over queer. I never believed it. But whether he felt it or not, there is such a sensation. I’ve got it now! Badly!” He glared at the rock. “Damn it! It’s impossible! Five years! Bird’s-nesting boys, picnic parties, thousands of people passing! It can’t be there! It’s a hundred to one against it!”

Julius’s smile widened. “I guess you’re rattled now all right. Well, here goes!” He thrust his hand into the crevice, grimaced. “It’s a tight fit. Jane’s hand must be smaller. I don’t feel anything—no—say, what’s this? Gee whiz!” He waved aloft a small discoloured packet. “It’s the goods all right. Sewn up in oilskin. Hold it while I get my penknife.”

The unbelievable had happened. Tommy held the packet tenderly. “It’s queer,” he murmured. “You’d think the stitches would have rotted. They look just as good as new.”

They cut the stitches, ripped away the oilskin. Inside was a small folded sheet. They unfolded it with trembling fingers. The sheet was blank.

“A dummy?” hazarded Julius. “Was Danvers just a decoy?”

Tommy’s face cleared. “I’ve got it! Sympathetic ink!”

“Worth trying. Heat usually does the trick.”

They kindled a little fire of twigs and leaves. Tommy held the paper near the glow. The paper curled; nothing more. Suddenly Julius gripped his arm. Faint brown characters were appearing.

“Gee whiz! You’ve got it! Say, that idea of yours was great.”

Tommy held steady until the heat had done its work, then withdrew it—only to utter a cry.

Across the sheet, in neat brown printing, ran the words: WITH THE COMPLIMENTS OF MR. BROWN.

## Chapter 3

They stared at each other stupidly. Somehow, inexplicably, Mr. Brown had forestalled them.

“It accounts for the stitches being new,” said Tommy dully. “We might have guessed....”

“Never mind the darned stitches,” cried Julius. “How did he get ahead of us? We hustled all we knew. It’s downright impossible. And how did he know? Was there a dictaphone in Jane’s room? Then one of the nurses was a crook and listened at the door. How’s that?”

“No one could have known beforehand that she was going to be in that house—much less that room,” objected Tommy. “He may have found out months ago and removed the papers then—No, that won’t wash! They’d have been published at once.”

“Sure thing they would! No, some one’s got ahead of us to-day by an hour or so. But how they did it gets my goat.”

“I wish that chap Peel Edgerton had been with us,” said Tommy.

“Why? The mischief was done when we came.”

Tommy could not explain the illogical sense that Sir James’s presence would somehow have averted the catastrophe. He pulled himself together. “It’s no good arguing. The game’s up. There’s only one thing for me to do: get back to London and warn Mr. Carter. It’s only a matter of hours now before the blow falls.”

He took the midnight mail. Julius elected to stay the night at Holyhead. Half an hour after arrival, haggard and pale, Tommy stood before his chief.

“I’ve come to report, sir. I’ve failed—failed badly.”

“You mean that the treaty——?”

“Is in the hands of Mr. Brown, sir.”

“Ah!” said Mr. Carter quietly. His face did not change, but Tommy caught a flicker of despair in his eyes. “Well, we mustn’t sag at the knees, I suppose. I’m glad to know definitely. We must do what we can.”

Through Tommy’s mind flashed the assurance: “It’s hopeless, and he knows it’s hopeless!”

“I blame myself,” said Mr. Carter. “I have been blaming myself ever since I heard this other news.”

A new fear gripped Tommy. “Is there—something more, sir?”

“I’m afraid so.” He handed him a typed sheet.

“Tuppence——?” faltered Tommy.

“Read for yourself.”

The typed words danced: the description of a green toque, a coat with a handkerchief in the pocket marked P.L.C. Mr. Carter answered his look: “Washed up on the Yorkshire coast—near Ebury. I’m afraid—it looks very much like foul play.”

“My God!” gasped Tommy. “Tuppence! Those devils—I’ll never rest till I’ve got even with them! I’ll hunt them down! I’ll——”

“I know what you feel like, my poor boy,” said Mr. Carter gently. “But it’s no good. You’ll waste your strength uselessly. It may sound harsh, but my advice is: Cut your losses. Time’s merciful. You’ll forget.”

“Forget Tuppence? Never!”

“So you think now. Well, it won’t bear thinking of—that brave little girl! I’m confoundedly sorry.”

“I’m taking up your time, sir,” said Tommy, making an effort. “There’s no need for you to blame yourself. I dare say we were a couple of young fools. You warned us all right. But I wish to God I’d been the one to get it in the neck. Good-bye, sir.”

Back at the Ritz, he packed mechanically, stunned by tragedy’s invasion of his cheerful commonplace life. What fun they had had, he and Tuppence! And now—oh, it couldn’t be true! Tuppence—dead! Little Tuppence, brimming over with life! It was a horrible dream.

They brought him a note: a few kind words of sympathy from Peel Edgerton, who had read the headline (EX-V.A.D. FEARED DROWNED). The letter ended with the offer of a post on a ranch in the Argentine.

“Kind old beggar,” muttered Tommy, flinging it aside.

The door burst open; Julius, with a newspaper.

“Say, what’s all this? They seem to have got some fool idea about Tuppence.”

“It’s true,” said Tommy quietly.

“You mean they’ve done her in?”

Tommy nodded. “I suppose when they got the treaty she wasn’t any good to them any longer, and they were afraid to let her go.”

“Well, I’m darned!” said Julius brokenly. “Little Tuppence. She sure was the pluckiest little girl——”

Something cracked in Tommy’s brain. He sprang up. “Oh, get out! You don’t really care, damn you! You asked her to marry you in your rotten cold-blooded way, but I loved her. I’d have given the soul out of my body to save her. I’d have stood by and let her marry you, because you could have given her the sort of time she ought to have had, and I was only a poor devil without a penny. But it wouldn’t have been because I didn’t care!”

“See here,” began Julius temperately.

“Oh, go to the devil! Don’t come here talking about ‘little Tuppence.’ Go and look after your cousin. Tuppence is my girl! I’ve always loved her—from the time we played together as kids. I shall never forget when I was in hospital, and she came in in that ridiculous cap and apron! It was like a miracle to see the girl I loved turn up in a nurse’s kit——”

“A nurse’s kit!” Julius swung round. “Gee whiz! I must be going to Colney Hatch! I could swear I’ve seen Jane in a nurse’s cap too. And that’s plumb impossible! No—by gum, I’ve got it! It was her I saw talking to Whittington at that nursing home in Bournemouth. She wasn’t a patient there! She was a nurse!”

“I dare say,” said Tommy angrily. “She’s probably been in with them from the start. I shouldn’t wonder if she stole those papers from Danvers to begin with.”

“I’m darned if she did!” shouted Julius. “She’s my cousin, and as patriotic a girl as ever stepped.”

“I don’t care a damn what she is, but get out of here!”

They were on the point of blows, when Julius’s anger dropped away.

“All right, son,” he said quietly, “I’m going. I don’t blame you. It’s mighty lucky you did say it. I’ve been the most almighty blithering idiot. Calm down—I’m going right away now—going to the London and North Western Railway depot, if you want to know.”

“I don’t care a damn where you’re going,” growled Tommy.

As the door closed, he turned back to his suit-case. “That’s the lot,” he muttered, and rang the bell.

“Take my luggage down.”

“Yes, sir. Going away, sir?”

“I’m going to the devil,” said Tommy.

“Yes, sir. Shall I call a taxi?”

Tommy nodded. Where was he going? He hadn’t the faintest idea—only a fixed determination to get even with Mr. Brown. He re-read Sir James’s letter and shook his head. Tuppence must be avenged. Still, it was kind.

“Better answer it,” he decided. He went to the writing-table. There were envelopes but no paper. He rang. No one came. Fuming, he remembered there was paper in Julius’s sitting-room. The American had announced his immediate departure; there was no fear of meeting him.

The room was deserted. Tommy crossed to the writing-table and opened the middle drawer. A photograph, carelessly thrust in face upwards, caught his eye. He stood rooted; then took it out, shut the drawer, and sat down, staring.

What on earth was a photograph of the French girl Annette doing in Julius Hersheimmer’s writing-table?

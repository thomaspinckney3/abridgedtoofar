ENTER SIR JAMES PEEL EDGERTON

Tuppence betrayed no awkwardness in her new duties. The archdeacon’s daughters had been well grounded in household tasks and in training a “raw girl.”

Mrs. Vandemeyer’s cook puzzled her: a first-rate hand, yet in deadly terror of her mistress, as though there were a hold somewhere.

A guest was expected to dinner. Tuppence, laying the beautifully polished table for two, hoped the visitor would not prove to be Whittington. At a few minutes past eight the bell rang. She opened the door with inward trepidation, and was relieved to see the second of the two men whom Tommy had followed.

“Count Stepanov,” he said.

“It is delightful to see you, Boris Ivanovitch,” purred Mrs. Vandemeyer, rising from the divan.

In the kitchen, Tuppence affected frank curiosity. “Who’s he?”

“A Russian gentleman, I believe,” said the cook shortly.

“Comes here much?”

“Once in a while.”

Whilst waiting at table, Tuppence listened closely. The talk ran on indifferent things—plays, dances, gossip. After dinner she carried in coffee and liqueurs to the boudoir where Mrs. Vandemeyer, stretched on the divan, looked more wickedly beautiful than ever.

“New, isn’t she?” said Boris.

“She came in to-day. The other was a fiend. This girl seems all right. She waits well.”

“Quite safe, I suppose?”

“Really, Boris, you are absurdly suspicious. I believe she’s the cousin of the hall porter, or something of the kind. And nobody even dreams that I have any connection with our—mutual friend, Mr. Brown.”

“For heaven’s sake, be careful, Rita. That door isn’t shut.”

“Well, shut it then,” laughed Mrs. Vandemeyer.

Tuppence, who had lingeringly “forgotten” the door, fled, and, clearing away with hospital speed, cast about for a safer vantage. The balcony! Mrs. Vandemeyer’s bedroom opened on to a long balcony that ran the length of the flat. Slipping through the French windows, Tuppence crept along to the boudoir. The window there stood a little ajar; the voices within came plainly.

They were at variance.

“With your persistent recklessness, you will end by ruining us!” Boris burst out.

“Bah!” laughed the woman. “Notoriety of the right kind is the best way of disarming suspicion. You will realize that one of these days—perhaps sooner than you think!”

“In the meantime you go everywhere with Peel Edgerton. Not only is he, perhaps, the most celebrated K.C. in England, but his special hobby is criminology! It is madness!”

“I know that his eloquence has saved untold men from the gallows,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer calmly. “What of it? I may need his assistance in that line myself some day. If so, how fortunate to have such a friend at court—or shall I say, in court?”

“You are a clever woman, Rita; but you are also a fool! Be guided by me, and give up Peel Edgerton.”

“I think not.”

“You refuse?” There was an ugly ring in the Russian’s voice.

“I do.”

“Then, by Heaven, we will see——”

She rose too, eyes flashing. “You forget, Boris. I am accountable to no one. I take my orders only from—Mr. Brown.”

He threw up his hands. “Impossible! Already it may be too late. They say Peel Edgerton can smell a criminal! Perhaps even now his suspicions are aroused.”

“Reassure yourself, my dear Boris. I am commonly accounted a beautiful woman. I assure you that is all that interests Peel Edgerton.”

“He has studied crime as no other man has studied it. Do you fancy that you can deceive him?”

Her eyes narrowed. “If he is all that you say—it would amuse me to try!”

“Good heavens, Rita——”

“Besides, he is extremely rich. I am not one who despises money. The sinews of war, you know, Boris!”

“Money—money! That is always the danger with you. I believe you would sell your soul for money. I believe——” He dropped his voice, slow and sinister. “Sometimes I believe you would sell—us!”

Mrs. Vandemeyer shrugged. “The price, at any rate, would have to be enormous,” she said lightly. “Beyond the power of anyone but a millionaire to pay.”

“Ah!” snarled the Russian. “You see!”

“My dear Boris, can you not take a joke?”

“Was it a joke?”

“Of course.”

“Then your ideas of humour are peculiar.”

“Let us not quarrel. Touch the bell. We will have some drinks.”

Tuppence beat a retreat, smoothed her apron in the long glass, and answered the summons demurely. The talk she had overheard proved the complicity of both Rita and Boris, named Mr. Brown, and showed Rita’s cool audacity; but there was no word of Jane Finn, nor anything of Tommy. The omission gnawed at her. Before leaving the Ritz she had arranged that all messages should be sent at once to a small stationer’s near at hand, where Albert was to call frequently. Morning brought the report: nothing.

A cold hand seemed to close round her heart. Supposing— She choked it down. Worry was no use.

“What day do you usually go out, Prudence?” asked Mrs. Vandemeyer later, idly watching the bright polish travel over silver.

“Friday’s my usual day, ma’am.”

“And to-day is Friday! But I suppose you hardly wish to go out to-day, as you only came yesterday.”

“I was thinking of asking if I might, ma’am.”

Mrs. Vandemeyer studied her, smiling, catlike. “I wish Count Stepanov could hear you. He made a suggestion about you last night.” The smile broadened. “Your request is very—typical. You can go out to-day. It makes no difference to me, as I shall not be dining at home.”

Tuppence thanked her, and, once out of that stare, breathed more freely. Beautiful as the woman was, Tuppence confessed to herself that she was horribly afraid of her.

The front bell rang. Tuppence went to answer it. This time the visitor was neither Whittington nor Boris, but a man of striking appearance. A shade over average height, he yet conveyed the impression of a big man. Clean-shaven, mobile face—power and force stamped upon it; magnetism seemed to radiate from him.

“Sir James Peel Edgerton,” he said.

Tuppence’s interest quickened. The name was famous. She had heard it said that he might one day be Prime Minister. He was known to have refused office in the interests of his profession, preferring to remain a simple Member for a Scotch constituency.

About a quarter of an hour later the bell rang for his departure. He had given her a piercing glance before. Now, as she handed him his hat and stick, she was conscious of his eyes raking her through.

“Not been doing this long, eh?”

Tuppence stared, astonished.

He nodded to his own thought. “V.A.D. and hard up, I suppose?”

“Did Mrs. Vandemeyer tell you that?” asked Tuppence suspiciously.

“No, child. The look of you told me. Good place here?”

“Very good, thank you, sir.”

“Ah, but there are plenty of good places nowadays. And a change does no harm sometimes.”

“Do you mean——?” began Tuppence.

But he was already on the topmost stair. He looked back with his kindly, shrewd glance. “Just a hint,” he said. “That’s all.”

Tuppence went back to the pantry more thoughtful than ever.

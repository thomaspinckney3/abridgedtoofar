When Tommy set forth on the trail of the two men, it took all Tuppence’s self-command not to run after him. Consoling herself that her “Rita” deduction had panned out, she drifted back to the entrance hall of South Audley Mansions. A small lift-boy was polishing brass and whistling. He glanced up. There was a certain amount of the gamin element in the girl, at all events she invariably got on well with small boys. A sympathetic bond seemed instantly to be formed.

“Well, William,” she said cheerfully, “getting a good shine up?”

“Albert, miss,” he corrected, grinning.

“Albert be it.” She looked round mysteriously, leaned in, and, with an ample gesture, flipped back her coat to show a small enamelled badge. It had lately pinned some flowers, but she had spied the corner of a threepenny detective tale in Albert’s pocket.

“American Detective Force!” she hissed.

Albert fell for it, eyes growing round. “Lord!”

“Know who I’m after?” She jerked a thumb upstairs. “No. 20. Calls herself Vandemeyer. Vandemeyer! Ha! ha!”

“A crook?” breathed Albert.

“A crook? I should say so. ‘Ready Rita’ they call her in the States.”

“Ready Rita,” he repeated ecstatically.

“Oh, ain’t it just like the pictures!”

It was. Tuppence was a great frequenter of the cinema.

“Has she been wearing any of the emeralds?” Tuppence asked carelessly.

“Emeralds? Them’s the green stones, ain’t they?”

“Peter B. Rysdale, the oil king,” said Tuppence promptly. “Finest collection of emeralds in the world. Worth a million dollars!”

“Lumme! It sounds more like the pictures every minute.”

She nodded, gratified. “We haven’t exactly proved it yet. But we’re after her. And”—Tuppence favoured him with a long-drawn wink—“I guess she won’t get away with the goods this time. Mind you, not a word—in the States we know a smart lad when we see one.”

“I’ll not breathe a word,” protested Albert eagerly. “Ain’t there anything I could do? A bit o’ shadowing?”

“Not the moment,” said Tuppence, considering. “But see here—didn’t you say a girl upstairs was leaving?”

“Annie. Regular turn up there’s been.”

“How would it be if you mentioned a young cousin—or a friend of yours had—who’d suit the place?”

“I’m there,” said Albert, radiant. “Leave it to me. I’ll fix the whole thing up in two ticks.”

“You might say the young woman could come in right away. Let me know at the Ritz—name of Cowley.”

“It must be a good job, this tec business,” sighed Albert, envious.

“It sure is—when old man Rysdale backs the bill. But don’t fret, son. If this goes well, you shall come in on the ground floor.”

Well pleased with her morning’s work, Tuppence walked briskly away. At the Ritz she scrawled a brief note to Mr. Carter; then, Tommy not yet returned (which did not surprise her), she set forth to shop—cheap clothing, a second-hand place or two, and finally a fashionable hairdresser, with tea and creamy cakes by the way. In her room, five minutes turned a dark-headed V.A.D. into a demure fair-haired parlourmaid. With an actress’s pencil she altered her brows; a new luxuriant growth of pale hair sat well above; she would add elevators to her shoes; the cap and apron would do the rest. “Yes,” said Tuppence to the pert face in the glass, “you’ll do.”

Dinner was solitary, and Tommy had not returned. Julius, likely, was harrying Prefects of Police on the Continent or telephones at the Admiralty. “All very well—but this is very dull for me!” said Tuppence. She summoned a small boy and, ten minutes later, was deep in Garnaby Williams, the Boy Detective, fortifying herself with local colour before further intercourse with Albert.

Morning brought Mr. Carter’s reply:

“Dear Miss Tuppence,—You have made a splendid start. But I warn you again: those people are desperate, and I can promise you no protection. If you choose to withdraw now, no one could blame you. If, despite my warnings, you go on, you will find everything arranged. You have lived for two years with Miss Dufferin, The Parsonage, Llanelly; Mrs. Vandemeyer can apply there for a reference. Stick as near to the truth as possible—it minimizes the danger of slips. Represent yourself as what you are, a former V.A.D. who has chosen domestic service as a profession. Whichever way you decide, good luck.—Your sincere friend, MR. CARTER.”

Tuppence’s spirits rose. His warnings passed unheeded; but she sensibly adopted his plan. A dirty postcard arrived too: “It’s O.K.” scrawled in Tommy’s hand.

By half-past ten a battered tin trunk held her purchases, artistically corded. With a blush, she had it placed in a taxi, deposited it at Paddington cloak-room, and repaired to the ladies’ waiting-room. Ten minutes later a metamorphosed Tuppence walked demurely out and took a bus.

At a few minutes past eleven she re-entered South Audley Mansions. Albert, vaguely relegated by a magnificent hall porter, was on the look-out, and did not at first recognize her.

“Blest if I’d have known you! That rig-out’s top-hole.”

“By the way, am I your cousin, or am I not?”

“I said as a friend o’ mine knew a young gal,” returned Albert, delighted with her “as English as anything!” voice. “Annie’s stopped on till to-day—to oblige, she said—but really it’s so as to put you against the place.”

“Nice girl,” said Tuppence, and quelled his wink with a glance as he whisked her up in the lift.

She rang No. 20. A smart maid answered, and in a moment Tuppence was ushered into a room on the right of the long passage.

A woman stood by the fireplace. Beauty there was, but hardened and menacing: pale gold hair, coiled low; eyes of a piercing electric blue that seemed to bore into one’s very soul; a red curving mouth with a cruel line. Indigo charmeuse enhanced a figure of exquisite grace, and yet through all that loveliness glinted a metallic strength—the steely ring in her voice, the gimlet quality of her gaze. For the first time Tuppence felt afraid. She had not feared Whittington, but this woman was different. As if fascinated, she watched the long cruel line of the red curving mouth, and again the sensation of panic swept her. Her usual self-confidence deserted her. Vaguely she felt that deceiving this woman would be very different to deceiving Whittington. Mr. Carter’s warning recurred to her mind. Here, indeed, she might expect no mercy.

Fighting down that instinct of panic which urged her to turn tail and run without further delay, Tuppence returned the lady’s gaze firmly and respectfully.

Mrs. Vandemeyer motioned to a chair. “You can sit down. How did you hear I wanted a house-parlourmaid?”

“Through a friend who knows the lift-boy here. He thought the place might suit me.”

“You speak like an educated girl?”

Glibly and on Carter’s lines, Tuppence told her tale: V.A.D. service, now taking up domestic work. The tension seemed to relax.

“Is there anyone I can write to for a reference?”

“I lived last with Miss Dufferin, The Parsonage, Llanelly. I was with her two years.”

“And then you thought you would get more money by coming to London, I suppose? Well, it doesn’t matter to me. I will give you £50—£60—whatever you want. You can come in at once?”

“To-day, if you like. My box is at Paddington.”

“Go and fetch it in a taxi, then. It is an easy place. I am out a good deal. By the way, what’s your name?”

“Prudence Cooper, ma’am.”

“Very well, Prudence. Go away and fetch your box. I shall be out to lunch. The cook will show you where everything is.”

Tuppence withdrew meekly past the magnificent porter, Albert nowhere in evidence. The adventure had begun; but, suddenly, her elation dropped. If the unknown Jane Finn had fallen into Mrs. Vandemeyer’s hands, it was likely to have gone hard with her.

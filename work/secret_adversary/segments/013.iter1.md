A FRIEND IN NEED

Friday and Saturday passed uneventfully. Tuppence had a brief answer from Mr. Carter: they had undertaken the work at their own risk; if anything had happened to Tommy he regretted it, but he could do nothing. Without Tommy all the savour went out of the adventure; though she prided herself on quick wits, she felt rudderless. Julius, though cleverer, did not steady her as Tommy’s sober common sense had done. He might be slow, but he was very sure. For the first time the mission looked sinister, its romance shorn off; Tommy--that was all that mattered. Many times a day she blinked resolutely back tears. “Little fool,” she apostrophized herself, “don’t snivel. Of course you’re fond of him. You’ve known him all your life. But there’s no need to be sentimental about it.”

Boris did not come; Julius and the car waited in vain. Tuppence, despite Julius’s objections, had not relinquished the idea of appealing to Sir James Peel Edgerton. He had looked kindly at her; perhaps he had meant to warn her; perhaps he might tell them something of Mrs. Vandemeyer and so lead to Tommy. Sunday being her afternoon out, she would meet Julius and beard the lion in his den.

Julius needed persuading, but at last gave in. An irreproachable butler showed them into Sir James’s library. Sir James rose from a roll-top desk strewn with papers.

“You have a message for me? Ah—” he recognized Tuppence with a smile—“it’s you, is it? Brought a message from Mrs. Vandemeyer, I suppose?”

“Not exactly,” said Tuppence; then, introducing Julius: “This is Mr. Hersheimmer, Sir James Peel Edgerton.”

“Won’t you both sit down?” said Sir James.

Tuppence plunged: “You’ll think it awful cheek, but I want to know what you meant the other day. Did you mean to warn me against Mrs. Vandemeyer?”

“Well, perhaps I did,” admitted Sir James gravely. “Without prejudice, then: if I had a young sister forced to earn her living, I should not like to see her in Mrs. Vandemeyer’s service.”

“I see,” said Tuppence. “But I’m not really inexperienced. I knew she was a bad lot when I went—indeed that’s why I went—” She broke off. “I think I’d better tell you the whole story. You’d know in a minute if I didn’t tell the truth.” Julius nodded her on. “I want to know who Tommy is,” added Sir James.

She told him everything. He listened closely. “Very interesting. A great deal of what you tell me, child, I already know. You’ve done extraordinarily well so far; it’s rather too bad of—what do you know him as?—Mr. Carter to pitchfork you two young things into an affair of this kind. By the way, where did Mr. Hersheimmer come in originally?”

“I’m Jane’s first cousin,” said Julius.

“H’m.” Sir James paced slowly. “When you arrived, young lady, I was just packing up my traps. Going to Scotland by the night train for a few days’ fishing. But there are different kinds of fishing. I’ve a good mind to stay, and see if we can’t get on the track of that young chap.”

“Oh!” Tuppence clasped her hands.

“All the same,” he went on kindly, “don’t be offended because I think you’re young, Miss—er—”

“Cowley. Prudence Cowley. But my friends call me Tuppence.”

“Well then, Miss Tuppence. Frankly, things look bad for him—he’s been butting in somewhere where he wasn’t wanted. But don’t give up hope. And this petty little business, as Mr. Hersheimmer calls it, bears directly on a very big business—bigger perhaps than either of you know. If this boy is alive, he may have very valuable information. Therefore, we must find him.”

“Yes, but how?” cried Tuppence.

“There’s one person near at hand who probably knows where he is, or where he is likely to be,” said Sir James. “Mrs. Vandemeyer.”

“She’d never tell us.”

“That is where I come in. I think it quite likely that I shall be able to make Mrs. Vandemeyer tell me what I want to know.” He tapped with his finger on the table, and Tuppence felt again the intense power that radiated from the man. “If she will not, there is always bribery.”

“Sure—and that’s where I come in!” cried Julius. “You can count on me, if necessary, for one million dollars.”

Sir James subjected him to a long scrutiny. “Mr. Hersheimmer, that is a very large sum.”

“I can deliver the goods.”

“There is no time to be lost,” said Sir James at last. “The sooner we strike the better. Is Mrs. Vandemeyer dining out to-night?”

“Yes; but not late.”

“Good. I will call upon her at ten o’clock. Be back by nine-thirty; do not return earlier. Mr. Hersheimmer will wait below in a car. If I obtain the address from her, we go there at once, taking Mrs. Vandemeyer with us if necessary. You understand?”

“Yes,” cried Tuppence, lightened at heart.

They parted. Tuppence, restless, walked, but as she neared Hyde Park Corner an unreasonable uneasiness grew. She would do no harm just to look at South Audley Mansions. Its stolidity reassured her a shade—when a piercing whistle brought Albert flying.

“I say, miss, she’s a-going!”

“Who?”

“Ready Rita. Mrs. Vandemeyer. She’s a-packing up, and she’s just sent down for a taxi.”

Tuppence clutched his arm. “Albert, you’re a brick. Quick—there’s a telephone box round the corner? Ring up the Ritz, ask for Mr. Hersheimmer; if you can’t get him, ring Sir James Peel Edgerton. Tell them to come at once, as Mrs. Vandemeyer is trying to hook it. Don’t forget the names.”

“You trust to me, miss. But what about you? Aren’t you afraid to trust yourself with her?”

“No—go and telephone. Be quick.”

Drawing a long breath, Tuppence rang No. 20. After delay, Mrs. Vandemeyer herself opened. She lifted her eyebrows.

“You?”

“I had a touch of toothache, ma’am,” said Tuppence glibly. “So thought it better to come home and have a quiet evening.”

“Cook is out,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer disagreeably. “You had better go to bed.”

There was a new ring in her voice that Tuppence did not like; she was being edged up the passage. She turned at bay—when cold steel touched her temple.

“You damned little fool! Do you think I don’t know? If you struggle or cry out, I’ll shoot you like a dog. Now then, march—into my room. In a minute, when I’ve done with you, you’ll go to bed as I told you to. And you’ll sleep—oh yes, my little spy, you’ll sleep all right!”

Clothes lay flung about; a suit-case and a hat-box stood half-packed. The pistol never left Tuppence’s forehead.

“You can’t shoot me,” she said steadily. “Every one in the building would hear the report.”

“I’d risk that,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer cheerfully. “But as long as you don’t sing out, you’re all right. Sit on the bed. Hands above your head. If you value your life, don’t move them.”

She laid the revolver on the washstand within reach, took a little stoppered bottle, poured, and filled it up with water.

“What’s that?” demanded Tuppence.

“Something to make you sleep soundly.”

“Are you going to poison me?”

“Perhaps,” said the other, smiling agreeably. “Don’t be a little fool. Poisoning you wouldn’t suit my book at all. It’s a sleeping draught. I don’t want the bother of tying and gagging you. Drink this down like a good girl, and you’ll be none the worse.”

In her heart Tuppence believed her; nevertheless she would not be quietly put to sleep without a bid for freedom. She lurched from the bed, clutching the other’s skirts, sobbing for mercy, edging nearer her mark.

“Drink it at once!” Imperiously the glass was pressed to her lips.

“You swear it won’t hurt me?” temporized Tuppence.

“Yes, yes,” said the other impatiently. “I swear it.”

Tuppence’s left hand rose tremulously—her mouth opened meekly—then like lightning she jerked the glass up; the fluid splashed into Mrs. Vandemeyer’s face; Tuppence’s right hand shot out and grasped the revolver. Springing back, she covered the other’s heart, the weapon steady. In the moment of victory, Tuppence betrayed a somewhat unsportsmanlike triumph. “Now who’s on top and who’s underneath?” she crowed.

Rage convulsed the beautiful face. With an effort Mrs. Vandemeyer controlled herself; a slow evil smile crept over her lips. “Not a fool, then, after all! You did that well, girl. But you shall pay for it—oh, yes, you shall pay for it! I have a long memory!”

“Supposing we sit down,” said Tuppence pleasantly. “Our present attitude is a little melodramatic. No—not on the bed. Draw a chair up to the table. I’ll sit opposite—with the revolver in front of me—just in case of accidents. Now, let’s talk.”

“What about?” sullenly.

Tuppence remembered Boris’s words: “I believe you would sell—us!” and her own light answer: “The price would have to be enormous.” Keeping her eyes steadily on the other: “Money—” she said quietly.

Mrs. Vandemeyer started. “What do you mean?”

“I mean that a long purse is more practical than a long memory. Revenge is very unsatisfactory. Money—well, there’s nothing unsatisfactory about money, is there?”

“Do you think I am the kind of woman to sell my friends?” scornfully.

“Yes,” said Tuppence promptly, “if the price was big enough.”

“A paltry hundred pounds or so!”

“No. I should suggest—” Tuppence’s voice was cool—“a hundred thousand!”

A faint flush rose. Fingers played nervously with a brooch; in that instant Tuppence knew the fish was hooked, and felt a sudden horror of her own money-loving spirit.

“You haven’t got it,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer at last.

“No—but I know some one who has.”

“Who?”

“A friend of mine.”

“Must be a millionaire,” unbelievingly.

“As a matter of fact he is. He’s an American. He’ll pay you that without a murmur. It’s a perfectly genuine proposition.”

“I’m inclined to believe you,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer slowly. After a pause: “What does he want to know?”

Julius’s interests must come first. “Where Jane Finn is,” said Tuppence boldly.

“I’m not sure where she is at the present moment,” said the other carelessly. “There would be no difficulty about finding out.”

“And—there’s a boy, a friend of mine. I’m afraid something’s happened to him, through your pal Boris. Tommy Beresford.”

“Never heard of him. But I’ll ask Boris. He’ll tell me anything he knows.”

“Thank you.” Tuppence’s spirits rose. One thing more—she leaned forward, lowered her voice:

“Who is Mr. Brown?”

The beautiful face paled. With an effort Mrs. Vandemeyer tried to resume her manner; the parody deceived no one. She shrugged. “You can’t have learnt much about us if you don’t know that nobody knows who Mr. Brown is....”

“You do,” said Tuppence quietly.

Again the colour ebbed. “What makes you think that?”

“I don’t know,” said the girl truthfully. “But I’m sure.”

Mrs. Vandemeyer stared before her a long time. “Yes,” she said hoarsely at last, “I know. I was beautiful, you see—very beautiful—”

“You are still,” said Tuppence with admiration.

“Not beautiful enough,” came the soft, dangerous voice. “Not—beautiful—enough! And sometimes, lately, I’ve been afraid.... It’s dangerous to know too much!” She leaned across. “Swear that my name shan’t be brought into it—that no one shall ever know.”

“I swear it. And, once he’s caught, you’ll be out of danger.”

“Shall I? Shall I ever be?” Terror swept her face. She clutched Tuppence’s arm. “You’re sure about the money?”

“Quite sure. He’ll be here presently. He may have to send cables—but there won’t be any delay—he’s a terrific hustler.”

“I’ll do it,” said Mrs. Vandemeyer resolutely. “It’s a great sum of money—and besides—” she smiled curiously—“it is not—wise to throw over a woman like me!”

She sat lightly tapping her fingers on the table; suddenly she started, blanching. “What was that?”

“I heard nothing.”

“If there should be some one listening—Even the walls might have ears. I tell you I’m frightened. You don’t know him!”

“Think of the hundred thousand pounds,” said Tuppence soothingly.

“You don’t know him,” she reiterated hoarsely. “He’s—ah!”

With a shriek she sprang up; her hand pointed over Tuppence’s head; she swayed and dropped in a dead faint.

Tuppence looked round. In the doorway stood Sir James Peel Edgerton and Julius Hersheimmer.

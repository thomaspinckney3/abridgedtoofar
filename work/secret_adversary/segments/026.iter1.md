Her arm through Jane’s, dragging her along, Tuppence reached the station. The train drew in as they gained the platform; she thrust Jane into an empty first-class, and they sank breathless on the padded seats.

A man looked in, then passed on. Jane started; her eyes were wide with terror.

“Is he one of them, do you think?” she breathed.

Tuppence shook her head. “No, no. It’s all right. Tommy wouldn’t have told us to do this unless he was sure we’d be all right.”

“But he doesn’t know them as I do! Five years! Five long years!” She shivered.

“Never mind. It’s all over.”

“Is it?”

The train sped through the night. Jane started up. “I thought I saw a face—looking in through the window.”

“There’s nothing. See.” Tuppence let the pane down, then drew Jane’s hand into hers. “Lie back, and don’t think.”

“My cousin didn’t think so. He didn’t want us to do this.”

Tuppence was silent a moment. “I was thinking of something. But I don’t want to tell you—not now. Do what I tell you—lie back and don’t think of anything.”

Jane’s lashes drooped. Tuppence sat bolt upright, a watchful terrier on guard, eyes flicking from window to window. Not that she disbelieved in Tommy, but occasionally she was shaken with doubts as to whether anyone so simple and honest as he was could ever be a match for the fiendish subtlety of the arch-criminal. If once they reached Sir James all would be well. But would they reach him?

At Charing Cross Jane sat up with a start. “Have we arrived?”

“Oh, I thought we’d get to London all right. If there’s going to be any fun, now is when it will begin. Quick. We’ll nip into a taxi.” As they started, a man looked in at the window; Tuppence had a horrible sense of being hemmed in.

“King’s Cross,” she directed, then, low to Jane: “If they think we’re going to Sir James, this will put them off the scent. Now they’ll imagine we’re going to Mr. Carter.”

A block at Holborn—what Tuppence had waited for. “Quick. Open the right-hand door!” They slipped out, took another taxi, and doubled back, this time direct to Carlton House Terrace.

“This ought to do them,” murmured Tuppence with satisfaction. “How that other taxi man will swear! But I’ll send him a postal order to-morrow— What’s this—Oh!”

A grinding bump. Another taxi had collided with them. Tuppence was out in a flash. A policeman approached; she pressed five shillings on the driver, and drew Jane into the crowd.

“It’s only a step or two now.” Trafalgar Square. “It may be my fancy, but I feel as though there was some one behind us.”

“Hurry!” breathed Jane.

At the corner of Carlton House Terrace a large, apparently intoxicated man lurched across their path. “Whither away so fast?” He clutched Jane’s shoulder. Footsteps sounded behind. Tuppence lowered her head and butted him full in the middle. He sat down abruptly on the pavement. They ran on, choking for breath. At Sir James’s door they pulled bell and knocker together. The man hesitated at the foot of the steps; the door opened; they tumbled into the hall.

Sir James came from the library, caught Jane as she swayed, laid her on a leather couch, and gave her brandy. “It’s all right. Don’t be afraid, my child. You’re quite safe.”

He looked quizzically at Tuppence. “So you’re not dead, Miss Tuppence, any more than that Tommy boy of yours was!”

“The Young Adventurers take a lot of killing,” boasted Tuppence.

“So it seems,” said Sir James dryly. “Am I right that the joint venture has ended in success, and that this—” he turned to the girl—“is Miss Jane Finn?”

Jane sat up. “Yes. I am Jane Finn. I have a lot to tell you.”

“When you are stronger——”

“No—now! I shall feel safer when I have told everything.”

“As you please,” said the lawyer, and Jane began, low and steady:

“I came over on the Lusitania to take up a post in a Paris hospital. I was dying to help. When the Lusitania was torpedoed, a man—Mr. Danvers—asked me if I was a patriotic American. He said he carried papers that were life or death to the Allies. He gave them to me. I was to watch for an advertisement in the Times. If it didn’t appear, I was to take them to the American Ambassador.

“At first I had no suspicions. Then, on the boat to Holyhead, I noticed a woman—Mrs. Vandemeyer—who had been ‘chummy’ with me, talking to queer-looking men, and they watched me. I’d seen her near Mr. Danvers when he gave me the packet. I got scared. I ripped open the oilskin, substituted blank paper, sewed it up again. The two real sheets I laid flat between advertisement pages of a magazine, gumming the edges, and stuffed the magazine into my ulster pocket.

“At Holyhead, no matter what I did, I seemed pushed into a carriage with Mrs. Vandemeyer and a nice-looking man and his wife. Just outside London I saw the man hand Mrs. Vandemeyer something—and he winked. ‘I can’t tell you how that wink sort of froze me through and through.’ My only thought was to get out into the corridor as quick as ever I could. I got up, trying to look natural and easy. She cried ‘Now!’—something was flung across my face—and a terrific blow on the head....”

She shuddered, then went on:

“I woke on a dirty bed behind a screen. Mrs. Vandemeyer and a man were talking. They hadn’t found the papers; they were mad. They spoke of—torturing me. I knew I couldn’t stand that. Suddenly I thought of loss of memory. I’d read all about it. I opened my eyes and began babbling in French. Mrs. Vandemeyer came—her face was wicked—but I smiled and asked where I was. The man spoke French too. He scared me worse than she did. He caught my wrist and twisted. I screamed and screamed—but in French. At last he said: ‘That’s not bluff! Anyway, a kid of her age wouldn’t know enough.’ After that, she was sweet as honey. She told me I was her niece, and I was to call her ‘Aunt Rita.’ She showed me the oilskin. I took it, turned it over, said it seemed as if I ought to remember—but couldn’t.

“I was suspicious still, and lay quite quiet for some time. In the end I got up and walked round the room, examining it. I thought that even if anyone was watching me from somewhere, it would seem natural enough under the circumstances. It was a squalid, dirty place. There were no windows. I guessed the door would be locked, but I didn’t try it. On the walls were battered old pictures—scenes from Faust. All of a sudden I sat down on the edge of the table, and put my face in my hands, sobbing out a ‘Mon Dieu! Mon Dieu!’ I’ve got very sharp ears. I distinctly heard the rustle of a dress, and a slight creak. That was enough for me. I was being watched!

“That night, about two, I got up softly. I unhooked one of the pictures—Marguerite with her casket of jewels—damped the brown paper backing, pulled it away, slipped the two stuck-together pages with their precious enclosure between the canvas and the paper, gummed it down again, rehung the picture, and put the magazine back in my pocket. No one would dream it had been tampered with.

“They kept me, questioning for hours together; the strain was awful. They took me back to Ireland over every step, in case I’d hidden the papers en route. I dared appeal to no one. In the end I was sent to a sanatorium at Bournemouth as a special patient. The nurse seemed nice, and I nearly confided in her, but I overheard her in the passage—she was one of them! After that my nerve went. I think I almost hypnotized myself. After a while, I almost forgot that I was really Jane Finn. I was so bent on playing the part of Janet Vandemeyer that my nerves began to play me tricks. I became really ill—for months I sank into a sort of stupor. A sane person shut up in a lunatic asylum often ends by becoming insane, they say. I guess I was like that. I wasn’t even unhappy—just apathetic. The years went on.

“Then suddenly there was a change. Mrs. Vandemeyer came down with a doctor; they questioned me, tried treatments; there was talk of Paris. They didn’t dare risk it. The nurse went instead, and consulted a specialist, pretending to be me. He exposed her loss of memory as fraudulent, but she noted his methods and tried them on me. Perhaps a specialist would have seen through me in a minute; I managed to hold my own.

“One night I was whisked to London—to the house in Soho. Away from the sanatorium I felt different, as though something in me was waking. They sent me in to wait on Mr. Beresford—of course I didn’t know his name then. I was suspicious; I knew there was a small hole high up in the wall. But he looked so honest. On the Sunday a message came; they were disturbed. He was to be killed. I thought I’d have time to snatch the papers—but I was caught. So I screamed that he was escaping, and begged to go back to Marguerite. I shouted the name three times. I knew they’d think I meant Mrs. Vandemeyer, but I hoped it would make Mr. Beresford think of the picture. He’d unhooked one the first day. I was taken away before I could get the papers. They’re still there.”

She sank back, exhausted.

Sir James rose and looked at his watch. “Come,” he said, “we must go at once.”

“To-night?” cried Tuppence.

“To-morrow may be too late,” said Sir James gravely. “Besides, by going to-night we have the chance of capturing that great man and super-criminal—Mr. Brown! You have been followed here—when we leave we shall be followed again, but not molested, for it is Mr. Brown’s plan that we are to lead him. The Soho house is under police supervision night and day. When we enter, Mr. Brown will not draw back—he will risk all on the chance of obtaining the spark to fire his mine. And he fancies the risk not great—since he will enter in the guise of a friend!”

Tuppence flushed, then, impulsively: “There’s something you don’t know—that we haven’t told you.” She hesitated, glanced at Jane. “If I’m wrong—oh, it would be dreadful.”

“You want me to help you out, eh?”

“Yes, please. You know who Mr. Brown is, don’t you?”

“Yes,” said Sir James gravely. “At last I do.”

“At last?” murmured Tuppence. “Oh, but I thought——”

“You thought correctly, Miss Tuppence. I have been morally certain of his identity ever since the night of Mrs. Vandemeyer’s mysterious death.”

“Ah!” breathed Tuppence.

“There are only two solutions. Either the chloral was administered by her own hand—which I reject utterly—or else it was in the brandy you gave her. Only three people touched that brandy—you, I myself, and one other—Mr. Julius Hersheimmer! At first it seemed impossible. As the son of a prominent millionaire, he was well known in America. It seemed impossible that he and Mr. Brown could be one and the same. But you cannot escape from the logic of facts. Remember Mrs. Vandemeyer’s sudden and inexplicable agitation. I gave you a hint. From some words of Mr. Hersheimmer’s at Manchester, I gathered you had understood. Then Mr. Beresford rang me—what I had already suspected—the photograph of Miss Jane Finn had never really been out of Mr. Hersheimmer’s possession——”

Jane sprang to her feet. “What do you mean? What are you trying to suggest? That Mr. Brown is Julius? Julius—my own cousin!”

“No, Miss Finn,” said Sir James unexpectedly. “Not your cousin. The man who calls himself Julius Hersheimmer is no relation to you whatsoever.”

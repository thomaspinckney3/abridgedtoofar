If audacity had carried him so far, let it carry him farther. Tommy mounted the filthy ramshackle stairs; the grimy paper hung in festoons, cobwebs choked every angle. The man below had retired—clearly no suspicion attached to a caller for Mr. Brown.

At the top a narrow passage ran right and left. From the nearest door on the left came a murmur of voices: the very room indicated. But opposite, half hidden by a torn velvet curtain, yawned a small recess commanding both that door and the head of the stairs—an ideal hiding-place. Tommy weighed his chances. “Mr. Brown” had likely been a password. Luck had carried him to the threshold; to go in was to risk immediate detection. He slipped into the recess and drew the curtain across.

The first arrival he saw was a man from the very dregs: beetling brows, bestial jaw. He knocked, was challenged: “Your number, comrade?”

“Fourteen, gov’nor.”

“Correct.” Through the brief opening Tommy glimpsed a long table, four or five seated round it, and at the head a tall man with close-cropped hair and a short pointed naval beard. His enunciation was correct and curiously precise. “If that isn’t a Hun, I’m a Dutchman!” Tommy told himself. “Lucky I didn’t roll in. I’d have given the wrong number.”

Another knock: an Irish Sinn Feiner. Certainly Mr. Brown’s organization was a far-reaching concern. The common criminal, the well-bred Irish gentleman, the pale Russian, and the efficient German master of the ceremonies! Truly a strange and sinister gathering! Who was this man who held in his finger these curiously variegated links of an unknown chain?

Then a shabbily dressed city clerk; a working-man with a vaguely familiar face; a commanding, exquisitely dressed gentleman Tommy felt he had seen before. Silence—Tommy was creeping out when a last-comer glided up so softly he was upon him before Tommy sensed it: a small very pale man, Slav cheek-bones, strange light eyes that seemed to burn through the curtain. Tommy shivered. He was no more fanciful than the majority of young Englishmen, but he could not rid himself of the impression that some unusually potent force emanated from the man. The creature reminded him of a venomous snake.

The newcomer knocked. Inside, every man rose. The bearded German came forward, heels clicking. “We are honoured. I much feared it would be impossible.”

“There were difficulties,” hissed the other. “It will not be possible again, I fear. But one meeting is essential—to define my policy. I can do nothing without—Mr. Brown. He is here?”

“We have received a message,” the German said, and hesitated.

“It is impossible for him to be present in person—”

A very slow smile passed over the pale face. He looked round at the uneasy circle. “Ah! I understand. He works in the dark and trusts no one. But all the same, it is possible that he is among us now....” He looked round him again, and again that expression of fear swept over the group. Each man seemed eyeing his neighbour doubtfully. The Russian tapped his cheek. “So be it. Let us proceed.”

The German indicated the head of the table; the other demurred; the German insisted: “It is the only possible place—for Number One. Perhaps Number Fourteen will shut the door?”

Tommy was once more facing bare panels. Impatience pricked him. He slipped off his shoes, tried the handle inch by inch; it would not yield. Locked or bolted. With the same infinite care he reversed the handle. There was a certain bulldog tenacity about Tommy that made him slow to admit defeat. Checkmated for the moment, he was far from abandoning the conflict. He still intended to hear what was going on in the locked room. As one plan had failed, he must hunt about for another.

He crept to the next room along, found it empty and filthy as the rest—and, blessedly, a communicating door by the window. The rusty bolt gave to patient wriggling; he turned the handle; a velvet portière within checked his view, but he could hear.

The Sinn Feiner’s rich voice: “That’s all very well. But more money is essential. No money—no results!”

Boris: “Will you guarantee there are results?”

“In a month from now—as you wish—I will guarantee you such a reign of terror in Ireland as shall shake the British Empire to its foundations.”

Number One’s soft sibilants: “Good! You shall have the money. Boris, you will see to that.”

“Via the Irish Americans, and Mr. Potter as usual?”

“I guess that’ll be all right,” drawled an American voice, “though I’d like to point out things are getting a mite difficult. There’s not the sympathy there was, and a disposition to let the Irish settle their own affairs.”

“Does that matter, since the money only nominally comes from the States?”

“The chief difficulty is landing the ammunition,” said the Sinn Feiner. “The money is conveyed in easily enough—thanks to our colleague here.”

“Think of the feelings of Belfast if they could hear you!” said the commanding Englishman drily.

“That is settled, then,” hissed Number One. “Now, the loan to an English newspaper—you have arranged the details, Boris?”

“I think so.”

“That is good. An official denial from Moscow will be forthcoming if necessary.”

Papers rustled; the German’s clear voice: “I am directed by—Mr. Brown to place the summaries of the reports from the different unions. That of the miners is most satisfactory. We must hold back the railways. There may be trouble with the A.S.E.”

Silence while papers turned; then the light tap-tap of fingers drumming on wood. “And—the date, my friend?”

“The 29th.”

“That is rather soon.”

“I know. But it was settled by the principal Labour leaders, and we cannot seem to interfere too much. They must believe it to be entirely their own show.”

The Russian laughed softly. “Yes, yes. They must have no inkling that we are using them for our own ends. They are honest men—and that is their value to us. It is curious—but you cannot make a revolution without honest men. The instinct of the populace is infallible. Every revolution has had its honest men. They are soon disposed of afterwards.”

“The next point,” resumed the German. “Clymes must go. He is too far-seeing. Number Fourteen will see to that.”

A hoarse assent: “That’s all right, gov’nor…. Suppose I’m nabbed?”

“You will have the best legal talent,” said the German coolly. “In any case you will wear gloves fitted with the finger-prints of a notorious housebreaker. You have little to fear.”

“Oh, I ain’t afraid. All for the good of the cause. The streets is going to run with blood, so they say.” He chuckled. “Dreams of it, sometimes, I does. And diamonds and pearls rolling about in the gutter for anyone to pick up!”

“Then all is arranged. We are assured of success?”

“I—think so.” Less confidence than before.

“What has gone wrong?”

“Nothing; but—the Labour leaders. Without them we can do nothing. If they do not declare a general strike on the 29th—”

“Why should they not?”

“They are honest. And, in spite of everything we’ve done to discredit the Government, I’m not sure they haven’t a sneaking belief in it. On the whole, public opinion swings to the Government. They will not go against it.”

“To the point. I was given to understand there was a certain document which assured success.”

“That is so. If that document were placed before the leaders, they would publish it broadcast and declare for revolution without a moment’s hesitation. The Government would be broken.”

“Then what more do you want?”

“The document itself.”

“Ah! It is not in your possession? But you know where it is?”

“No.”

“Does anyone know?”

“One person—perhaps. And we are not sure even of that.”

“Who is this person?”

“A girl.”

Tommy held his breath.

“A girl?” The Russian’s contempt was icy. “And you have not made her speak? In Russia we have ways of making a girl talk.”

“This case is different,” said the German sullenly.

“How—different? Where is the girl now?”

“She is—”

A crashing blow descended on Tommy’s head. All was darkness.

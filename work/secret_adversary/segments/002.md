THE YOUNG ADVENTURERS, LTD.

“Tommy, old thing!”

“Tuppence, old bean!”

They almost blocked the Dover Street Tube exit with their affectionate greeting. The adjective “old” was misleading. Their united ages would certainly not have totalled forty-five. “Not seen you for simply centuries,” said the young man. “Come and chew a bun with me. We’re getting a bit unpopular here--blocking the gangway as it were. Let’s get out of it.”

They walked towards Piccadilly. “Where shall we go?” asked Tommy with a faint anxiety that did not escape Miss Prudence Cowley—“Tuppence” to her friends.

“Tommy, you’re stony!”

“Rolling in cash,” said Tommy unconvincingly.

“You always were a shocking liar,” said Tuppence. But she steered them into Lyons. The place was full; as they hunted for a table, Tommy said: “Funny scraps one overhears. I passed two Johnnies to-day talking about some one called Jane Finn. Did you ever hear such a name?”

Two elderly ladies rose; Tuppence slid into a seat. “Tea and buns,” ordered Tommy. “Tea and buttered toast. And mind the tea comes in separate teapots,” added Tuppence severely.

Tommy’s slicked-back red hair shone; his suit was perilously near the end of its tether. His face was pleasantly ugly--nondescript, yet unmistakably the face of a gentleman and a sportsman. They were an essentially modern-looking couple as they sat there. Tuppence had no claim to beauty, but there was character and charm in the elfin lines of her little face, with its determined chin and large, wide-apart grey eyes that looked mistily out from under straight, black brows. She wore a small bright green toque over her black bobbed hair, and her extremely short and rather shabby skirt revealed a pair of uncommonly dainty ankles.

“Now then,” said Tommy, “let’s get up-to-date. I haven’t seen you since 1916.”

“Abridged biography,” said Tuppence. “I left home early in the war, went to an officers’ hospital. Did chores, climbed, with mop and pail, to parlourmaid, then to waiting on Sisters. Met Lieutenant Thomas Beresford—bow, Tommy!—left in a blaze of glory. Drove a delivery van, a motor-lorry, then a Government office, and after the Armistice they combed me out. Since then: job-hunting.”

Tommy’s tale was shorter. “France, Mesopotamia, a second wound, hospital, Egypt; demobbed; ten long, weary months job-hunting. There aren’t any jobs. What good am I? I know nothing about business.”

“What about the colonies?”

“They wouldn’t like me.”

“Rich relations?”

“Wanted to adopt me once. I refused—for the mater’s sake.” Tuppence’s eyes grew misty. “You’re a good sort.”

“Rot! I’m desperate.”

“So am I!” burst out Tuppence. “I’ve touted, answered advertisements, screwed and saved. But it’s no good. I shall have to go home.”

“Money! I think about money morning, noon and night! There are only three ways to get it: to be left it, to marry it, or to make it. First is ruled out. I always help old ladies over crossings in case they’re eccentric millionaires—but not one has ever asked my name!”

“Marriage?” suggested Tommy.

“My best chance—so I made up my mind early to marry money. Only I never meet rich men. The boys I know are as hard up as I am.”

“Your general?”

“Keeps a bicycle shop in time of peace,” said Tuppence. “Now, you could marry a rich girl.”

“I don’t know any.”

“You can always get to know one. Tread on her foot as she comes out of the Ritz, pick up her handkerchief. If she thinks you want to know her she’s flattered.”

“You overrate my manly charms.”

“Whereas my millionaire would run for his life! No—remains to make money.”

“We’ve tried, and failed.”

“We’ve tried the orthodox ways. Suppose we try the unorthodox. Tommy, let’s be adventurers!”

“Certainly,” said Tommy. “How do we begin?”

“If we could make ourselves known, people might hire us to commit crimes for them.”

“Delightful—from a clergyman’s daughter!”

“The moral guilt would be theirs—not mine. There’s a difference between stealing a diamond necklace for yourself and being hired to steal it.”

“There wouldn’t be the least difference if you were caught!”

“I shouldn’t be caught. I’m so clever.”

“Modesty always was your besetting sin.”

“Don’t rag. Let’s form a business partnership. A joint venture! It struck me as such a romantic phrase to come across in the middle of musty old figures. It’s got an Elizabethan flavour about it--makes one think of galleons and doubloons. A joint venture! Trading under the name of the Young Adventurers, Ltd.”

“How do we get employers?”

“Advertisement. Have you a pencil?” Tommy produced a shabby green notebook. “Shall we begin: ‘Young officer, twice wounded—’”
“Certainly not.”

“Well, well! Now listen: ‘Two young adventurers for hire. Willing to do anything, go anywhere. Pay must be good.’ We might add: ‘No reasonable offer refused’—like flats.”

“Any offer we get will be unreasonable.”

“Genius! ‘No unreasonable offer refused.’ How’s that?”

“Don’t mention pay again. It looks eager.”

“It couldn’t look as eager as I feel! Now—straight through. ‘Two young adventurers for hire. Willing to do anything, go anywhere. Pay must be good. No unreasonable offer refused.’”
“It would strike me as either being a hoax, or else written by a lunatic.”

“It’s not half so insane as a thing I read this morning beginning ‘Petunia’ and signed ‘Best Boy.’”
“Times, reply to Box so-and-so. About five shillings. Here’s half a crown.”

“Shall we really try it?” said Tommy at last. “Just for the fun of the thing?”

“Tommy, you’re a sport! Let’s drink to success.” They solemnly raised cold dregs. “Here’s to our joint venture, and may it prosper!”

“The Young Adventurers, Ltd.!”

They laughed, a trifle uncertainly. Tuppence rose. “I must return to my palatial suite at the hostel.”

“Perhaps it’s time I strolled round to the Ritz,” said Tommy. “Where shall we meet?”

“Twelve to-morrow. Piccadilly Tube station.”

“My time is my own,” said Mr. Beresford magnificently.
“So long.”

“Good-bye, old thing.”

They went off in opposite directions. Half-way across St. James’s Park a man’s voice behind Tuppence made her start.

“Excuse me,” it said.

“But may I speak to you for a moment?”

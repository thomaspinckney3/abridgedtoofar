Tuppence betrayed no awkwardness in her new duties—the archdeacon's daughters were well grounded in household tasks. The cook went in deadly terror of her mistress; for the rest, she cooked like a *chef*. Mrs. Vandemeyer was expecting a guest to dinner, and Tuppence laid the table for two with some inward trepidation, fearing it might prove to be Whittington. The bell rang at a few minutes past eight; she was relieved to find the visitor was the second of the two men Tommy had set out to follow.

He gave his name as Count Stepanov. Mrs. Vandemeyer rose with a quick murmur of pleasure: "It is delightful to see you, Boris Ivanovitch."

Whilst waiting at table, Tuppence listened closely, but the talk ran only on plays and society gossip. After dinner the pair repaired to the small boudoir for coffee and liqueurs. As she retired she heard the Russian say, "New, isn't she?"

"She came in to-day. The other was a fiend. This girl seems all right."

She lingered a moment by the door, which she had carefully neglected to close.

"Quite safe, I suppose?"

"Really, Boris, you are absurdly suspicious. She's the cousin of the hall porter, or something. Nobody even dreams that I have any connection with our—mutual friend, Mr. Brown."

"For heaven's sake, be careful, Rita. That door isn't shut."

Tuppence removed herself speedily, cleared away at hospital speed, then slipped along the passage to Mrs. Vandemeyer's bedroom. Long French windows gave on a balcony running the length of the flat; she crept along it noiselessly until she reached the boudoir window, which stood a little ajar.

"With your persistent recklessness you will end by ruining us!" the Russian was saying bitterly.

"Notoriety of the right kind is the best way of disarming suspicion."

"In the meantime, you are going about everywhere with Peel Edgerton. Not only is he perhaps the most celebrated K.C. in England, but his special hobby is criminology! It is madness!"

"His eloquence has saved untold men from the gallows," said Mrs. Vandemeyer calmly. "I may need his assistance in that line myself one day."

"Give him up, Rita."

"I think not."

"Then, by Heaven—"

"You forget, Boris. I am accountable to no one. I take my orders only from—Mr. Brown."

"They say Peel Edgerton can *smell* a criminal! How do we know what is at the bottom of his sudden interest in you?"

"Reassure yourself. I am commonly accounted a beautiful woman. I assure you that is all that interests him. Besides," she added, "he is extremely rich. The 'sinews of war,' you know, Boris."

"Money—money! Sometimes I believe you would sell—*us*."

"The price would have to be enormous. Beyond the power of anyone but a millionaire to pay."

"Ah! You see, I was right!"

"My dear Boris, can you not take a joke? Touch the bell. We will have some drinks."

Tuppence beat a hasty retreat. The conversation proved beyond doubt the complicity of both Rita and Boris, but threw little light on present preoccupations; Jane Finn's name had not been mentioned.

The next morning a few brief words at the stationer's confirmed nothing was waiting for her there. It seemed incredible that Tommy should not send word. A cold hand seemed to close round her heart. But she leapt at a chance offered by Mrs. Vandemeyer.

"What day do you usually go out, Prudence?"

"Friday's my usual day, ma'am."

"And to-day is Friday. But I suppose you hardly wish—"

"I was thinking of asking you if I might."

Mrs. Vandemeyer looked at her a minute longer, then smiled. "I wish Count Stepanov could hear you. He made a suggestion about you last night." Her smile broadened, catlike. "Your request is very—typical. You can go out to-day. I shall not be dining at home."

Once out of the other's presence, Tuppence admitted to herself that she was afraid, horribly afraid, of the beautiful woman with the cruel eyes.

In the midst of a desultory polishing of her silver, the front door bell disturbed her. The visitor this time was a man of striking appearance—just a shade over average height, yet conveying the impression of a big man; his face, clean-shaven and exquisitely mobile, was stamped with an expression of power and force far beyond the ordinary. Magnetism seemed to radiate from him. Her doubts whether to put him down as actor or lawyer were soon solved when he gave his name: Sir James Peel Edgerton.

This, then, was the famous K.C. whose name was familiar all over England. She had heard it said he might one day be Prime Minister. She returned to her pantry thoughtfully; she understood Boris's agitation. Peel Edgerton would not be an easy man to deceive.

In about a quarter of an hour the bell rang and Tuppence repaired to the hall to show the visitor out. As she handed him his hat and stick, she was conscious of his eyes raking her through. He stopped in the doorway.

"Not been doing this long, eh?"

She raised her eyes, astonished. She read in his glance kindliness, and something else more difficult to fathom.

"V.A.D. and hard up, I suppose?"

"Did Mrs. Vandemeyer tell you that?" she asked suspiciously.

"No, child. The look of you told me. Good place here?"

"Very good, thank you, sir."

"Ah, but there are plenty of good places nowadays. And a change does no harm sometimes."

"Do you mean—?"

But Sir James was already on the topmost stair. He looked back with his kindly, shrewd glance. "Just a hint. That's all."

Tuppence went back to the pantry more thoughtful than ever.

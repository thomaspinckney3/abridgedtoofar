A veil might with profit be drawn over the next half-hour. Suffice it to say that no such person as Inspector Brown was known to Scotland Yard, and the photograph of Jane Finn, which would have been of the utmost value, was lost beyond recovery. Once again Mr. Brown had triumphed.

The reverse brought Julius Hersheimmer and the Young Adventurers into sudden intimacy. Tuppence and Tommy, barriers down, told him the whole story; Julius declared himself “tickled to death,” and, at Tuppence’s suggestion that they keep in touch with Jane’s only relation, they installed themselves forthwith at the Ritz. “Put like that,” said Tuppence to Tommy, “nobody could boggle at the expense!” Nobody did.

“And now—to work!” she announced next morning.

“My union does not permit me to work before eleven,” murmured Tommy behind the Daily Mail.

“Tommy, you’ll have something thrown at you. We must map out a plan of campaign.”

“There’s something of the simplicity of the truly great mind about you, Tuppence. Fire ahead.”

“To begin with—what have we to go upon?”

“Absolutely nothing,” said Tommy cheerily.

“Wrong. We have two distinct clues. First, we know one of the gang.”

“Whittington?”

“Yes. I’d recognize him anywhere.”

“Hum. A thousand to one against our meeting him by accident.”

“I’m not so sure. Coincidences run in droves. Still, we can’t rely on that. But there are places where everyone turns up sooner or later—Piccadilly Circus. I could sell flags there—”

“What about meals?”

“How like a man! Anyway, clue No. 2 is more promising. We’ve a Christian name—Rita. Whittington mentioned it. I believe Danvers was shadowed on the way over—by a woman, and a good-looking one. If she was saved, the gang knew Jane had the papers. If she was ‘Rita’—then we hunt the Lusitania survivors till we find her.”

“The first thing is the list,” said Tommy.

“I wrote to Mr. Carter. He sent it this morning.” She spread it out. “But very few Christian names. Nearly all Mrs. or Miss.”

“That complicates matters.”

“We’ll just get down to it. London first. Note the addresses of any women in or near town while I put on my hat.”

They set out. A couple of fruitless calls later—Tommy’s inspired impersonation of a municipal clerk compiling the voting register had supplied many a Christian name and slammed many a door—they lunched briefly, and Tuppence read from the notebook: “Mrs. Vandemeyer, 20 South Audley Mansions.”

“Clearly indicated,” said Tommy. “Your longing for vulgar sensation will have to be curbed, Tuppence. Mr. Brown is a wonder he has not ere now done us to death.”

“You’re more conceited than I am—with less excuse! Still, it is queer he hasn’t wreaked vengeance upon us. We pass on our way unscathed.”

“Perhaps he doesn’t think us worth bothering about,” suggested Tommy.

“How horrid! We work like moles in the dark,” she concluded with dignity. “Ha ha!”

“Ha ha!” echoed Tommy.

South Audley Mansions rose imposing just off Park Lane. No. 20’s door was opened by an elderly woman of the housekeeper type. Tommy delivered his glib formula.

“Christian name?”

“Margaret.”

Tommy spelt it; the woman corrected him: “No—g u e.”

“Oh, Marguerite—the French way. We had her down as ‘Rita’ Vandemeyer—”

“She’s mostly called that, sir, but Marguerite’s her name.”

“Thank you. That’s all.”

Hardly containing himself, Tommy rejoined Tuppence. “You heard?”

“Yes. Oh, Tommy!”

“It’s so lovely,” she cried, “to think of things—and then for them really to happen!”

Her hand still in his, they reached the entrance hall. Voices and footsteps sounded from above. To Tommy’s surprise Tuppence jerked him into the shadow by the lift.

“Hush!”

Two men descended and passed out. Tuppence’s fingers tightened. “Quick—follow them. I daren’t. He might recognize me. I don’t know the other, but the big one is Whittington.”

Tommy nodded once and was gone.

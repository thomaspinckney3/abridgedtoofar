Sir James brushed past Julius and bent over the fallen woman.

“Heart,” he said sharply.

“Brandy—and quickly, or she’ll slip through our fingers.”

Julius made for the washstand.

“Not there,” said Tuppence. “In the tantalus in the dining-room. Second door down the passage.”

Between them Sir James and Tuppence carried Mrs. Vandemeyer to the bed. Water on the face did nothing. The lawyer kept a finger on her pulse.

“Touch and go,” he muttered.

Julius came back with a half-glass of brandy. While Tuppence raised the heavy head, Sir James forced a little between the closed lips. Presently the woman’s lids fluttered; colour crept back. She tried to sit up, then fell with a groan, a hand at her side.

“It’s my heart,” she whispered.

“I mustn’t talk.”

She lay still. Sir James watched the wrist a minute longer, then nodded.

“She’ll do now.”

All three moved away, and stood together talking in low voices. One and all were conscious of a certain feeling of anticlimax. Clearly any scheme for cross-questioning the lady was out of the question for the moment.

Tuppence told how Mrs. Vandemeyer had been on the point of naming Mr. Brown, and had promised to disclose Jane Finn’s hiding-place. Julius was jubilant.

“That’s fine, Miss Tuppence. I guess that hundred thousand pounds will look just as good in the morning to the lady as it did over night. She won’t speak without the cash, you bet!”

Sir James was more cautious. “I must confess I could wish we had not interrupted when we did. Still—only a matter of waiting till morning.”

He looked across at the inert figure. Through half-shut lids Mrs. Vandemeyer’s face wore for a second such fear and malice that Tuppence’s protest died. The look vanished instantly; the woman seemed inert as before. Nevertheless the girl determined to be on the alert.

“We must not leave the flat,” said Sir James quietly. “If only for Mrs. Vandemeyer’s sake.”

Julius stared. “You think he’d get after her? Between now and to-morrow morning. How could he know, even?”

“You forget your own dictaphone,” said Sir James dryly. “We have a formidable adversary. If we exercise all due care, there is a good chance of delivering him into our hands. But we must neglect no precaution. Miss Tuppence should go to bed; you and I, Mr. Hersheimmer, will share the vigil.”

Tuppence, turning to the bed, saw the lids lift again. The lips shaped a breath:

“Don’t—leave——”

“Sleepy,” the whisper broke, then tried again. Tuppence bent lower.

“Mr.—Brown——”

The eyes sent an agonized message. On impulse Tuppence said quickly:

“I shan’t leave the flat. I shall sit up all night.”

A flash of relief; the lids fell. Apparently Mrs. Vandemeyer slept. But her words had awakened a new uneasiness in Tuppence. What had she meant by that low murmur: “Mr. Brown?” Tuppence caught herself nervously looking over her shoulder. The big wardrobe loomed up in a sinister fashion before her eyes. Plenty of room for a man to hide in that.... Half-ashamed of herself, Tuppence pulled it open and looked inside. No one--of course! She stooped down and looked under the bed. There was no other possible hiding-place.

Tuppence gave her familiar shake of the shoulders. It was absurd, this giving way to nerves! Slowly she went out of the room. Julius and Sir James were talking in a low voice. Sir James turned to her.

“Lock the door outside, please, Miss Tuppence, and take the key. There must be no chance of anyone entering.”

“Say,” remarked Julius suddenly, “there’s Tuppence’s bright boy below. I’ll ease his mind.”

“How did you get in?” asked Tuppence.

“Albert phoned me; I ran round for Sir James; we came up by the coal lift.” Julius departed.

“Where do we take up our quarters?” asked Sir James.

“Mrs. Vandemeyer’s boudoir,” said Tuppence, leading the way.

“Very well. But do go to bed, child.”

“I couldn’t. I should dream of Mr. Brown all night!”

Julius returned presently, having reassured and rewarded Albert. Tuppence refusing bed, he foraged and came back with a cold pie and three plates. After they had eaten, her spirits rose.

“And now, Miss Tuppence, your adventures,” said Sir James.

She told her tale with complacence; Julius interjected an admiring “Bully.” Sir James’s quiet “Well done, Miss Tuppence,” brought the warm colour to her face.

“There’s one point I don’t get,” said Julius. “What put her up to clearing out?”

“I don’t know,” confessed Tuppence.

“The room was in disorder,” mused Sir James. “Looks as though her flight was unpremeditated—as though she got a sudden warning.”

“Mr. Brown, I suppose,” said Julius scoffingly.

“Why not?” returned Sir James. “Remember, you yourself have once been worsted by him.”

Julius flushed. “I feel mad when I think how I handed out Jane’s photograph to him like a lamb. If I ever lay hands on it again, I’ll freeze on to it like—like hell!”

“That contingency is likely to be remote,” said the other dryly.

“In any case, it’s the original I’m after. Where do you think she can be?”

“Impossible to say,” said Sir James. “But I have a very good idea where she has been.”

“Where?”

“At the scene of your nocturnal adventures—the Bournemouth nursing home.”

“Impossible. I asked.”

“You asked for ‘Jane Finn.’ If the girl had been placed there it would almost certainly be under an assumed name.”

“Bully for you! I never thought of that.”

“Perhaps the doctor’s in it too,” suggested Tuppence.

“I don’t think so,” said Julius. “I took to him. Dr. Hall’s all right.”

“Hall?” Sir James lifted his brows. “Curious—very curious. I met him this morning. He is staying at the Métropole. You did not mention his name this afternoon, or I would have given you a card.”

“I’m a mutt,” said Julius humbly. “But it doesn’t matter now. We’ve got Mrs. Vandemeyer on a string.”

“Yes,” said Tuppence, without conviction.

Silence fell. The night took hold—creaks, rustlings. Suddenly Tuppence sprang up.

“I can’t help it. I know Mr. Brown’s somewhere in the flat! I can feel him.”

“Why, Tuppence,” protested Julius, “this door’s open to the hall. No one could have come in the front way without our seeing.”

“With due deference to your feelings—and mine,” said Sir James gravely, “I do not see how it is humanly possible for anyone to be here without our knowledge.”

“Sitting up at night is always rather jumpy,” admitted Tuppence.

“We are like people at a séance,” observed Sir James. “With a medium, we might get marvellous results.”

“Do you believe in spiritualism?” asked Tuppence.

“There is some truth in it,” said the lawyer, shrugging. “But most of the testimony would not pass muster in the witness-box.”

With the first grey, Sir James drew the curtains. They beheld the slow rising of the sun over the sleeping city. Dreads seemed absurd; Tuppence’s spirits flew.

“Hooray! It’s going to be gorgeous. And we shall find Tommy. And Jane Finn. And everything will be lovely. I shall ask Mr. Carter if I can’t be made a Dame!”

At seven she volunteered to make tea, and came back with a tray and four cups.

“Who’s the other cup for?” asked Julius.

“The prisoner, of course.”

“Taking tea seems a kind of anticlimax,” Julius mused.

“Come along, both of you. We don’t know what mood she’ll wake in.”

Sir James and Julius went to the door with her.

Tuppence turned the key she had carried all night. “Supposing, after all, she’s escaped?” she whispered.

“Plumb impossible,” said Julius. Sir James said nothing.

They entered. Mrs. Vandemeyer lay on the bed. Relief—Tuppence went to pull up the blinds, then turned. The figure had not moved. A fear clutched her; she ran to the bed. The hand she lifted was ice-cold.

Her cry brought the others. A very few minutes sufficed. Mrs. Vandemeyer was dead—must have been dead for hours. She had died in her sleep.

“If that isn’t the cruellest luck,” cried Julius.

Sir James’s eyes had a curious gleam. “If it is luck,” he said.

“You don’t think—Why, that’s plumb impossible—no one could have got in.”

“I don’t see how,” admitted the lawyer. “And yet—she is on the point of betraying Mr. Brown, and—she dies. Is it only chance?”

“But how——”

“Yes—how! That we must find out.” He stood there silently, gently stroking his chin. “We must find out,” he said quietly, and Tuppence felt that if she was Mr. Brown she would not like the tone of those simple words.

Julius glanced at the window. “The window’s open. Do you think——”

“The balcony only runs to the boudoir,” said Tuppence. “We were there.”

“Mr. Brown’s methods are not so crude,” Sir James cut Julius short. “In the meantime we must send for a doctor. Before we do so, is there anything here that might be of value to us?”

The grate held a charred mass; papers had been burned on the eve of flight. They searched the other rooms in haste. Nothing.

“There’s that,” said Tuppence, pointing to a small old-fashioned wall safe. “It’s for jewellery—but there might be something else.”

The key was in the lock. Julius swung the door open and searched long. Tuppence waited.

“Well?” she said impatiently.

A pause. Julius withdrew his head and shut the door.

“Nothing,” he said.

A brisk young doctor came, deferential to Sir James.

“Heart failure—or possibly an overdose of some sleeping-draught.” He sniffed. “There’s a distinct odour of chloral.”

Tuppence remembered the little glass she had upset. She went to the washstand. The bottle from which Mrs. Vandemeyer had poured a few drops the night before had been three parts full. Now— _it was empty_.

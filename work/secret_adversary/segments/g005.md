## Chapter 1

THE VIGIL

Sir James brushed past Julius and bent over the fallen woman.

"Heart," he said sharply. "Brandy—quickly, or she'll slip through our fingers."

Tuppence directed him to the tantalus in the dining-room. Between them they lifted Mrs. Vandemeyer to the bed and dashed water on her face, but with no result. Julius returned with the spirit, and Sir James forced a little between her closed lips. Finally she opened her eyes feebly, drank, and the colour came back to her white cheeks.

"It's my heart," she whispered. "I mustn't talk." She lay back with closed eyes.

Sir James kept his finger on her wrist a moment longer, then withdrew it with a nod. "She'll do now."

The three drew aside, conscious of anticlimax. Tuppence related how Mrs. Vandemeyer had agreed to disclose Mr. Brown's identity and Jane Finn's whereabouts.

"That hundred thousand pounds will look just as good in the morning," said Julius. "She won't speak without the cash anyway, you bet."

"I confess I wish we had not interrupted at the minute we did," said Sir James. "Still, it cannot be helped."

"We mustn't leave the flat," Tuppence said. "She seemed terribly frightened of Mr. Brown. She looked round and said even walls had ears."

"She meant a dictaphone, maybe," said Julius.

"Miss Tuppence is right," Sir James said quietly. "We have a formidable adversary. We must not leave the flat—if only for Mrs. Vandemeyer's sake. I would suggest Miss Tuppence go to bed, and that Mr. Hersheimmer and I share the vigil."

Tuppence was about to protest when she glanced at the bed. Mrs. Vandemeyer's eyes were half-open, her face such a mingling of fear and malevolence that the words froze on Tuppence's lips. For a moment she wondered whether the faint had been a sham; then the expression vanished as by magic, and the woman lay inert and motionless as before.

As they prepared to leave, Mrs. Vandemeyer lifted her lids. She seemed to struggle to speak. Tuppence bent close.

"Don't—leave—" Then, almost inaudibly: "Mr.—Brown—"

Tuppence said quickly, "I shan't leave the flat. I shall sit up all night."

A flash of relief showed before the lids descended once more.

In the passage Sir James said, "Lock the door on the outside, Miss Tuppence, and take the key. There must be no chance of anyone entering that room."

His gravity impressed them. Tuppence pulled open the wardrobe—of course, no one—and stooped to look beneath the bed before she left, then chid herself for nerves.

She suggested Mrs. Vandemeyer's boudoir for their quarters. Sir James pressed her again to sleep; she shook her head. "I should dream of Mr. Brown all night."

After a hearty meal of cold pie that Julius produced from the larder, Tuppence narrated her adventures. Sir James's quiet "Well done, Miss Tuppence" made her flush with pleasure.

"What put her up to clearing out?" Julius asked.

"The room was in great disorder," Sir James said. "Almost as though she got a sudden warning to go from someone."

"Mr. Brown, I suppose," said Julius scoffingly.

The lawyer looked at him deliberately. "Why not? Remember, you yourself have once been worsted by him."

"In any case, it's the original I'm out after," Julius said. "Where do you think Jane is, Sir James?"

"I've a very good idea where she has been. The scene of your nocturnal adventures—the Bournemouth nursing home."

"Impossible. I asked—"

"You asked if anyone of the name of Jane Finn had been there. If she was placed there it would almost certainly be under an assumed name."

"Bully for you. I never thought of that."

"Perhaps the doctor's in it too," suggested Tuppence.

"I took to him at once. Dr. Hall's all right."

"Hall?" said Sir James. "Curious—I happened to meet him this morning. Staying at the Métropole. You did not mention his name this afternoon, or I would have suggested you go to him for further information."

A silence settled on the party. Little by little the magic of the night took hold—creaks of furniture, rustlings in curtains. Tuppence sprang up.

"I can't help it. I know Mr. Brown's somewhere in the flat. I can _feel_ him."

"This door's open into the hall," Julius said. "No one could have come in by the front door without our seeing him."

"With due deference to your feelings, Miss Tuppence," said Sir James gravely, "I do not see how it is humanly possible for anyone to be in the flat without our knowledge. We are in the condition of people holding a séance. Perhaps if a medium were present we might get some marvellous results."

The hours drew on. With the first faint glimmerings of dawn Sir James drew aside the curtains, and the dreads of the night seemed absurd.

"Hooray!" said Tuppence. "It's going to be a gorgeous day. We shall find Tommy. And Jane Finn. And everything will be lovely."

At seven she made tea—four cups, the fourth for "the prisoner."

"Supposing she's escaped?" she whispered at the door, the key in her hand.

"Plumb impossible," replied Julius.

But Sir James said nothing.

Tuppence entered, drew a long breath as she saw the figure on the bed. "Good morning. I've brought you some tea."

No answer. Tuppence put down the cup, drew the blinds. When she turned, Mrs. Vandemeyer still lay without movement. The hand she lifted was cold as ice.

Mrs. Vandemeyer was dead—must have been dead some hours. She had evidently died in her sleep.

"If that isn't the cruellest luck," cried Julius.

Sir James was calmer, but there was a curious gleam in his eyes. "If it is luck."

"You don't think—but, say, that's plumb impossible. No one could have got in."

"And yet—she is on the point of betraying Mr. Brown, and—she dies. Is it only chance?" He stood there, gently stroking his chin. "We must find out."

A charred mass in the grate showed Mrs. Vandemeyer had been burning papers on the eve of her flight. They searched—nothing of importance remained, though there was a small old-fashioned safe let into the wall.

"It's for jewellery, I believe," said Tuppence, "but there might be something else."

The key was in the lock. Julius swung open the door and searched inside. He was some time over the task.

"Well?" said Tuppence impatiently.

There was a pause before he withdrew his head and shut the door. "Nothing."

A young doctor arrived, deferential to Sir James. "Heart failure, or possibly an overdose of some sleeping-draught." He sniffed. "Rather an odour of chloral in the air."

A new thought drove Tuppence to the washstand. She found the little bottle from which Mrs. Vandemeyer had poured a few drops. It had been three parts full. Now— _it was empty_.

## Chapter 2

A CONSULTATION

Nothing was more bewildering to Tuppence than the ease with which everything was arranged through Sir James's skilful handling. The doctor accepted that Mrs. Vandemeyer had taken an accidental overdose of chloral and doubted whether an inquest would be necessary. A nurse arrived to take charge, and they left the ill-omened building.

"And what now?" asked Julius, with a gesture of despair.

"There is still the chance that Dr. Hall may be able to tell us something," said Sir James. "I told you he is staying at the Métropole. I suggest we call upon him after a bath and breakfast."

A little after eleven they drew up before the Métropole. The little doctor came hurrying.

"Ah, my young friend of the tree episode! Ankle all right, eh?"

"Cured owing to your skilful treatment, doc."

"Can we have a word with you in private?" asked Sir James.

He led them to a quiet room, and Sir James came at once to the point.

"Dr. Hall, I am very anxious to find a young lady who has been at one time in your establishment at Bournemouth. She would almost certainly have been sent to you under an assumed name. Are you acquainted with a Mrs. Vandemeyer?"

"Mrs. Vandemeyer, of 20 South Audley Mansions? I know her slightly."

"You are not aware that she is dead? She took an overdose of chloral last night."

"Dear, dear, I had no idea. Very sad."

"Is it not a fact that she committed a young relative of hers to your charge?"

Julius leaned forward eagerly.

"That is the case," said the doctor. "She came to me in June or July of 1915, under the name of Janet Vandemeyer. I understood her to be a niece. Mrs. Vandemeyer told me the girl had been on the _Lusitania_ when that ill-fated ship was sunk, and had suffered a severe shock in consequence."

"We're on the right track, I think?" Sir James said.

"As I said before, I'm a mutt!" returned Julius.

"You spoke of wanting a statement from her," said the doctor. "Supposing she is not able to give one? She is perfectly sane—but if you want a statement concerning any events prior to May 7, 1915, she will not be able to give it to you. Janet Vandemeyer is suffering from a complete loss of memory."

_"What?"_

"Quite so. After May 7, 1915, her memory is as good as yours or mine. Before that, nothing. She did not know her own name, or where she had come from. She couldn't even speak her own tongue."

"Sooner or later," he went on, "the memory will return—as suddenly as it went. But in all probability the girl will have entirely forgotten the intervening period, and take up life where she left off—at the sinking of the _Lusitania_."

"And when do you expect this to happen?"

The doctor shrugged. "Sometimes a matter of months, sometimes as long as twenty years. Sometimes another shock does the trick. One restores what the other took away."

"Another shock, eh?" said Julius thoughtfully. He fell into a brown study, then suddenly hit the table such a resounding bang that everyone jumped.

"I've got it! Doc, what about your medical opinion on this. Say Jane was to cross the herring pond again, and the same thing was to happen—the submarine, the sinking ship, every one taking to the boats. Wouldn't that give a mighty big bump to her subconscious self, and start it functioning again?"

"In my own opinion, it would be successful. Unfortunately there is no chance of the conditions repeating themselves."

"Not by nature, doc. But I'm talking about art. Hire a liner, hire some passengers, hire a submarine—it's done every day in the States for the movies. By the time she's got a life-belt on her, with a well-drilled lot of artistes doing the hysterical stunt on deck—why, she ought to be right back where she was in May, 1915."

"But the expense, my dear sir!" Dr. Hall's voice rose. "It will be _colossal!_"

"Money doesn't worry me any," explained Julius simply.

Dr. Hall turned an appealing face to Sir James, who smiled slightly. "Mr. Hersheimmer is very well off—very well off indeed."

The doctor's glance came back to Julius with a new deference.

"Very remarkable plan. The movies—of course! And you really mean to carry it out?"

"You bet your bottom dollar I do. Can we get on the long distance to your place right away, and ask them to send her up?"

The doctor stared. "I beg your pardon. I thought you understood. Miss Vandemeyer is no longer under my care."

## Chapter 3

TUPPENCE RECEIVES A PROPOSAL

Julius sprang up. "What?"

"I thought you were aware. To-day is Monday, is it not? It must have been last Wednesday—the same evening that you—er—fell out of my tree. A very urgent message arrived from Mrs. Vandemeyer. The young lady and the nurse left by the night train."

Julius sank back. "Nurse Edith—left with a patient—I remember. My God, to have been so near."

Dr. Hall looked bewildered. "Is the young lady not with her aunt, after all?"

Tuppence was about to speak when a warning glance from Sir James made her hold her tongue. The lawyer rose.

"I'm much obliged to you, Hall. I'm afraid we're now in the position of having to track Miss Vandemeyer anew."

Outside they stood by the car.

"How maddening," cried Tuppence. "Julius must have been actually under the same roof with her for a few hours."

"I was a darned idiot."

"You might advertise for the nurse," said Sir James. "That is the only course I can suggest. Otherwise there is nothing to be done."

"Nothing?" said Tuppence blankly. "And—Tommy?"

"We must hope for the best." Over her downcast head his eyes met Julius's, and almost imperceptibly he shook his head. "You must let me know if anything further comes to light."

"You are going away?"

"To Scotland. My dear young lady, I can do nothing more, I fear. Our clues have all ended in thin air."

His words gave Tuppence an extraordinarily desolate feeling. As Julius bent over the car, Sir James's keen eyes rested on her face.

"Don't be too disconsolate, Miss Tuppence," he said in a low voice. "Remember, holiday-time isn't always all playtime. One sometimes manages to put in some work as well."

Something in his tone made her glance up sharply.

"No, I shan't say any more. Great mistake to say too much. Remember that. Never tell all you know—not even to the person you know best."

He strode away. Was this a hint? Did he mean that, secretly, he would be working on it still?

"You're looking kind of thoughtful," remarked Julius as they started off. "Did the old guy say anything more?"

Tuppence opened her mouth impulsively, then shut it again. Sir James's words sounded in her ears. And like a flash there came another memory: Julius before the safe in the flat, her question and the pause before his reply, "Nothing." Was there really nothing? Or had he found something he wished to keep to himself? If he could make a reservation, so could she.

"Nothing particular," she replied.

For a while they ran on under the trees in silence.

"Say, Miss Tuppence, do you think I'm ever going to find Jane?" Julius spoke in a discouraged voice. "Sir James to-day hadn't got any hope at all, I could see that. I've half a mind to go back to the States."

"Oh no! We've got to find Tommy."

"I sure forgot Beresford. We must find him. But after—Miss Tuppence, there's something I'd like to ask you. You and Beresford. What about it?"

"I don't understand you," replied Tuppence with dignity. "And anyway, you're wrong."

"Not got a sort of kindly feeling for one another?"

"Certainly not. Tommy and I are friends—nothing more."

"Supposing we never find Beresford. Supposing he's—dead. What are you going to do?"

"I don't know," said Tuppence forlornly.

"What about marriage? What about me?"

" _You?_ "

"Sure thing."

"Oh, I couldn't!"

"Why not?"

"It would seem so unfair."

"I admire you immensely, Miss Tuppence, more than any girl I've ever met. You're so darned plucky. Say the word, and we'll run round to some high-class jeweller and fix up the ring business."

"I can't," gasped Tuppence.

"Because of Beresford?"

"No, no, _no!_"

Tuppence shook her head violently. "Thanking you very much, but I think I'd better say no."

"I'd be obliged if you'd think it over until to-morrow."

Neither spoke again until they reached the Ritz.

Tuppence went upstairs, sat in front of the glass, and stared at her reflection.

"Fool," she murmured. "Little fool. Everything you want—everything you've ever hoped for, and you bleat out 'no' like an idiotic little sheep. It's your one chance. Why don't you take it? Grab it? Snatch at it? What more do you want?"

Her eyes fell on a small snapshot of Tommy in a shabby frame on her dressing-table. She held it to her lips and burst into a fit of sobbing.

"Oh, Tommy, Tommy, I do love you so—and I may never see you again."

After five minutes she sat up, blew her nose, pushed back her hair.

"I seem to have fallen in love with an idiot of a boy who probably doesn't care two straws about me. He'd never have dared to say so. I've always jumped on sentiment—and here I am being more sentimental than anybody. What idiots girls are. I wonder if he did find anything in that safe—"

Tuppence reviewed the events of last night carefully and persistently. Somehow they seemed bound up with Sir James's enigmatical words.

Suddenly she gave a great start—the colour faded out of her face. Her eyes, fascinated, gazed in front of her, the pupils dilated.

"Impossible," she murmured. "Impossible! I must be going mad even to think of such a thing—"

Monstrous—yet it explained everything.

After a moment's reflection she sat down and wrote a note, weighing each word as she did so. She slipped it into an envelope addressed to Julius and went down the passage to his sitting-room. The room was empty. She left the note on the table.

A small page-boy was waiting outside her own door when she returned.

"Telegram for you, miss."

Tuppence tore it open carelessly. Then she gave a cry. The telegram was from Tommy.

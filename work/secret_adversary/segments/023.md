The Prime Minister tapped the desk with nervous fingers. "Do you really mean things are not so desperate after all?"

"So this lad seems to think." Mr. Carter handed the letter across. It was written in a sprawling boyish hand.

> DEAR MR. CARTER,
>
> Something's turned up that has given me a jar. If my conclusions are right, that girl at Manchester was just a plant — the whole thing prearranged to make us think the game was up. Therefore we must have been pretty hot on the scent.
>
> I think I know who the real Jane Finn is, and I've an idea where the papers are. I enclose it in a sealed envelope; please don't open it until midnight on the 28th. Tuppence's drowning is a plant too. As a last chance they'll let Jane Finn escape, hoping she'll lead them to the cache; but if they know the papers have been recovered, neither girl's life will be worth an hour's purchase.
>
> I want a repeat of that telegram sent to Tuppence at the Ritz. And please have that house in Soho watched day and night.
>
> Yours,
> THOMAS BERESFORD.

"The enclosure?"

"In the vaults of the Bank. I am taking no chances."

"Hadn't we better open it now? Secure the document."

"Once it's known, I wouldn't give that"—Carter snapped his fingers—"for the lives of those two girls. The boy trusted me; I shan't let him down."

"What's he like?"

"Outwardly an ordinary clean-limbed, rather block-headed young Englishman, slow in his mental processes — but impossible to lead astray through his imagination; he hasn't got any. The little lady's quite different. More intuition and less common sense. Pace and stamina."

"And it is this — boy who will defeat the master criminal of our time?"

"This boy, as you say. But I sometimes fancy I see a shadow behind. Peel Edgerton — working in the dark. I've always felt that if anyone was to run Mr. Brown to earth, Peel Edgerton would be the man. He sent me an odd request the other day — a cutting about a man's body found near the docks in New York, badly disfigured, never identified."

"You think the matters connected?"

"Somehow I do."

Sir James was shown in. The Premier rose with a half-whimsical thought: *My successor, perhaps.*

"You've seen young Beresford, I suppose?" said Carter.

"You suppose wrong. He rang me up." Sir James stroked his chin. "He thanked me for a letter offering him a job, then reminded me of something I'd said at Manchester about that bogus telegram. He had discovered a photograph in a drawer in Mr. Hersheimmer's room. I asked if it bore a Californian photographer's mark. He replied: 'You're on to it, sir. It had.' The original was the French girl, Annette, who saved his life."

"What?"

"Exactly. He had put the photograph back where he found it — distinctly good, that. Once Manchester was proved a plant, everything was altered. He couldn't trust his judgment about Miss Cowley, however. I told him there was a very decided chance she was alive, and advised him to apply to you for the original wire."

Carter took a sheet out and read aloud: "Come at once, Astley Priors, Gatehouse, Kent. Great developments — TOMMY."

"Very simple, and very ingenious. The one important clue they overlooked — the page-boy's statement that Miss Cowley drove to Charing Cross. They took it for granted he had made a mistake."

"Then young Beresford is now—"

"At Gatehouse, Kent."

"I rather wonder you're not there too."

"I'm busy on a case. Any more facts about that American chap?"

"Is it important to find out who he was?"

"Oh, I know who he was. I can't prove it yet — but I know."

The other two asked no questions.

"But how," said the Prime Minister suddenly, "did that photograph come to be in Mr. Hersheimmer's drawer?"

"Perhaps it never left it," suggested the lawyer gently.

"But the bogus inspector — Inspector Brown, who conducted the search?"

"Ah," said Sir James thoughtfully. He rose. "I mustn't keep you. I must get back to my case."

Two days later Julius Hersheimmer returned from Manchester. A note from Tommy lay on his table: sorry for losing his temper, off to take a job in the Argentine.

A peculiar smile lingered on Julius's face. He threw the letter into the waste-paper basket.

"The darned fool!" he murmured.

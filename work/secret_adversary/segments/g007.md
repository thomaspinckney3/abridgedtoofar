## Chapter 1

The Prime Minister tapped the desk with nervous fingers. "Do you really mean things are not so desperate after all?"

"So this lad seems to think." Mr. Carter handed the letter across. It was written in a sprawling boyish hand.

> DEAR MR. CARTER,
>
> Something's turned up that has given me a jar. If my conclusions are right, that girl at Manchester was just a plant — the whole thing prearranged to make us think the game was up. Therefore we must have been pretty hot on the scent.
>
> I think I know who the real Jane Finn is, and I've an idea where the papers are. I enclose it in a sealed envelope; please don't open it until midnight on the 28th. Tuppence's drowning is a plant too. As a last chance they'll let Jane Finn escape, hoping she'll lead them to the cache; but if they know the papers have been recovered, neither girl's life will be worth an hour's purchase.
>
> I want a repeat of that telegram sent to Tuppence at the Ritz. And please have that house in Soho watched day and night.
>
> Yours,
> THOMAS BERESFORD.

"The enclosure?"

"In the vaults of the Bank. I am taking no chances."

"Hadn't we better open it now? Secure the document."

"Once it's known, I wouldn't give that"—Carter snapped his fingers—"for the lives of those two girls. The boy trusted me; I shan't let him down."

"What's he like?"

"Outwardly an ordinary clean-limbed, rather block-headed young Englishman, slow in his mental processes — but impossible to lead astray through his imagination; he hasn't got any. The little lady's quite different. More intuition and less common sense. Pace and stamina."

"And it is this — boy who will defeat the master criminal of our time?"

"This boy, as you say. But I sometimes fancy I see a shadow behind. Peel Edgerton — working in the dark. I've always felt that if anyone was to run Mr. Brown to earth, Peel Edgerton would be the man. He sent me an odd request the other day — a cutting about a man's body found near the docks in New York, badly disfigured, never identified."

"You think the matters connected?"

"Somehow I do."

Sir James was shown in. The Premier rose with a half-whimsical thought: *My successor, perhaps.*

"You've seen young Beresford, I suppose?" said Carter.

"You suppose wrong. He rang me up." Sir James stroked his chin. "He thanked me for a letter offering him a job, then reminded me of something I'd said at Manchester about that bogus telegram. He had discovered a photograph in a drawer in Mr. Hersheimmer's room. I asked if it bore a Californian photographer's mark. He replied: 'You're on to it, sir. It had.' The original was the French girl, Annette, who saved his life."

"What?"

"Exactly. He had put the photograph back where he found it — distinctly good, that. Once Manchester was proved a plant, everything was altered. He couldn't trust his judgment about Miss Cowley, however. I told him there was a very decided chance she was alive, and advised him to apply to you for the original wire."

Carter took a sheet out and read aloud: "Come at once, Astley Priors, Gatehouse, Kent. Great developments — TOMMY."

"Very simple, and very ingenious. The one important clue they overlooked — the page-boy's statement that Miss Cowley drove to Charing Cross. They took it for granted he had made a mistake."

"Then young Beresford is now—"

"At Gatehouse, Kent."

"I rather wonder you're not there too."

"I'm busy on a case. Any more facts about that American chap?"

"Is it important to find out who he was?"

"Oh, I know who he was. I can't prove it yet — but I know."

The other two asked no questions.

"But how," said the Prime Minister suddenly, "did that photograph come to be in Mr. Hersheimmer's drawer?"

"Perhaps it never left it," suggested the lawyer gently.

"But the bogus inspector?"

"Ah," said Sir James thoughtfully. He rose. "I mustn't keep you. I must get back to my case."

Two days later Julius Hersheimmer returned from Manchester. A note from Tommy lay on his table: sorry for losing his temper, off to take a job in the Argentine.

A peculiar smile lingered on Julius's face. He threw the letter into the waste-paper basket.

"The darned fool!" he murmured.

## Chapter 2

After ringing up Sir James, Tommy enlisted Tuppence's friend Albert, and the two took quarters at the inn in Gatehouse. Astley Priors, the village volunteered, was the property of a Dr. Adams — retired, but kept a few private patients ("balmy ones — you understand"). A popular figure, given to subscriptions, "a very pleasant, affable gentleman." Scientific; always visitors.

The volubility raised Tommy's doubts. Suppose it was all a gigantic mistake? He inquired carefully if there was a young lady amongst the patients, describing Tuppence and Annette. Nothing. The patients were seldom seen outside the grounds.

That evening they explored. The grounds, like those of any private house after nightfall, seemed untenanted. They reached a shrubbery near the house unmolested. The blinds of the dining-room window were up. A normal, pleasant company sat round the table; through the open window floated a heated discussion of county cricket. Again Tommy felt the cold chill of uncertainty. The fair-bearded gentleman at the head of the table looked singularly honest.

The next morning Albert took the place of the greengrocer's boy and ingratiated himself with the cook. Repeating the trick the following day, he brought confirmation: a French young lady was staying in the house.

Time pressed. To-day was the 27th; the 29th, the much-talked-of "Labour Day," loomed. Newspapers were full of sensational hints of a Labour *coup d'état*. With the fatal document in Mr. Brown's hands, public opinion would swing to the extremists. Failing that, the battle was even — at the cost of great suffering. But Tommy nourished a more preposterous dream: with Mr. Brown unmasked, the whole organization would crumble in panic. The strange permeating influence of the unseen chief held it together.

"This is a one-man show," said Tommy to himself. "The thing to do is to get hold of the man."

That evening they once more penetrated the grounds. As they approached, Tommy gave a sudden gasp. On the second floor a silhouette stood between window and light. He would have known it anywhere. Tuppence was in that house.

"Stay here. When I begin to sing, watch that window."

He retreated to the main drive, and began with a deep roar, coupled with an unsteady gait:

> *I am a Soldier*
> *A jolly British Soldier;*
> *You can see that I'm a Soldier by my feet…*

It had been a favourite on the gramophone in Tuppence's hospital days. Tommy had not a note of music in his voice, but his lungs were excellent.

Presently an unimpeachable butler issued from the front door with an equally unimpeachable footman. They ran him neatly down the drive and out of the gate, threatening police if he intruded again. It was beautifully done — only, as it happened, the butler was Whittington.

Tommy retired to the inn. Albert reappeared with a scrap of paper wrapped round a letterweight: *To-morrow — same time.*

"I wrote a message and chucked it through the window," he added breathlessly. "Said we was at the inn. If she could get away, to come there and croak like a frog."

"Your zeal will be the undoing of us, Albert. That butler's an old friend of mine — I bet he knew who I was. It's not their game to show suspicion. They don't want to discourage me altogether. I'm a pawn in their game. If the spider lets the fly walk out too easily, the fly might suspect a put-up job. But later, Mr. T. Beresford had better look out."

About twelve, his calm was shaken. Someone was demanding him in the bar — a rude-looking carter coated with mud.

"Might this be for you, sir?" The note was very dirty: *Take this to the gentleman at the inn near Astley Priors. He will give you ten shillings.*

The handwriting was Tuppence's. Tommy paid him and unfastened it.

> DEAR TOMMY,
> I knew it was you last night. Don't go this evening. They'll be lying in wait for you. They're taking us away this morning. I heard something about Wales — Holyhead, I think. I'll drop this on the road if I get a chance. Annette told me how you'd escaped. Buck up.
> Yours,
> TWOPENCE.

"Pack my bag!" Tommy shouted. "We're off!"

Holyhead. Did that mean—? He read the note again slowly. The boots above continued busy.

"Albert! I'm a damned fool! Unpack that bag!"

He smoothed out the note thoughtfully.

"Yes, a damned fool. But so's some one else. And at last I know who it is."

## Chapter 3

In his suite at Claridge's, Kramenin was informed that a Mr. Julius P. Hersheimmer was below. The name promised a millionaire several times over.

"Bring him up."

Julius was escorted in. "Monsieur Kramenin? I've got some very important business I'd like to talk over with you, if I can see you alone."

The secretary was dismissed. Julius watched him go, closed the door, and came back to the centre of the room.

"Now, Mr. Hersheimmer, perhaps you will be so kind as to come to the point."

"I guess that won't take a minute." Then, with an abrupt change of manner: "Hands up — or I shoot."

Kramenin stared into the big automatic, then with almost comical haste flung up his hands. In that instant Julius had taken his measure: an abject physical coward — the rest would be easy.

"This is an outrage!" cried Kramenin in a high hysterical voice. "Do you mean to kill me?"

"Not if you keep your voice down. I'm not proposing to kill you this trip — that is, if you're reasonable."

"What do you want? Money?"

"No. I want Jane Finn."

"Jane Finn? I — never heard of her."

"You're a darned liar. And Little Willie here is just hopping mad to go off."

The Russian wilted. "Granted I do know who you mean — what of it?"

"You will tell me where she is to be found."

"I daren't. Mr. Brown — he is a man to fear. I have seen him. Spoken to him face to face. I did not know it until afterwards. He was one of a crowd. He knows everything, and his vengeance is swift. Even I — Kramenin! — would not be exempt."

"He'll never know," said Julius. He raised the revolver. "I'm going to count five. One — two — three — four—"

"Do not shoot! I will do all you wish. The girl is at Gatehouse, in Kent. Astley Priors, the place is called. The little fool has lost her memory, curse her."

"And the other girl, the one you decoyed away over a week ago?"

"She's there too."

"Isn't it all panning out beautifully? And a lovely night for the run."

"What run?"

"Down to Gatehouse. I'm not such a kid as to leave you here to ring up your friends."

The Russian was hustled into a coat and down to the waiting car. Julius warned him that the chauffeur — a naval man whose brother had been murdered by Bolsheviks — would not assist any escape. To pass the time on the run, Julius narrated, with picturesque inaccuracy, a tale of a man he had once shot in Arizona, a tough from 'Frisco, and an episode in the Rockies. By the end of the hour, Kramenin was more dead than alive.

The car swept up the drive of Astley Priors.

"Turn the car first. Keep the engine going, and be ready to scoot like hell."

The front door opened. Kramenin felt the muzzle pressed against his ribs.

"It is I — Kramenin," he managed. "Bring down the girl at once. There is no time to lose."

Whittington came down the steps, astonished. "You! What's up? Surely you know the plan—"

"We have been betrayed. The girl! And at once. The other little fool had better come too."

"You have orders — from *him?*"

"Naturally. Hurry."

Whittington turned and ran back into the house. The agonizing minutes went by. Then two figures hastily huddled in cloaks were hustled into the car. The smaller resisted; Whittington shoved her in unceremoniously. As Julius leaned forward, the light from the open door lit his face. A man on the steps gave a startled exclamation.

"Get a move on!" shouted Julius.

The car bounded away. A flash, a report — the bullet just missed the taller girl by an inch.

"Get down, Jane!" Julius cried, thrusting her flat. He took aim and fired back. "Hit him — not killed."

"Where's Tommy?" cried Tuppence. "And who's this?"

"Tommy's making tracks for the Argentine. He thought you'd turned up your toes. May I present Monsieur Kramenin? I persuaded him to come on the trip for his health."

"But what made them let us go?"

"Monsieur Kramenin asked them so prettily they couldn't refuse."

The Russian burst out: "Curse you! They know now that I betrayed them. My life won't be safe an hour in this country."

"I'd advise you to make tracks for Russia right away. Pull up — the gentleman's not making the return trip."

Before the car had halted the Russian had swung out and disappeared into the night.

"How did you 'persuade' him?" asked the smaller girl — the first time she'd spoken.

Julius tapped his revolver. "Little Willie here takes the credit."

"Splendid!" The colour surged into her face.

"Annette and I didn't know what was going to happen," said Tuppence. "We thought it was lambs to the slaughter."

"Annette," said Julius. His mind seemed to be adjusting itself to a new idea.

"It's her name."

"Shucks. She may think it's her name, because her memory's gone, poor kid. But it's the one real and original Jane Finn we've got here."

"What?"

A bullet embedded itself in the upholstery just behind Tuppence's head.

"Down! It's an ambush." The car leapt forward; three more shots went wide. Julius raised his hand to his cheek.

"You are hurt?" said Annette quickly.

"Only a scratch."

The girl sprang up. "Let me out! It is me they're after. I'm the one they want. You shall not lose your lives because of me."

Julius caught her by both arms. She had spoken with no trace of foreign accent.

"Sit down, kid. I guess there's nothing wrong with your memory. Been fooling them all the time, eh?"

She nodded, and burst into tears.

"You're from home," she said indistinctly. "I can tell by your voice. It makes me homesick."

"Sure I'm from home. I'm your cousin — Julius Hersheimmer. I came over to Europe on purpose to find you — and a pretty dance you've led me."

The car slackened. "Cross-roads here, sir. I'm not sure of the way."

As it slowed, a figure climbed suddenly over the back, and plunged head first into the midst of them.

"Sorry," said Tommy, extricating himself. "Was in the bushes by the drive. Hung on behind. Now then, you girls, get out. There's a station up that road. Train due in three minutes."

"What the devil are you driving at?" demanded Julius. "You can't let those girls go off alone."

"You and I aren't going to leave the car. Only the girls. Get out at once, Tuppence. Take her with you. Take the train to London. Go straight to Sir James Peel Edgerton."

"Darn you! Jane, you stay where you are."

With a sudden swift movement Tommy snatched the revolver from Julius's hand, and levelled it at him.

"Now will you believe I'm in earnest? Get out, both of you, or I'll shoot."

Tuppence sprang out, dragging the unwilling Jane after her. "Come on. If Tommy's sure — he's sure."

They started running.

"What the hell—" Julius's pent-up rage burst forth.

"Dry up. I want a few words with you, Mr. Julius Hersheimmer."

JULIUS TELLS A STORY

Dressed for her “afternoon out,” Tuppence went first to the stationer’s—no message—then to the Ritz. Tommy had not returned. It was what she had feared. She resolved to appeal to Mr. Carter, and was addressing a note in Julius Hersheimmer’s sitting-room when the door burst open.

“What the hell—” began Julius, checking himself. “I beg your pardon, Miss Tuppence. Those fools said Beresford hadn’t been here since Wednesday. Is that so?”

Tuppence nodded. “You don’t know where he is?”

“I? How should I know? I haven’t had one darned word from him, though I wired yesterday morning.”

“Your wire’s probably unopened at the office.”

“But where is he?”

“I don’t know. I hoped you might.”

“I haven’t had one darned word from him since we parted at the depot on Wednesday.”

“What depot?”

“Waterloo—your London and South Western road.”

“Waterloo?” frowned Tuppence.

“Didn’t he tell you? He called me up, said to hustle. He was trailing two crooks. The big one was mine—the guy you bluffed. Tommy shoved a ticket into my hand, told me to get aboard. He was going to sleuth the other.”

“Julius,” said Tuppence, “stop walking up and down. Sit down in that armchair, and tell me the whole story with as few fancy turns of speech as possible.”

“Sure,” said Julius. “Where shall I begin?”

“At Waterloo.”

“I got into one of your dear old-fashioned first-class British compartments. A guard told me mighty politely I wasn’t in a smoking-carriage. I handed him half a dollar, and that settled that. I prospected along to the next coach—Whittington was there right enough. When I saw the skunk and thought of poor little Jane, I felt real mad I hadn’t got a gun.

“We reached Bournemouth. He took a cab, named an hotel. I did likewise. He sat in the lounge, read papers, dawdled through dinner. I figured his real business would be later, since he hadn’t changed.

“Sure enough, about nine he took a car across town, paid it off, and struck out along the pine-woods on the cliff. I was there too. By degrees the villas thinned out till we came to a big place with piny grounds. It was black as pitch; I heard him ring and get admitted.

“I waited in the rain till I got almighty cold and kind of restive. All the ground-floor windows were shuttered, but upstairs I spotted a light with the curtains not drawn. Opposite there was a tree. I figured if I climbed it, I might see in. The rain had made the boughs mighty slippery, but bit by bit I got level with the window—too far left. Just a strip of curtain and wallpaper. I was going to climb down, when somebody moved. A shadow fell on my bit of wall—by gum, it was Whittington!

“After that, my blood was up. A long branch ran out towards the window. If I could swarm along, I’d get my look. It was mighty uncertain if it’d bear me, but I started. Inch by inch—creak and sway—till I was where I wanted.

“The room was bare and hygienic. A table with a lamp. Whittington sat facing me, talking to a woman dressed as a hospital nurse, her back to me. The window was shut; I couldn’t hear. He seemed emphatic—banged the table once or twice. Presently he got up; she came across to the window and looked out. Just then the moon came out. I tried to move back. The jerk was too much for that rotten old branch. With an almighty crash, down it came—and Julius P. Hersheimmer with it!”

“Oh, Julius,” breathed Tuppence. “Go on.”

“I pitched into a good soft bed of earth, but it put me out of action. Next thing I knew, I was in bed with a hospital nurse (not Whittington’s) and a little black-bearded man with gold glasses—medical man written all over him. ‘Ah! So our young friend is coming round again. Capital. Capital.’ He called himself Dr. Hall. ‘This,’ he said, ‘is my private nursing home.’

“I wasn’t going to put him wise. I mumbled a piece about a girl—stern guardian, nervous breakdown, thought I recognized her among his patients—hence my nocturnal stunt. He took it genial. ‘Quite a romance.’ So I said: ‘Now, doc, will you be frank? Have you here now, or have you had at any time, a young girl called Jane Finn?’ He repeated it, thoughtful. ‘Jane Finn? No. It’s an uncommon name. I should not have been likely to forget it.’

“That laid me out. ‘Another matter,’ I said. ‘When I was hugging that darned branch I thought I recognized an old friend talking to one of your nurses.’ He answered at once: ‘Mr. Whittington, perhaps?’ ‘That’s the fellow. What’s he doing down here?’ Dr. Hall laughed. ‘He came to see one of my nurses—Nurse Edith—who is a niece of his.’ ‘Fancy that! Is he still here?’ ‘No, he went back to town almost immediately.’ ‘But perhaps I could speak to his niece—Nurse Edith?’ The doctor shook his head. ‘I’m afraid that, too, is impossible. Nurse Edith left with a patient to-night also.’ ‘Real unlucky,’ I said. ‘Have you Mr. Whittington’s address?’ ‘I don’t know it. I can write to Nurse Edith for it if you like.’ ‘Don’t say who wants it. I’d like to give him a little surprise.’

“That was about all I could do. I wired Beresford where I was, laid up with a sprained foot, told him to come if he wasn’t busy. No answer. The foot was only ricked, so to-day I said good-bye to the little doctor, asked him to send word if he heard from Nurse Edith, and came right back to town. Say, Miss Tuppence, you’re looking mighty pale!”

“It’s Tommy,” said Tuppence. “What can have happened?”

“Buck up. I guess he’s all right. Maybe he’s gone abroad—Poland or something?”

“He couldn’t—passports and things. Besides, I’ve seen that man Boris since. He dined with Mrs. Vandemeyer last night.”

“Mrs. Who?”

“I forgot—you don’t know.” And Tuppence related the last two days—how she had entered Mrs. Vandemeyer’s service, seen Boris, and the steely glitter in her mistress’s eyes.

“Bully for you! Fancy you a menial. It just tickles me to death!” Then, gravely: “But I don’t like it. You’re plucky as they make ’em, but these crooks would as soon croak a girl as a man.”

“Do you think I’m afraid?” said Tuppence indignantly. “I’ve written to Mr. Carter,” she added, telling him the gist of her letter.

“I guess that’s good as far as it goes. But it’s for us to get busy. We’d better get on the track of Boris. You say he’s been to your place. Is he likely to come again?”

“He might. I don’t know.”

“I’ll buy a car, dress as a chauffeur, and hang about outside. If Boris comes, you make a signal, and I’ll trail him. How’s that?”

“Splendid—but he mightn’t come for weeks.”

“We’ll have to chance that. I’m glad you like the plan.” He rose.

“Where are you going?”

“To buy the car, of course. What make do you like? I guess you’ll do some riding in it before we’ve finished.”

“I like Rolls-Royces, but—”

“Sure. What you say goes. I’ll get one.”

“But you can’t at once. People wait ages sometimes.”

“Little Julius doesn’t. Don’t you worry. I’ll be round in half an hour.”

“Oh; but I forgot—I met Sir James Peel Edgerton this morning,” said Tuppence, and told of the ambiguous warning.

Julius negatived it sharply. “We don’t want any lawyers mixed up. That guy couldn’t help us any.”

“I believe he could,” said Tuppence obstinately.

“Don’t you think it. So long.”

Thirty-five minutes later Julius returned, took her to the window. “There she is.”

“Oh!” said Tuppence reverently, gazing at the enormous car.

“She’s some pace-maker,” said Julius complacently.

“How did you get it?”

“She was being sent home to some bigwig. I went round to his house. I said a car like that was worth every penny of twenty thousand dollars. Then I told him it was worth just about fifty thousand to me if he’d get out.”

“Well?” said Tuppence, intoxicated.

“Well,” returned Julius, “he got out, that’s all.”

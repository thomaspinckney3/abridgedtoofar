After ringing up Sir James, Tommy made straight for South Audley Mansions. He found Albert at his post and introduced himself as a friend of Tuppence’s. Albert’s eyes brightened.

“Things has been very quiet here lately,” he said wistfully.

“Hope the young lady’s keeping well, sir?”

“That’s just the point, Albert. She’s disappeared.”

“You don’t mean as the crooks have got her? In the Underworld?”

“No, dash it, in this world!”

“It’s a h’expression, sir. At the pictures the crooks always have a restoorant in the Underworld. But do you think as they’ve done her in?”

“I hope not. By the way, have you an aunt, a cousin, a grandmother—any suitable female relation likely to kick the bucket?”

A delighted grin spread over Albert’s countenance. “I’m on, sir. My poor aunt what lives in the country has been mortal bad a long time, and she’s asking for me with her dying breath.”

“Good. Can you report that properly and meet me at Charing Cross in an hour?”

“I’ll be there, sir.”

Together they took rooms at the inn in Gatehouse. Albert, indefatigable, cemented an alliance with the greengrocer’s boy and ingratiated himself with the cook at Malthouse. Astley Priors, he learned, belonged to a Dr. Adams—retired, “but he took a few private patients”—here the landlord tapped his forehead knowingly—“balmy ones!” A popular figure in the village, free with subscriptions.

Tommy’s doubts pricked him. Could this genial, well‑known figure be a dangerous criminal? No hint of anything sinister. Suppose it was a gigantic mistake? Then he remembered the “private patients.” He inquired quietly whether there was a young lady among them—describing Tuppence. Nobody knew; patients were seldom seen. A guarded description of Annette also failed.

That evening he and Albert crawled, at Albert’s insistence, painfully on their stomachs through the shrubbery, thereby producing a great deal more noise than if they had stood upright. In any case, these precautions were totally unnecessary. Tommy had imagined a possible fierce watchdog. Albert’s fancy ran to a puma, or a tame cobra. But they reached a shrubbery near the house quite unmolested.

The blinds of the dining‑room were up; a large company sat round the table; the port went its round; scraps of county‑cricket talk drifted through the open window. The fair‑bearded spectacled gentleman at the head looked honest and normal. Again the cold chill seized Tommy.

The following day, after more errands kitchen‑ward, Albert brought the first hopeful news: there was a French young lady staying in the house. Tommy thrust doubts aside. To‑day was the 27th. The 29th—“Labour Day”—was at hand; rumours of a general strike were rife; the Government said nothing; Labour leaders were divided while subtle forces urged extremism. With the fatal document in Mr. Brown’s hands, public opinion would swing to the revolutionaries; failing that, the issue was an even chance. Tommy nourished a preposterous dream: unmask Mr. Brown, and the whole organization would crumble; honest men, left to themselves, might patch up an eleventh‑hour peace. “This is a one‑man show,” said Tommy to himself. “The thing to do is to get hold of the man.” Hence the sealed envelope—bait.

That evening, crouched in the drive, he gave a sudden gasp. Against a second‑floor blind a silhouette stood out—a silhouette he would have known anywhere. Tuppence was in that house!

He clutched Albert. “Stay here. When I begin to sing, watch that window.”

He retreated to the drive, and in a deep, unsteady roar began:

I am a Soldier
A jolly British Soldier;
You can see that I’m a Soldier by my feet...

In Tuppence’s hospital days the gramophone had ground it out a hundred times. An unimpeachable butler, with an unimpeachable footman, issued from the front door. With perfect decorum they seized him by either arm and ran him out of the gate. “If you intrude again, I shall send for the police,” said the butler—Whittington—beautifully.

Tommy waited at the inn till Albert returned, triumphant. “While they was a‑running of you out the window opened, and something was chucked. It was wrapped round a letterweight.” On the scrap were three words: “To‑morrow—same time.”

“Good egg!” said Tommy.

“I wrote a message on a piece of paper, wrapped it round a stone, and chucked it through the window,” added Albert, breathless and then crestfallen under Tommy’s groan. “Said we was a‑staying at the inn. If she could get away, to come there and croak like a frog.”

“Your zeal will be the undoing of us, Albert. What did you say?”

He repeated it, abashed.

“She’ll know that’s you,” said Tommy with a sigh of relief. “Your imagination runs away with you, you know. Why, you wouldn’t recognize a frog croaking if you heard it. Cheer up. No harm done. That butler’s an old friend of mine—I bet he knew who I was, though he didn’t let on. It’s not their game to show suspicion. That’s why we’ve found it fairly plain sailing. They don’t want to discourage me altogether. On the other hand, they don’t want to make it too easy. I’m a pawn in their game, Albert, that’s what I am. You see, if the spider lets the fly walk out too easily, the fly might suspect it was a put‑up job. Hence the usefulness of that promising youth, Mr. T. Beresford, who’s blundered in just at the right moment for them. But later, Mr. T. Beresford had better look out!”

He elaborated a plan for the morrow, and retired for the night in some elation, confident they would not interfere up to a point. It was after that Tommy proposed to give them a surprise.

Near midnight a rude‑looking carter, mud‑spattered, demanded him in the bar, and held out a dirty folded note: “Take this to the gentleman at the inn near Astley Priors. He will give you ten shillings.” The handwriting was Tuppence’s. Tommy snatched it, paid.

“DEAR TOMMY,

“I knew it was you last night. Don’t go this evening. They’ll be lying in wait for you. They’re taking us away this morning. I heard something about Wales—Holyhead, I think. I’ll drop this on the road if I get a chance. Annette told me how you’d escaped. Buck up.

“Yours, TWOPENCE.”

“Pack my bag! We’re off!” he shouted to Albert—then, before the boots had ceased thundering upstairs, another cry: “Albert! I’m a damned fool! Unpack that bag!”

He smoothed the note thoughtfully. “Yes, a damned fool,” he said softly.

“But so’s some one else! And at last I know who it is!”

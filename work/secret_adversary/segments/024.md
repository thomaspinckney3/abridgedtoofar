After ringing up Sir James, Tommy enlisted Tuppence's friend Albert, and the two took quarters at the inn in Gatehouse. Astley Priors, the village volunteered, was the property of a Dr. Adams — retired, but kept a few private patients ("balmy ones — you understand"). A popular figure, given to subscriptions, "a very pleasant, affable gentleman." Scientific; always visitors.

The volubility raised Tommy's doubts. Suppose it was all a gigantic mistake? He inquired carefully if there was a young lady amongst the patients, describing Tuppence and Annette. Nothing. The patients were seldom seen outside the grounds.

That evening they explored. The grounds, like those of any private house after nightfall, seemed untenanted. They reached a shrubbery near the house unmolested. The blinds of the dining-room window were up. A normal, pleasant company sat round the table; through the open window floated a heated discussion of county cricket. Again Tommy felt the cold chill of uncertainty. The fair-bearded gentleman at the head of the table looked singularly honest.

The next morning Albert took the place of the greengrocer's boy and ingratiated himself with the cook. Repeating the trick the following day, he brought confirmation: a French young lady was staying in the house.

Time pressed. To-day was the 27th; the 29th, the much-talked-of "Labour Day," loomed. Newspapers were full of sensational hints of a Labour *coup d'état*. With the fatal document in Mr. Brown's hands, public opinion would swing to the extremists. Failing that, the battle was even — at the cost of great suffering. But Tommy nourished a more preposterous dream: with Mr. Brown unmasked, the whole organization would crumble in panic. The strange permeating influence of the unseen chief held it together.

"This is a one-man show," said Tommy to himself. "The thing to do is to get hold of the man."

That evening they once more penetrated the grounds. As they approached, Tommy gave a sudden gasp. On the second floor a silhouette stood between window and light. He would have known it anywhere. Tuppence was in that house.

"Stay here. When I begin to sing, watch that window."

He retreated to the main drive, and began with a deep roar, coupled with an unsteady gait:

> *I am a Soldier*
> *A jolly British Soldier;*
> *You can see that I'm a Soldier by my feet…*

It had been a favourite on the gramophone in Tuppence's hospital days. Tommy had not a note of music in his voice, but his lungs were excellent.

Presently an unimpeachable butler issued from the front door with an equally unimpeachable footman. They ran him neatly down the drive and out of the gate, threatening police if he intruded again. It was beautifully done — only, as it happened, the butler was Whittington.

Tommy retired to the inn. Albert reappeared with a scrap of paper wrapped round a letterweight: *To-morrow — same time.*

"I wrote a message and chucked it through the window," he added breathlessly. "Said we was at the inn. If she could get away, to come there and croak like a frog."

"Your zeal will be the undoing of us, Albert. That butler's an old friend of mine — I bet he knew who I was. It's not their game to show suspicion. They don't want to discourage me altogether. I'm a pawn in their game. If the spider lets the fly walk out too easily, the fly might suspect a put-up job. But later, Mr. T. Beresford had better look out."

About twelve, his calm was shaken. Someone was demanding him in the bar — a rude-looking carter coated with mud.

"Might this be for you, sir?" The note was very dirty: *Take this to the gentleman at the inn near Astley Priors. He will give you ten shillings.*

The handwriting was Tuppence's. Tommy paid him and unfastened it.

> DEAR TOMMY,
> I knew it was you last night. Don't go this evening. They'll be lying in wait for you. They're taking us away this morning. I heard something about Wales — Holyhead, I think. I'll drop this on the road if I get a chance. Annette told me how you'd escaped. Buck up.
> Yours,
> TWOPENCE.

"Pack my bag!" Tommy shouted. "We're off!"

Holyhead. Did that mean—? He read the note again slowly. The boots above continued busy.

"Albert! I'm a damned fool! Unpack that bag!"

He smoothed out the note thoughtfully.

"Yes, a damned fool. But so's some one else. And at last I know who it is."

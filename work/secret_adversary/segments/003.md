Tuppence turned sharply. The rebuff that trembled on her lips went unsaid; the man’s appearance and manner did not bear out her first assumption. Big, clean-shaven, heavy-jowled; small cunning eyes that shifted under her direct gaze. She disliked and distrusted him, but believed he meant no disrespect.

“Well, what is it?”

“I happened to overhear part of your conversation with the young gentleman in Lyons’.”

“Well—what of it?”

“Nothing—except that I think I may be of some use to you.”

“You followed me here?”

“I took that liberty.” He handed her a card: Mr. Edward Whittington; Esthonia Glassware Co.; a City address. “If you will call upon me to-morrow morning at eleven o’clock, I will lay the details of my proposition before you.”

Tuppence considered, then nodded. “Very well. I’ll be there.”

He raised his hat with a flourish and walked away. Tuppence remained for some minutes gazing after him. Then she gave a curious movement of her shoulders, rather as a terrier shakes himself. “The adventures have begun,” she murmured to herself.

“What does he want me to do, I wonder? There’s something about you, Mr. Whittington, that I don’t like at all. But, on the other hand, I’m not the least bit afraid of you. And as I’ve said before, and shall doubtless say again, little Tuppence can look after herself, thank you!” She turned aside into a post office, weighed five shillings against ninepence. Disdaining the spiky pen and thick, black treacle which a beneficent Government had provided, Tuppence drew out Tommy’s pencil which she had retained and wrote rapidly: “Don’t put in advertisement. Will explain to-morrow.”

“It may catch him,” she murmured.

“Anyway, it’s worth trying.”

That night in her tiny cubicle she munched buns and wondered what earthly need an Esthonia Glassware Co. could have for her services; she dreamed Mr. Whittington set her to wash hospital plates masquerading as Esthonian glass.

At eleven to the minute she mounted to the top-floor door with “Esthonia Glassware Co.” on ground glass. A middle-aged clerk ushered her through a “Private” door.

Mr. Whittington, sleek prosperity and shifty eye, nodded her to a chair, shuffled papers, then leaned across the desk.

“Now, my dear young lady, let us come to business. You want work? I have work to offer you. What should you say now to £100 down, and all expenses paid?” He thrust his thumbs into his waistcoat.

“And the nature of the work?”

“Nominal—purely nominal. A pleasant trip, that is all.”

“Where to?”

“Paris.” He smiled. “To re-enter one of those charming pensionnats de jeunes filles—Madame Colombier’s, Avenue de Neuilly.”

Tuppence knew the name; more select could hardly be. “You want me to go there? For how long?”

“Possibly three months. You would go in the character of my ward. Absolute secrecy: no communication with your friends. By the way, you are English?”

“Yes.”

“Yet you speak with a slight American accent?”

“My great pal in hospital was a little American girl. I can soon get out of it again.”

“On the contrary, pass as an American. Details of an English past are harder to sustain.” He was smooth again. “Then—”

“One moment, Mr. Whittington! You seem to be taking my consent for granted.”

“Surely you are not thinking of refusing? Madame Colombier’s is most high-class and orthodox. And the terms are most liberal.”

“Exactly. The terms are almost too liberal. I cannot see any way in which I can be worth that amount of money to you.”

“No? I could obtain some one else for very much less. What I am willing to pay for is a young lady with sufficient intelligence and presence of mind to sustain her part well—and sufficient discretion not to ask too many questions.”

“There’s another thing. So far there has been no mention of Mr. Beresford. Where does he come in?”

“Mr. Beresford?”

“My partner. You saw us together yesterday.”

“I’m afraid we shan’t require his services.”

“Then it’s off!” Tuppence rose. “It’s both or neither. Sorry—but that’s how it is. Good morning, Mr. Whittington.”

“Wait. Let us see if something can’t be managed. Sit down again, Miss—?”

Her conscience pricked at “archdeacon.” The first name that came into her head leapt to her lips. “Jane Finn,” she said hastily—and stared. The geniality fell from his face; it went purple; veins stood out.

“So that’s your little game, is it?” he hissed.

Tuppence, utterly taken aback, kept her head. She had no idea what he meant, but she “kept her end up.”

“Been playing with me like a cat and mouse? Knew all the time what I wanted you for? Who’s been blabbing? Rita?”

“Rita knows nothing about me,” said Tuppence truthfully.

His eyes bored into her. “How much do you know?”

“Very little indeed,” said Tuppence—and saw his uneasiness grow.

“Anyway, you knew enough to come in here and plump out that name.”

“It might be my own name.”

“Likely, isn’t it? Two girls with a name like that?”

“Or I might just have hit upon it by chance,” said Tuppence, intoxicated with the success of truth.

He brought his fist down. “Quit fooling! How much do you know? And how much do you want?”

The last five words took Tuppence’s fancy mightily, especially after a meagre breakfast and a supper of buns the night before. Her present part was of the adventuress rather than the adventurous order, but she did not deny its possibilities. She sat up and smiled with the air of one who has the situation thoroughly well in hand.

“My dear Mr. Whittington,” she said, “let us by all means lay our cards upon the table. You heard me say yesterday I proposed to live by my wits. I have now proved I have some wits to live by! I admit I have knowledge of a certain name—but perhaps my knowledge ends there.”

“Yes—and perhaps it doesn’t. How much?”

“Suppose we say a little something down, and a fuller discussion later?”

“Blackmail, eh?”

“Oh no! Shall we say payment of services in advance? You see—I’m so very fond of money!”

“You’re about the limit,” growled Whittington, with unwilling admiration. “I thought you were a meek little kid with just enough brains for my purpose.”

“Life,” said Tuppence, “is full of surprises.”

A discreet knock. The clerk entered with a telephone message. Whittington read, frowned. “That’ll do, Brown.” He turned back. “Come to-morrow at the same time. I’m busy now. Here’s fifty to go on with.”

He pushed notes across. Tuppence counted them, slipped them into her handbag, and rose. “Au revoir, Mr. Whittington.”

“Exactly. Au revoir, my clever and charming young lady.”

Tuppence sped down the stairs, elated. A clock showed five minutes to twelve. “Let’s give Tommy a surprise!” She hailed a taxi to Piccadilly Tube. Tommy, just within the entrance, stared as she alighted.

“Pay the thing, will you, old bean? I’ve got nothing smaller than a five-pound note!”

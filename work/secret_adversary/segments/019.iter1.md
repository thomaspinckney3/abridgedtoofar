THE TELEGRAM

Baffled for the moment, Tommy strolled into the restaurant and ordered a meal of surpassing excellence. He was in the act of conveying a particularly choice morsel of Sole à la Jeanette to his mouth when he caught sight of Julius. Tommy waved cheerfully. Julius’s eyes seemed as though they would pop out of his head. He pump‑handled Tommy’s hand with unnecessary vigour.

“Holy snakes! Is it really you?”

“Of course. Why shouldn’t it be?”

“Why shouldn’t it be? Say, man, don’t you know you’ve been given up for dead? We’d have had a solemn requiem for you in another few days.”

“Who thought I was dead?”

“Tuppence.”

“She remembered the proverb about the good dying young, I suppose. There must be a certain amount of original sin in me to have survived. Where is Tuppence, by the way?”

“Isn’t she here? I dropped her in the car about an hour ago.”

They sat; Julius ordered; Tommy told his tale; Julius, spellbound, forgot half his dishes. Then in turn he narrated his own doings—Bournemouth a washout; back to London; the car; Tuppence growing anxious; a call on Sir James; and the sensational occurrences of the previous night at Mrs. Vandemeyer’s.

“The doctor kidded himself she took it herself,” he ended dryly.

“And Sir James?”

“Being a legal luminary, he is likewise a human oyster. I should say he reserved judgment.” He added the morning’s news.

“Lost her memory, eh?” said Tommy, interested. “By Jove, that explains why they looked queer when I spoke of questioning her. Bit of a slip on my part, that! But it wasn’t the sort of thing a fellow would be likely to guess.”

They agreed no hint had been given of Jane’s whereabouts.

“I guess you’re lucky to be here at all,” said Julius. “That bluff of yours was the goods all right. How you ever came to think of it all so pat beats me to a frazzle!”

“I was in such a funk I had to think of something,” said Tommy simply.

On Mrs. Vandemeyer’s death, Tommy said thoughtfully: “Mr. Brown?”

“Sure thing.”

“All the same, Mr. Brown hasn’t got wings. I don’t see how he got in and out.”

They bandied a moment’s nonsense about “magnetic influence,” then Tommy sprang up. “Let’s go round to the scene of the crime. I wish we could get hold of Tuppence. The Ritz would enjoy the spectacle of the glad reunion.”

Inquiry at the office revealed she had not returned. Julius went upstairs to look in his rooms. A diminutive page hovered by Tommy and whispered shyly that the young lady had taken a taxi “to Charing Cross,” after asking for an A.B.C. and a Bradshaw—“when I took her the telegram, sir.”

“A telegram? When?”

“About half‑past twelve, sir.”

Julius rejoined them with an open letter in his hand. “Tuppence has gone off sleuthing on her own,” said Tommy. “She went off to Charing Cross in the deuce of a hurry after getting a telegram. Oh; she left a note for you. Where’s she off to?”

Julius folded the letter and pocketed it, a trifle embarrassed. “I guess this is nothing to do with it. It’s about something else—something I asked her to let me know about.” He looked Tommy in the eye. “I’d better put you wise. I asked Miss Tuppence to marry me this morning.”

Tommy felt dazed. “That’s all right,” he said quickly, lighting a cigarette with a hand that shook. “Tuppence and I have been pals for years. Nothing more.”

“Oh, I guess it’ll be the dollars that’ll do the trick. Miss Tuppence put me wise to that right away. We ought to gee along together very well.”

Tommy looked at him curiously for a minute, as though he were about to speak, then changed his mind and said nothing. Nevertheless, Tommy did blame her. He was filled with a passionate and utterly illogical resentment. It was all very well to say things like that—but a real girl would never marry for money. Tuppence was utterly cold‑blooded and selfish, and he would be delighted if he never saw her again! And it was a rotten world!

“What did she say in that note?” he demanded fiercely.

“There’s no earthly clue in it,” said Julius, handing it over.

In Tuppence’s well‑known schoolboy scrawl:

“DEAR JULIUS,

“It’s always better to have things in black and white. I don’t feel I can be bothered to think of marriage until Tommy is found. Let’s leave it till then.

“Yours affectionately,

“TUPPENCE.”

Tommy handed it back, his eyes shining. His feelings had undergone a sharp reaction. He now felt that Tuppence was all that was noble and disinterested. Had she not refused Julius without hesitation? True, the note betokened signs of weakening, but he could excuse that. It read almost like a bribe to Julius to spur him on in his efforts to find Tommy, but he supposed she had not really meant it that way. Darling Tuppence, there was not a girl in the world to touch her!

He wheeled on the page. “One thing more. What did the young lady do with the telegram?”

“She crumpled it into a ball, sir, and threw it into the grate.”

“Come on, Julius. We must find that telegram.”

They hurried to Tuppence’s room. In the fireplace lay a crumpled orange‑and‑white ball. Tommy smoothed it out:

“Come at once, Moat House, Ebury, Yorkshire, great developments--TOMMY.”

“You didn’t send it?”

“Of course not.”

“I guess it means the worst,” said Julius quietly. “They’ve got her. They signed your name, and she fell into the trap like a lamb.”

“My God! What shall we do?”

“Get busy, and go after her! Right now! Where’s that Bradshaw?”

Tommy abandoned the Bradshaw for an A.B.C. “Here we are. Ebury, Yorks. From King’s Cross. 12.50—that’s the train she went by; 3.20 the next.”

“What about the car?”

“Send it up if you like, but we’d better stick to the train. The great thing is to keep calm.”

“That’s so,” groaned Julius, “but it gets my goat to think of that innocent young girl in danger!”

Tommy puckered his brow. “I don’t think it’s their game to do her any harm. She’s a hostage, that’s what she is. She’s in no immediate danger; if we tumble on to anything, she’s damned useful to them. As long as they’ve got her, they’ve got the whip hand of us. See?”

“Sure thing,” said Julius thoughtfully. “That’s so.”

The journey was wearisome. At a deserted station they got meticulous but perplexing directions to “the big house near the sea.” Rain, an overgrown drive, shuttered windows, mossed steps—silence everywhere. The rusty bell jangled discordantly; no one came. They walked round—still no sign of life.

“There must be a village handy,” said Julius. In a hamlet a workman told them the Moat House had been empty for years; the post office held the key. Presently they stood in the dark hall; thick dust lay everywhere, undisturbed. They searched in vain; returned in daylight; the tale was the same. They were about to give up when, near the gate among the leaves, Tommy stooped with a cry and held up a small gold brooch.

“That’s Tuppence’s!”

“I guess that settles it. She came as far as here, anyway. We’ll make the pub our headquarters and raise hell till we find her. Somebody must have seen her.”

They campaigned forthwith, separately and together, but nobody in the neighbourhood had seen a girl like Tuppence, or a car by the Moat House. Julius wired for his own car; daily they scoured lanes and properties; a grey limousine was traced to Harrogate—and a highly respectable maiden lady. Owners of motors were cross‑examined and apologised to. So well had the abduction been planned that the girl seemed literally to have vanished into thin air.

A week wore by. Over breakfast Tommy said grimly: “Do you know how long we’ve been here? And next Sunday is the 29th!”

“Shucks,” said Julius. “I’d almost forgotten the 29th. I’ve been thinking of nothing but Tuppence.”

“So have I. But time’s getting short. If we’re ever going to get hold of her at all, we must do it before the 29th—her life won’t be worth an hour’s purchase afterwards. I’m beginning to feel we’ve made a big mistake. We’ve wasted time.”

“I’m with you. We’ve been a couple of mutts. I’m going right back to London to put the case in the hands of your British police. Scotland Yard for me! Better late than never. We’ve been like a couple of babes playing ‘Here we go round the Mulberry Bush.’ Now I’m going right along to Scotland Yard to ask them to take me by the hand and show me the way I should go. I guess the professional always scores over the amateur in the end. Are you coming along?”

“What’s the good? One of us is enough. I’ll nose round here a bit longer. Something might turn up.”

“So long. I’ll be back in a couple of shakes with a few inspectors along.”

But the plan miscarried. Later in the day Tommy received a wire:

“Join me Manchester Midland Hotel. Important news--JULIUS.”

At 7.30 he arrived by a slow cross‑country train. Julius was on the platform.

“What is it? Is Tuppence found?”

“No. But I found this waiting in London. Just arrived.”

He handed over a telegraph form. Tommy’s eyes opened wide:

“Jane Finn found. Come Manchester Midland Hotel immediately--PEEL EDGERTON.”

Julius folded the form. “Queer,” he said thoughtfully. “I thought that lawyer chap had quit!”

TOO LATE

In the street they held an informal council. Sir James consulted his watch. “The boat train to Holyhead stops at Chester at 12.14. If you start at once you can catch the connection.”

Tommy looked up, puzzled. “Is there any need to hurry, sir? To-day is only the 24th.”

“I guess it’s always well to get up early in the morning,” said Julius. “We’ll make tracks for the depot right away.”

A little frown settled on Sir James’s brow. “I wish I could come. I am due to speak at two o’clock. It is unfortunate.”

“I guess there’s nothing complicated about this deal,” Julius remarked. “Just a game of hide-and-seek, that’s all.”

“I hope so,” said Sir James.

“Sure thing. What else could it be?”

“You are still young, Mr. Hersheimmer. At my age you will probably have learnt one lesson. ‘Never underestimate your adversary.’”

The gravity of his tone impressed Tommy, but had little effect upon Julius.

“You think Mr. Brown might come along and take a hand? If he does, I’m ready for him.” He patted his pocket. “Little Willie here travels round with me everywhere.” He produced a murderous-looking automatic, tapped it, and returned it to its home. “But he won’t be needed this trip. There’s nobody to put Mr. Brown wise.”

“There was nobody to put Mr. Brown wise to the fact that Mrs. Vandemeyer meant to betray him,” said Sir James dryly. “Nevertheless, Mrs. Vandemeyer died without speaking.” He held out his hand. “Good-bye, and good luck. Take no unnecessary risks once the papers are in your hands. If there is any reason to believe that you have been shadowed, destroy them at once. The game is in your hands now.”

Ten minutes later they were in a first-class carriage en route for Chester. Silence held them for some time; then Julius said unexpectedly:

“Say, did you ever make a darned fool of yourself over a girl’s face?”

Tommy searched his mind. “Can’t say I have. Why?”

“Because for the last two months I’ve been making a sentimental idiot of myself over Jane! First moment I clapped eyes on her photograph my heart did all the usual stunts you read about in novels. I guess I’m ashamed to admit it, but I came over here determined to find her and fix it all up, and take her back as Mrs. Julius P. Hersheimmer!”

“Oh!” said Tommy, amazed.

Julius uncrossed his legs brusquely and continued: “Just shows what an almighty fool a man can make of himself! One look at the girl in the flesh, and I was cured! No disparagement to Jane. She’s a real nice girl, and some fellow will fall in love with her right away. But there was something about that photo—” He shook his head and sighed. “I guess romance is a mighty queer thing!”

“It must be,” said Tommy coldly, “if you can come over here in love with one girl, and propose to another within a fortnight.”

Julius coloured. “Well, you see, I’d got kind of tired thinking I’d never find Jane—and then—oh, well, the French are much more sensible. They keep romance and marriage apart——”

Tommy flushed. “Well, I’m damned! If that’s——”

Julius hastened to interrupt. “Say now, don’t be hasty. I don’t mean what you mean. I take it Americans have a higher opinion of morality than you have even. What I meant was that the French set about marriage in a businesslike way—find two people who are suited to one another, look after the money affairs, and see the whole thing practically, and in a businesslike spirit.”

“If you ask me,” said Tommy hotly, “we’re all too damned businesslike nowadays.”

“Cool down, son,” said Julius; and prudently said no more.

By Holyhead the cheerful grin had returned to Tommy’s face. With a road map and brief consultation they hired a taxi and drove out towards Treaddur Bay, made the man go slowly, and watched for the path. Not long after leaving the town Tommy stopped the car, asked carelessly if the path led to the sea, and, hearing it did, paid off the driver.

They turned to the narrow path between swelling hedges of golden gorse. Julius led; twice Tommy looked back uneasily.

“What is it?” asked Julius.

“I don’t know. I keep fancying there’s some one following us.”

“Can’t be. We’d see him.”

Nevertheless Tommy’s sense of the enemy’s omniscience deepened.

“I rather wish that fellow would come along,” said Julius. He patted his pocket. “Little William here is just aching for exercise!”

“Do you always carry it—him—with you?” asked Tommy, impressed.

“Most always. You never know what might turn up.”

The path ran along the side of the cliff. Suddenly Julius halted so abruptly that Tommy cannoned into him.

“What’s up?”

“Look there. If that doesn’t beat the band!”

Half obstructing the path stood a huge boulder, fancifully like a begging terrier.

“Well,” said Tommy, refusing to be thrilled, “it’s what we expected to see.”

“Sure we expected it,” said Julius sadly. “But it kind of rattles me all the same to see it sitting there just where we expected to find it!”

“Push on. What about the hole?”

They scanned the cliff. “The gorse won’t be there after all these years,” said Tommy idiotically.

“I guess you’re right,” said Julius solemnly.

Tommy pointed with a shaking hand. “What about that crevice there?”

“That’s it—for sure,” breathed Julius.

“When I was in France,” said Tommy, “whenever my batman failed to call me, he said he had come over queer. I never believed it. But whether he felt it or not, there is such a sensation. I’ve got it now! Badly!” He glared at the rock. “Damn it! It’s impossible! Five years! Bird’s-nesting boys, picnic parties, thousands of people passing! It can’t be there! It’s a hundred to one against it! It’s against all reason!”

Indeed, he felt it to be impossible—more, perhaps, because he could not believe in his own success where so many others had failed. The thing was too easy, therefore it could not be. The hole would be empty.

Julius’s smile widened. “I guess you’re rattled now all right. Well, here goes!” He thrust his hand into the crevice, grimaced. “It’s a tight fit. Jane’s hand must be smaller. I don’t feel anything—no—say, what’s this? Gee whiz!” He waved aloft a small discoloured packet. “It’s the goods all right. Sewn up in oilskin. Hold it while I get my penknife.”

The unbelievable had happened. Tommy held the packet tenderly. “It’s queer,” he murmured. “You’d think the stitches would have rotted. They look just as good as new.”

They cut the stitches, ripped away the oilskin. Inside was a small folded sheet. They unfolded it with trembling fingers. The sheet was blank.

“A dummy?” hazarded Julius. “Was Danvers just a decoy?”

Tommy’s face cleared. “I’ve got it! Sympathetic ink!”

“Worth trying. Heat usually does the trick.”

They kindled a little fire of twigs and leaves. Tommy held the paper near the glow. The paper curled; nothing more. Suddenly Julius gripped his arm. Faint brown characters were appearing.

“Gee whiz! You’ve got it! Say, that idea of yours was great.”

Tommy held steady until the heat had done its work, then withdrew it—only to utter a cry.

Across the sheet, in neat brown printing, ran the words: WITH THE COMPLIMENTS OF MR. BROWN.

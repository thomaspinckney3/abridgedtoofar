## Chapter 1

A PLAN OF CAMPAIGN

A veil might with profit be drawn over the next half-hour. No such person as "Inspector Brown" was known to Scotland Yard. The photograph of Jane Finn was lost beyond recovery. Once again Mr. Brown had triumphed.

The immediate result was a *rapprochement* between Julius Hersheimmer and the Young Adventurers. All barriers crashed down; Tommy and Tuppence revealed the whole history of the joint venture, whereat the young man declared himself "tickled to death."

"I've always had a kind of idea that English girls were just a mite moss-grown," he told Tuppence. "Old-fashioned and sweet, but scared to move round without a footman or a maiden aunt. I guess I'm a bit behind the times!"

The upshot was that Tommy and Tuppence took up their abode at the *Ritz*, in order, as Tuppence put it, to keep in touch with Jane Finn's only living relation. "And put like that," she added confidentially to Tommy, "nobody could boggle at the expense!"

Nobody did, which was the great thing.

"And now," said Tuppence the morning after their installation, "to work!"

Tommy lowered the *Daily Mail* and applauded with somewhat unnecessary vigour. He was politely requested by his colleague not to be an ass.

"Dash it all, Tommy, we've got to *do* something for our money."

"Yes, I fear even the dear old Government will not support us at the *Ritz* in idleness for ever."

"Therefore we must do something. We have two distinct clues. First, we know one of the gang."

"Whittington?"

"Yes. I'd recognize him anywhere."

"Hum. You don't know where to look for him, and it's about a thousand to one against running into him by accident."

"Once coincidences start happening, they go on happening in the most extraordinary way. Still—clue No. 2 is more promising. Only a Christian name—Rita."

She set out her reasoning: the man Danvers had been shadowed on the way over, very likely by a woman, a good-looking one. That woman must have been saved—else how would they have known Jane Finn had the papers? With luck, she might be Rita.

"Then we've got to hunt through the survivors of the *Lusitania* till we find her. The first thing is to get a list."

"I've got it." Tuppence produced Mr. Carter's reply enclosing the official statement of those saved. But the list gave few Christian names; mostly Mrs. or Miss.

"That complicates matters."

Tuppence gave her characteristic terrier shake. "We've just got to get down to it. We'll start with the London area."

Five minutes later they emerged into Piccadilly, bearing a list of seven. The first address proved a dilapidated house with a few grimy bushes for a front garden. As Tuppence reached for the bell, Tommy arrested her hand.

"What are you going to say?"

"Why—I shall say—oh dear, I don't know."

"I thought as much. How like a woman! Stand aside, and see how easily the mere male deals with the situation." He pressed the bell. A slatternly servant answered.

"Good morning," said Tommy briskly. "From the Hampstead Borough Council. The new Voting Register. Mrs. Edgar Keith?"

"Yaas."

"Christian name?"

"Eleanor Jane."

"Thank you." He closed the notebook with a snap.

"I thought perhaps as you'd come about the gas," the servant observed cryptically, and shut the door.

"Child's play to the masculine mind."

"For once you've scored handsomely. I should never have thought of it."

By lunch they had collected a Gladys Mary and a Marjorie, been baffled by one change of address, and endured a long lecture on universal suffrage from a vivacious American named Sadie.

"Where's the next draw?"

"Mrs. Vandemeyer, 20 South Audley Mansions."

"The Mayfair lady is clearly indicated."

"Tommy, I'm getting discouraged."

"Buck up, old bean. We always knew it was an outside chance."

South Audley Mansions was an imposing block just off Park Lane. To the elderly woman who opened the door of No. 20—looking more like a housekeeper than a servant—Tommy rattled off his formula.

"Christian name?"

"Margaret." She watched him begin to spell it, then corrected: "No—g-u-e."

"Oh, Marguerite; French way, I see." He plunged boldly. "We had her down as Rita Vandemeyer, but I suppose that's incorrect?"

"She's mostly called that, sir, but Marguerite's her name."

Hardly able to contain his excitement, Tommy hurried down. Tuppence was waiting at the angle of the turn.

"You heard?"

"Yes. Oh, *Tommy!*"

He squeezed her arm sympathetically. "I know, old thing. I feel the same."

"It's so lovely to think of things—and then for them really to happen!"

There were footsteps on the stairs above, and voices. Suddenly Tuppence dragged him into the shadow beside the lift.

"Hush!"

Two men passed out through the entrance. Tuppence's hand closed tighter on his arm.

"Quick—follow them. I daren't. He might recognize me. The bigger of the two was Whittington."

## Chapter 2

THE HOUSE IN SOHO

Whittington and his companion walked at a good pace through the small Mayfair streets. Tommy started in pursuit. Following anyone, he discovered, was fraught with difficulties unmentioned in the novels he had read. But the men did not hail a taxi, and presently they zigzagged into Oxford Street.

Just before Bond Street Tube they crossed and entered the big Lyons'. Tommy took a seat directly behind Whittington at the next table. The other man was fair, with a weak unpleasant face—Russian or Pole, Tommy judged—about fifty, his shoulders cringing a little, his eyes small and crafty. Whittington addressed him as Boris.

He talked earnestly in a low voice; Tommy caught only fragments—"Ireland," "propaganda," but no mention of Jane Finn. Then in a lull he heard Whittington whole: "Ah, but you don't know Flossie. She's a marvel. An archbishop would swear she was his own mother. She gets the voice right every time, and that's really the principal thing."

Then Tommy lost the thread, until two words uttered by Boris had a most stimulating effect upon the listener: "Mr. Brown."

Whittington seemed to remonstrate; Boris merely laughed.

"Why not, my friend? It is a name most respectable—most common. Did he not choose it for that reason? Ah, I should like to meet him—Mr. Brown."

There was a steely ring in Whittington's voice. "Who knows? You may have met him already."

"Bah! That is children's talk—a fable for the police. Or is it indeed true that he is with us and amongst us, unknown to all but a chosen few? If so, he keeps his secret well. We never know. We look at each other—*one of us is Mr. Brown*—which? He commands—but also he serves. Among us—in the midst of us. And no one knows which he is...."

With an effort the Russian shook off the fancy, and looked at his watch.

Outside, Whittington hailed a taxi and directed it to Waterloo. Tommy followed in another. At the booking-office Whittington took a first-class single to Bournemouth; Tommy did the same. He overheard Boris glance up at the clock: "You are early. You have nearly half an hour."

So Whittington was making the journey alone. Tommy could not follow both men—unless. He hurried into a telephone box. He dared not waste time tracing Tuppence; he rang the *Ritz* and asked for Hersheimmer.

"That you, Hersheimmer? Beresford speaking. I'm at Waterloo. I've followed Whittington and another man here. Whittington's off to Bournemouth by the 3.30. Can you get there by then?"

"Sure. I'll hustle."

Tommy put back the receiver with a sigh of relief. The taking of his ticket had left him with only a few shillings; it was to be hoped Julius would arrive better provided.

3.15. 3.20. 3.27. Doors banged. 3.29.... Then a hand fell on his shoulder.

"Here I am, son. Your British traffic beats description!"

"That's Whittington—the big dark man, getting in now. The other is the foreign chap he's talking to. Got any money?"

Julius shook his head. "I guess I haven't more than three or four hundred dollars on me."

Tommy whooped. "Oh, Lord, you millionaires! Climb aboard the lugger. Here's your ticket. Whittington's your man."

The train slid out as Julius swung himself aboard. Tommy drew a deep breath. Boris was coming along the platform; Tommy let him pass and took up the chase again.

From Waterloo Boris took the tube to Piccadilly Circus, walked up Shaftesbury Avenue, and turned into the maze of mean streets round Soho. Tommy followed at a judicious distance. They reached at length a small dilapidated square—a cul-de-sac, sinister in dirt and decay. Boris glanced round; Tommy drew back into a friendly porch.

From the doorway he watched Boris go up the steps of a particularly evil-looking house and rap, with a peculiar rhythm, on the door. It opened promptly; he passed inside.

It was at this juncture that Tommy lost his head. What any sane man would have done was wait. What he did do was entirely foreign to the sober common sense which was, as a rule, his leading characteristic. Something seemed to snap in his brain. He went up the steps and reproduced the peculiar knock as best he could.

The door swung open. A villainous-faced man with close-cropped hair stood in the doorway.

"Well?" he grunted.

The full realization of his folly began to come home to Tommy. But he dared not hesitate. He seized at the first words that came into his mind.

"Mr. Brown?"

To his surprise the man stood aside.

"Upstairs," he said, jerking his thumb. "Second door on your left."

## Chapter 3

THE ADVENTURES OF TOMMY

Tommy passed quietly into the house and mounted the ramshackle staircase. Everything was filthy beyond words. The man below had disappeared into a back room; clearly no suspicion attached. To come and ask for "Mr. Brown" appeared a reasonable proceeding.

At the top a narrow passage ran in front of him, doors on either side. From the one nearest on the left came a low murmur of voices. What fascinated him was a small recess immediately on his right, half concealed by a torn velvet curtain—directly opposite the left-hand door, commanding a view of the staircase. As a hiding-place it was ideal.

He thought it over in his slow, steady way. "Mr. Brown" was probably a password used by the gang; his lucky use of it had gained him admission. To enter the room itself would be a colossal risk—a further password might be required, or proof of identity. He could not hope to sustain his part indefinitely.

A repetition of the signal knock sounded below. Tommy slipped into the recess and drew the curtain across. There were rents enough in the ancient material to give him a good view.

The man who came up was unknown—obviously of the dregs of society, his low beetling brows and criminal jaw a type Scotland Yard would have recognized at a glance. He gave the signal knock at the door opposite. As he was admitted, Tommy caught a momentary glimpse of the room: four or five people round a long table, and at its head a tall man with close-cropped hair and a short, pointed, naval-looking beard, papers before him. He spoke with a curiously precise enunciation.

"Your number, comrade?"

"Fourteen, gov'nor," the other replied hoarsely.

"Correct."

"If that isn't a Hun, I'm a Dutchman," Tommy thought. "Lucky I didn't roll in. I'd have given the wrong number."

The next visitor proved an Irish Sinn Feiner; the next, a quiet, intelligent man like a city clerk; then a working-man whose face was vaguely familiar; then a man of commanding appearance, exquisitely dressed, evidently well born—his face also not unknown to the watcher, though he could not place it. A strange and sinister gathering. Who held in his finger these curiously variegated links of an unknown chain?

After his arrival there was a long wait. Tommy was cautiously creeping out when another knock sent him scuttling back. This last-comer came up so quietly he was almost abreast of Tommy before the young man realized his presence—small, very pale, with a gentle, almost womanish air. The angle of the cheek-bones hinted at his Slavonic ancestry. As he passed, he turned his head slowly. The strange light eyes seemed to burn through the curtain; Tommy could hardly believe that the man did not know he was there, and in spite of himself he shivered. The creature reminded him of a venomous snake.

His reception was different. The bearded man rose, and all the others followed suit. The German came forward and shook hands, his heels clicking together.

"We are honoured. I much feared that it would be impossible."

The other answered in a low voice with a kind of hiss in it: "There were difficulties. But one meeting is essential—to define my policy. I can do nothing without—Mr. Brown. He is here?"

The German's voice changed audibly. "We have received a message. It is impossible for him to be present in person." He gave a curious impression of having left the sentence unfinished.

A very slow smile overspread the other's face. "Ah! I understand. He works in the dark and trusts no one. But, all the same, it is possible that he is among us now...." He looked round, and an expression of fear swept the group. Each man seemed eyeing his neighbour doubtfully.

"So be it. Let us proceed."

Number Fourteen shut the door, and the voices sank once more to an undistinguishable murmur. Tommy must hear more. He removed his shoes and crept to the door, turned the handle by infinitesimal degrees, then pressed. The door did not budge. He pressed harder, then in desperation pushed with all his might. Locked or bolted on the inside.

"Well, I'm damned!" he muttered. "What a dirty trick!"

There was a bulldog tenacity about Tommy that made him slow to admit defeat. A little farther along the passage was a second door. He slipped through into an untenanted bedroom—and there was the thing he had hoped to find: a communicating door. The bolt was rusty; with patient wriggling he drew it back, then turned the handle. The door swung open a crack. A velvet *portière* inside prevented him seeing, but he could recognize the voices.

The Sinn Feiner was speaking. "More money is essential. No money—no results!"

"Will you guarantee that there *are* results?" That was Boris.

"In a month from now I will guarantee you such a reign of terror in Ireland as shall shake the British Empire to its foundations."

A pause; then the soft, sibilant accents of Number One: "Good! You shall have the money. Boris, you will see to that."

"Via the Irish Americans, and Mr. Potter as usual?"

"I guess that'll be all right," said a new voice with a transatlantic intonation, "though there's a growing disposition to let the Irish settle their own affairs without interference from America."

"Does that matter, since the money only nominally comes from the States?"

"Think of the feelings of Belfast if they could hear you!" said the well-bred voice.

"That is settled," said the sibilant tones. "Now, the loan to an English newspaper—Boris?"

"I think so."

"An official denial from Moscow will be forthcoming if necessary."

The German resumed: "I am directed by—Mr. Brown, to place the summaries of the reports from the different unions before you. The miners are most satisfactory. We must hold back the railways. There may be trouble with the A.S.E."

For a long time there was only the rustle of papers. Then fingers drumming on the table.

"And—the date, my friend?"

"The 29th."

"That is rather soon."

"It was settled by the principal Labour leaders. They must believe it to be entirely their own show."

The Russian laughed softly, as though amused. "Yes, yes. They must have no inkling that we are using them for our own ends. They are honest men—and that is their value to us. It is curious—but you cannot make a revolution without honest men. The instinct of the populace is infallible." He paused, and then repeated, as though the phrase pleased him: "Every revolution has had its honest men. They are soon disposed of afterwards."

There was a sinister note in his voice.

"Clymes must go," resumed the German. "He is too far-seeing. Number Fourteen will see to that."

"That's all right, gov'nor. Suppose I'm nabbed."

"You will have the best legal talent. But in any case you will wear gloves fitted with the finger-prints of a notorious housebreaker."

"Oh, I ain't afraid, gov'nor. All for the good of the cause. The streets is going to run with blood, so they say."

"Then all is arranged. We are assured of success?"

"I—think so." But the German spoke with less than his usual confidence.

Number One's voice held suddenly a dangerous quality: "What has gone wrong?"

"The Labour leaders. If they do not declare a general strike on the 29th—"

"Why should they not?"

"They're honest. Public opinion swings to the side of the Government. They will not go against it."

The Russian's fingers drummed. "I was given to understand there was a certain document in existence which assured success."

"That is so. If that document were placed before the leaders, they would publish it broadcast and declare for the revolution without a moment's hesitation. The Government would be broken finally and completely."

"Then what more do you want?"

"The document itself. It is not in our possession."

"But you know where it is?"

"No."

"Does anyone know?"

"One person—perhaps. And we are not sure of that even."

"Who?"

"A girl."

Tommy held his breath.

"A girl?" The Russian's voice rose contemptuously. "And you have not made her speak? In Russia we have ways."

"This case is different."

"How—different? Where is the girl now?"

"She is—"

But Tommy heard no more. A crashing blow descended on his head, and all was darkness.

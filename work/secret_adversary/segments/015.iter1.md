A CONSULTATION

Nothing was more surprising and bewildering to Tuppence than the ease and simplicity with which everything was arranged, owing to Sir James’s skilful handling. The doctor accepted readily that Mrs. Vandemeyer had accidentally taken an overdose of chloral, doubted an inquest would be necessary, and promised to communicate with Sir James. Shortly afterwards a nurse arrived to take charge, and the others left the ill-omened building.

“And what now?” asked Julius, with a gesture of despair. “I guess we’re down and out for good.”

“No,” said Sir James quietly. “There is still Dr. Hall. He is staying at the Métropole. Let us call on him after a bath and breakfast.”

It was arranged that Tuppence and Julius should return to the Ritz and call for Sir James in the car. A little after eleven they drew up before the hotel, and a page went in search of Dr. Hall. In a few minutes the little doctor came hurrying towards them.

“Can you spare us a few minutes, Dr. Hall?” said Sir James. “Miss Cowley. Mr. Hersheimmer, I think you already know.”

“Aha—my young friend of the tree episode! Ankle all right, eh?”

“I guess it’s cured owing to your skilful treatment, doc.”

“And the heart trouble? Ha ha!”

“Still searching,” said Julius.

“To the point—can we have a word in private?” asked Sir James.

“Certainly.” He led them to a quiet room, and looked inquiringly at Sir James.

“I am anxious to find a certain young lady for the purpose of obtaining a statement. I believe she has been, at one time or another, in your establishment at Bournemouth. I hope I transgress no professional etiquette in questioning you?”

“I suppose it is a matter of testimony?”

Sir James hesitated, then nodded.

“I shall be pleased to give you any information in my power. What is the young lady’s name? Mr. Hersheimmer asked me, I remember——”

“The name is immaterial,” said Sir James bluntly. “She would almost certainly have been sent to you under an assumed one. Are you acquainted with a Mrs. Vandemeyer?”

“Mrs. Vandemeyer, of 20 South Audley Mansions? Slightly.”

“You are not aware she is dead?”

“Dear, dear! When did it happen?”

“She took an overdose of chloral last night.”

“Purposely?”

“Accidentally, it is believed. I found her dead this morning.”

“Very sad. A singularly handsome woman.” He paused. “But I do not see—”

“It bears thus: did not Mrs. Vandemeyer commit a young relative to your charge?”

Julius leaned forward.

“That is the case,” said the doctor quietly.

“Under the name of——?”

“Janet Vandemeyer. I understood her to be a niece.”

“And she came to you——?”

“In June or July of 1915, as far as I remember.”

“Was she a mental case?”

“She is perfectly sane, if that is what you mean. I understood from Mrs. Vandemeyer that the girl had been with her on the Lusitania, and had suffered a severe shock.”

“We’re on the right track,” said Sir James.

“As I said before, I’m a mutt,” muttered Julius.

“You spoke of wanting a statement,” said Dr. Hall. “Supposing she is not able to give one?”

“What? You have just said she is perfectly sane,” said Sir James.

“So she is. Nevertheless, if you want a statement concerning any events prior to May 7, 1915, she will not be able to give it to you.”

They stared. The little man nodded cheerfully.

“An interesting case—very interesting. Not so uncommon as you would think. It’s the first of the kind I’ve had under my own observation, and I must admit that I’ve found it of absorbing interest.” There was something rather ghoulish in the little man’s satisfaction. “Janet Vandemeyer is suffering from a complete loss of memory.”

“Nothing prior to May 7,” he went on. “After that her memory is as good as yours or mine. The first thing she remembers is landing with the survivors. She did not know her own name, or where she had come from; she could not even speak her own tongue.”

“But surely that is unusual?” said Julius.

“No, my dear sir, quite normal under the circumstances. Severe shock to the nervous system. I suggested a specialist—a very good man in Paris—but Mrs. Vandemeyer opposed the publicity that might result.”

“I can imagine she would,” said Sir James grimly.

“I fell in with her views. The girl was very young—nineteen, I believe. There is really no special treatment; it is a matter of waiting.”

“Waiting?” said Sir James.

“Yes—sooner or later the memory returns—suddenly, as it went. Often the intervening period is forgotten, and life is taken up where it left off—at the sinking of the Lusitania.”

“And when?” asked Sir James.

The doctor shrugged. “Sometimes months, sometimes years—twenty years have been known. Sometimes another shock does the trick. One restores what the other took away.”

“Another shock, eh?” said Julius thoughtfully.

The little man’s voice trailed on. Julius, frowning, struck the table with his fist. They all jumped.

“I’ve got it! Doc, suppose we re-created the whole catastrophe—the submarine, the sinking ship, the rush to the boats. Wouldn’t that big bump to her subconscious self set it going again?”

“A very interesting speculation. In my opinion, it would be successful. It is unfortunate there is no chance of the conditions repeating themselves.”

“Not by nature,” said Julius. “I’m talking about art.” In a few hard phrases he sketched hiring ship and passengers, and a panic well drilled for the screen.

Dr. Hall stared, then found his voice.

“But the expense—my dear sir—the expense! It will be colossal!”

“Money doesn’t worry me,” said Julius simply.

Sir James smiled slightly. “Mr. Hersheimmer is very well off—very well off indeed.”

The doctor’s glance came back to Julius with a new and subtle quality in it. This was no longer an eccentric young fellow with a habit of falling off trees. The doctor’s eyes held the deference accorded to a really rich man.

“Very remarkable plan. Very remarkable,” he murmured. “And you really mean to carry out this remarkable plan of yours.”

“You bet your bottom dollar I do.”

The doctor believed him—which was a tribute to his nationality. If an Englishman had suggested such a thing, he would have had grave doubts as to his sanity.

“I cannot guarantee a cure,” he pointed out.

“Sure. You just trot out Jane and leave the rest to me.”

“Jane?”

“Miss Janet Vandemeyer. Can we get on long distance to your place and ask them to send her up—or shall I fetch her in my car?”

The doctor stared. “I beg your pardon, Mr. Hersheimmer. I thought you understood.”

“Understood what?”

“That Miss Vandemeyer is no longer under my care.”

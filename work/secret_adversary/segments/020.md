It was Sir James Peel Edgerton, however, who reached them first, with a wire summoning them both to Manchester. Julius had got in half an hour earlier and led the way out of the station as Tommy arrived.

"He's booked rooms for us, and will be round to dine at eight."

"What made you think he'd ceased to take any interest in the case?" Tommy asked curiously.

"What he said," Julius replied dryly. "The old bird's as close as an oyster. Wouldn't commit himself till he was sure he could deliver the goods."

Tommy shook his head, unconvinced.

Sir James arrived punctually. "I have heard so much about you from Miss Tuppence," he said, shaking Tommy's hand warmly, "that it really seems as though I already know you quite well."

Tommy felt at once the magnetism of the lawyer's personality. He was reminded of Mr. Carter — the same keen-edged quality of mind beneath the professional reserve. He was conscious, too, of being read through and through, while learning nothing in return. Sir James took in everything, but gave out only what he chose.

Julius broke into eager questions. How had Sir James tracked the girl? Why had he kept silent?

The lawyer stroked his chin and smiled. "Just so, just so. Well, she's found. That's the great thing, isn't it?"

"Sure it is. But just how did you strike her trail? We thought you'd quit for good."

"Did you really? H'm, dear me." He shot a lightning glance. "Well, I don't know that I should go so far as to say that."

"But where is she?"

"The young lady was knocked down in a street accident, and has sustained slight injuries to the head. She was taken to the infirmary, and on recovering consciousness gave her name as Jane Finn. I arranged for her to be removed to the house of a friend, and wired at once for you. She has not spoken since."

"Her memory's come back?" cried Julius.

"Undoubtedly, since she was able to give her real name."

"And you just happened to be on the spot," said Tommy. "Seems quite like a fairy tale."

"Coincidences are curious things," said Sir James dryly.

But Tommy was now certain of what he had only suspected. Sir James had not abandoned the case at all; by some means of his own, he had run the missing girl to earth.

"After dinner," Julius announced, "I'll go right away and see Jane."

"Quite impossible. They would not allow visitors at this hour. To-morrow morning at ten."

"All the same, I reckon I'll go round to-night and ginger them up."

"It will be quite useless, Mr. Hersheimmer."

The words came out like the crack of a pistol. For a moment hostility flared between the two men; then Julius lowered his eyes. "For the moment, I reckon you're the boss."

Sir James turned smoothly to Tommy. He had heard, he said, that his friends had been anxious. Helped by questions, Tommy gave an abbreviated account of his adventures, including the moment near the end when a girl in the house had cried out, wanting to be taken to 'Marguerite.'

"You got yourself out of a tight place very well," Sir James said gravely. "I congratulate you."

"I couldn't have got away but for the girl, sir."

"It was lucky for you she happened to take a fancy to you. There's no doubt about her being one of the gang?"

"I'm afraid not. She went back to them when she could have got away."

Sir James nodded. "Mrs. Vandemeyer always signed herself Rita — never Marguerite. Curious that the girl was crying out for her by her full name at the very moment Vandemeyer was either dead or dying. There are one or two points that strike me as obscure. The house was raided?"

"They'd all cleared out. Not a clue left behind."

"I wonder——" The lawyer tapped the table. "And since then? What have you been doing?"

For a moment Tommy stared. Then it dawned on him — Sir James didn't know.

"I forgot you didn't know about Tuppence."

The lawyer laid down his knife and fork sharply. "Has anything happened to Miss Tuppence?"

"She's disappeared," said Julius. "A week ago."

Between them they gave the history of the futile search. Sir James went at once to the root of the matter.

"A wire signed with your name? Their kidnapping of Miss Tuppence is the counter-move to your escape. They could seal your lips with a threat of what might happen to her."

"That's just what I thought, sir."

"Not bad. The curious thing is that they certainly did not know anything about you when they first held you prisoner. Some one put them wise — and not earlier than Sunday afternoon."

"That almighty omniscient Mr. Brown, of course!" There was a note of derision in Julius's voice.

"You don't believe in Mr. Brown?"

"No, sir. I reckon he's a figurehead — a bogy name to frighten the children. The real head is that Russian, Kramenin."

"I disagree. Mr. Brown exists." Sir James turned to Tommy. "We'll deal with Miss Jane Finn first. Afterwards, we'll set to work to rescue Miss Tuppence. She's in no immediate danger so long as they don't know we've got Jane Finn, and that her memory has returned. We must keep that dark at all costs."

At ten the next morning they were shown upstairs. Tommy's heart beat sensibly faster. Jane Finn at last! The long-sought, the elusive Jane Finn — the girl who held the future of England in her hands. If only Tuppence could have been at his side.

On the white bed, bandages round her head, lay the girl. The whole scene seemed unreal — so exactly what one expected that it gave the effect of being beautifully staged.

"Miss Finn," said Sir James, "this is your cousin, Mr. Julius P. Hersheimmer."

A faint flush flitted over the girl's face. "Are you really Uncle Hiram's son?"

Her voice, with its slight Western warmth, had an almost thrilling quality. It seemed vaguely familiar to Tommy, but he thrust the impression aside as impossible.

"Sure thing," said Julius, with a tremor in his voice.

"They've been telling me dreadful things — that my memory went, and that there are years lost out of my life. It seems no time since we were being hustled into those boats."

"Don't worry. Now, Jane — there was a man on that boat with some mighty important papers. The big guns in this country have a notion he passed them to you."

"Yes. He said they'd have a better chance with me, because they would save the women and children first. He said they might make all the difference to the Allies. Then you can hand them over right away?"

"But I haven't got them. I hid them. I got uneasy. People seemed to be watching me."

"Go on," said Sir James in his quiet penetrating tones. "What do you remember?"

"It was at Holyhead. In the confusion on the quay I slipped away. I took a car, told the man to drive me out of the town. The path led to the cliff, and down to the sea between big yellow gorse bushes — they were like golden flames. Just level with my head was a hole in the rock — small, but it went a long way back. I took the oilskin packet from round my neck and shoved it right in. Then I tore off a bit of gorse and plugged the hole. I marked the place in my own mind. There was a queer boulder in the path just there — for all the world like a dog sitting up begging. I went back and caught the train. By and by, I saw the man opposite me wink at the woman next to me, and was glad the papers were safe. I went out in the corridor. The woman called me back, said I'd dropped something, and when I stooped, something seemed to hit me — here." She placed her hand to the back of her head. "I don't remember anything more until I woke up in the hospital."

"Thank you, Miss Finn," said Sir James.

Julius took her hand. "So long, Cousin Jane. I'm going to get busy after those papers."

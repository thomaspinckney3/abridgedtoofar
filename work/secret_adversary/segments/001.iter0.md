It was 2 p.m. on May 7, 1915. The Lusitania had been struck by two torpedoes and was sinking rapidly; the boats were being launched with all possible speed. Women and children were being lined up. Some clung to husbands and fathers; others clasped their children. One girl stood a little apart, quite young—eighteen—grave, steadfast eyes looking straight ahead.

“I beg your pardon.”

A man’s voice beside her made her start and turn. She had noticed him more than once amongst the first-class passengers: a hint of mystery, a nervous way of glancing over his shoulder, quick to rebuff any overture. Now he was greatly agitated; beads of perspiration stood on his brow. He did not strike her as the kind of man afraid to meet death, and yet he was in overmastering fear.

“Yes?” Her grave eyes met his.

He stood with desperate irresolution. “It must be! Yes—it is the only way.” Aloud, abruptly: “You are an American?”

“Yes.”

“A patriotic one?”

She flushed. “I guess you’ve no right to ask such a thing! Of course I am!”

“Don’t be offended. You wouldn’t be if you knew how much there was at stake. But I’ve got to trust some one—and it must be a woman.”

“Why?”

“Because of ‘women and children first.’” He lowered his voice. “I’m carrying papers—vitally important papers. They may make all the difference to the Allies in the war. These papers have got to be saved! They’ve more chance with you than with me. Will you take them?”

She held out her hand.

“Wait—I must warn you. There may be a risk—if I’ve been followed. I don’t think I have, but one never knows. If so, there will be danger. Have you the nerve to go through with it?”

The girl smiled. “I’ll go through with it all right. And I’m real proud to be chosen! What am I to do with them afterwards?”

“Watch the newspapers! I’ll advertise in the personal column of the Times, beginning ‘Shipmate.’ At the end of three days if there’s nothing—well, you’ll know I’m down and out. Then take the packet to the American Embassy, and deliver it into the Ambassador’s own hands. Is that clear?”

“Quite clear.”

“Then be ready—I’m going to say good-bye.” He took her hand. “Good-bye. Good luck to you,” he said in a louder tone.

Her hand closed on the oilskin packet that had lain in his palm.

The Lusitania settled with a more decided list to starboard. At a quick command, the girl went forward to take her place in the boat.

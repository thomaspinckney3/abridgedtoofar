The Prime Minister’s fingers tapped the desk; his face was worn. “Do you really mean things are not so desperate after all?”

“So this lad seems to think,” said Mr. Carter, handing him a sprawling boyish letter.

“DEAR MR. CARTER,

“Something’s turned up that has given me a jar. Of course I may be an awful ass, but I don’t think so. If I’m right, that girl at Manchester was just a plant. Sham packet and all—prearranged to make us think the game was up; therefore we must have been pretty hot on the scent.

“I think I know who the real Jane Finn is, and I’ve even got an idea where the papers are. That last’s only a guess, but I’ve a sort of feeling it’ll turn out right. Anyhow, I enclose it in a sealed envelope for what it’s worth. Please don’t open it till the very last moment—midnight on the 28th—you’ll understand why in a minute.

“You see, I’ve figured it out that those things of Tuppence’s are a plant too, and she’s no more drowned than I am. As a last chance they’ll let Jane Finn escape, hoping she’s been shamming this memory stunt, and that once she thinks she’s free she’ll go right away to the cache. Of course it’s an awful risk for them, because she knows all about them—but they’re desperate for that treaty. But if they know the papers have been recovered by us, neither of those two girls’ lives will be worth an hour’s purchase. I must try and get hold of Tuppence before Jane escapes.

“I want a repeat of that telegram sent to Tuppence at the Ritz. Sir James Peel Edgerton said you’d manage that for me. He’s frightfully clever.

“One last thing—please have that house in Soho watched day and night.

“Yours, etc., THOMAS BERESFORD.”

“The enclosure?”

“In the vaults of the Bank,” said Carter. “I’m taking no chances.”

“Would it not be better to open it now?” suggested the Prime Minister. “Secure the document at once, and keep it secret?”

“Can we keep it secret?” Carter snapped his fingers. “Spies all round us. Once it’s known, I wouldn’t give that—for the lives of those girls. The boy trusted me; I shan’t let him down.”

He went on: “Outwardly, he’s an ordinary, clean‑limbed, rather block‑headed young Englishman—slow. But impossible to lead astray through imagination—he hasn’t any. He worries things out, and once he’s got hold of anything he doesn’t let go. The little lady’s quite different. More intuition and less common sense. They make a pretty pair: pace and stamina.”

“He seems confident,” mused the Prime Minister.

“Yes, and that’s what gives me hope. He’s the kind of diffident youth who would have to be _very_ sure before he ventured an opinion at all.”

“And it is this boy who will defeat the master criminal of our time?” murmured the Prime Minister.

“This—boy. But I sometimes fancy I see a shadow behind.”

“Peel Edgerton?” The Prime Minister was surprised.

“Yes. I see his hand in this.” He struck the letter. “If anyone runs Mr. Brown to earth, Peel Edgerton will. He’s on the case now, and doesn’t want it known. He sent me a cutting from an American paper—man’s body found near the docks in New York about three weeks ago; young fellow, thirty‑five, poorly dressed, face badly disfigured—never identified. He asked for any information.”

“And you fancy the matters are connected?”

“Somehow I do.”

Sir James arrived. A half whimsical thought flashed across the Premier’s mind. “My successor, perhaps!” To Carter’s direct question he said smilingly: “He rang me up. Thanked me for a letter—I’d offered him a job. Then he reminded me that Manchester bogus telegram. I asked if anything untoward had occurred. He said it had: in a drawer in Mr. Hersheimmer’s room he had discovered a photograph.”

“I asked him if the photograph bore the name and address of a Californian photographer. He replied: ‘You’re on to it, sir. It had.’”

“The French girl, Annette, who saved his life,” Sir James added. “I asked what he had done with it. He replied: put it back. That was good. From the moment the Manchester girl was proved a plant, everything was altered. Young Beresford saw that without my telling him. But he couldn’t trust his judgment on Miss Cowley; did I think she was alive? Weighing the evidence, I told him there was a very decided chance in favour of it. That brought us to the telegram. I advised him to apply to you for a copy of the original wire. Words might have been erased and altered with the express intention of sending searchers on a false trail.”

Carter produced a paper and read: “Come at once, Astley Priors, Gatehouse, Kent. Great developments—TOMMY.”

“Very simple and very ingenious,” said Sir James. “And the one clue they overlooked was the page‑boy’s statement that Miss Cowley drove to Charing Cross. They were so sure of themselves they took it for granted he was mistaken.”

“Then young Beresford is now—?”

“At Gatehouse, Kent, unless I’m much mistaken.”

“I rather wonder you’re not there too.”

“I’m busy on a case,” said Sir James. “Perhaps it would be more correct to say I’m preparing a case.”

“What I don’t understand,” said the Prime Minister suddenly, “is how that photograph came to be in Mr. Hersheimmer’s drawer.”

“Perhaps it never left it,” suggested the lawyer gently.

“But the bogus inspector? Inspector Brown?”

“Ah!” said Sir James thoughtfully. He rose. “I mustn’t keep you. Go on with the affairs of the nation. I must get back to—my case.”

Two days later Julius Hersheimmer came back from Manchester. A note lay on his table:

“DEAR HERSHEIMMER,

“Sorry I lost my temper. In case I don’t see you again, good‑bye. I’ve been offered a job in the Argentine, and might as well take it.

“Yours, TOMMY BERESFORD.”

A peculiar smile lingered on Julius’s face. He threw the letter into the basket.

“The darned fool!” he murmured.

They pinched and walked on Thursday; “walking is cheap,” decreed Tuppence. On Friday, letters might be expected. Tommy, honour-bound not to open them, met her at ten in the National Gallery. Tuppence, on a red velvet seat, stared blindly at Turners till he came.

“Well?”

“Well,” said Tommy provokingly. “Which is your favourite picture?”

“Don’t be a wretch. Aren’t there any answers?”

Tommy lengthened his face. “It’s too bad. Good money wasted. Still—there it is. The advertisement has appeared, and—there are only two answers!”

“Tommy, you devil! Give them to me. How could you be so mean!”

“Your language, Tuppence, your language! They’re very particular at the National Gallery. Government show, you know. And do remember, as I have pointed out to you before, that as a clergyman’s daughter——”

“I ought to be on the stage!” finished Tuppence with a snap.

“That is not what I intended to say. But if you are sure that you have enjoyed to the full the reaction of joy after despair with which I have kindly provided you free of charge, let us get down to our mail, as the saying goes.”

She snatched the envelopes and scrutinized them. “Thick paper—this looks rich—we’ll keep it to the last.” She ripped open the first.

“DEAR SIR, Referring to your advertisement, I may be able to be of some use to you. Perhaps you could call and see me at the above address at eleven o’clock to-morrow morning. Yours truly, A. CARTER. 27 Carshalton Gardens.”

“The plan of campaign,” said Tommy. “Ushered in: ‘Please take a seat, Mr.—er?’ I reply: ‘Edward Whittington!’ He turns purple and gasps: ‘How much?’ I pocket the fifty—”

“Don’t be absurd. Now the other. Oh, this is from the Ritz! ‘DEAR SIR, Re your advertisement, I should be glad if you would call round somewhere about lunch-time. Yours truly, JULIUS P. HERSHEIMMER.’”

“Ha!” said Tommy. “Do I smell a Boche? Or only an American millionaire of unfortunate ancestry? At all events we’ll call at lunch-time. It’s a good time—frequently leads to free food for two.”

They hurried to Carshalton Gardens, were shown into a small study, and a tall man with a lean, hawklike face and a tired manner came in.

“Mr. Y. A.?” He smiled. “Do sit down, both of you.”

“We wanted to know—would you be so kind as to tell us anything you know about Jane Finn?” Tuppence began.

“Jane Finn? Ah!” He reflected. “Well, the question is, what do you know about her?”

“That’s got nothing to do with it,” said Tuppence, drawing herself up.

“No? But it has, you know.” He smiled again. “Come now—what do you know? Suppose you tell me....”

Tuppence struggled against his quiet persuasiveness. “We couldn’t do that, could we, Tommy?”

But Tommy’s tone held deference. “I dare say the little we know won’t be any good to you, sir. But such as it is, you’re welcome to it.”

His eyes questioned; Tommy nodded. “Yes, sir, I recognized you at once. Saw you in France—with the Intelligence.”

“No names, please,” he said quickly.

“I’m known as Mr. Carter here. Now—who’s going to tell me the story?”

Tuppence told it from the forming of the Young Adventurers, Ltd., downwards. Mr. Carter listened in silence, the tired manner resumed.

“Not much. But suggestive. Quite suggestive. You’re a curious young couple. You might succeed where others have failed. I believe in luck, you know—always have.... Well, how about it? You’re out for adventure. How would you like to work for me? All quite unofficial. Expenses paid, and a moderate screw?”

“What should we have to do?”

“Just go on with what you’re doing now. Find Jane Finn.”

“Yes, but—who is Jane Finn?”

“You’re entitled to know that.”

He leaned back. “Secret diplomacy (which, by the way, is nearly always bad policy!) does not concern you. In early 1915, a certain document—a draft of a secret agreement—was drawn up in America. It was dispatched to England by a special messenger, a young fellow called Danvers. He sailed on the Lusitania, carrying the papers in an oilskin packet next his skin. The Lusitania was torpedoed. Danvers was among the missing; later his body was washed ashore—identified beyond doubt. But the oilskin packet was missing.

“Had it been taken? Or had he passed it on? After the torpedo struck, Danvers was seen speaking to a young American girl. No one actually saw him pass anything, but it’s likely he entrusted the papers to her, believing she had a better chance. Her name was Jane Finn. She appeared among the survivors; then vanished. We traced her across Ireland; once she set foot in England, nothing. The treaty was never used; the war shifted; the thing was forgotten.”

Tuppence broke in: “But why has it cropped up again? The war’s over.”

“Because it seems the papers were not destroyed after all, and might be resurrected with a new and deadly significance. Five years ago that draft treaty was a weapon in our hands; to-day it is a weapon against us. If its terms were made public, it would mean disaster.... It might possibly bring about another war—not with Germany this time! It implicates a number of our statesmen whom we cannot afford to have discredited. As a party cry for Labour it would be irresistible; but that is a mere nothing to the real danger.”

He went on quietly: “There is Bolshevist influence at work behind the present Labour unrest. Bolshevist gold is pouring into this country for the specific purpose of procuring a Revolution. And there is a certain man—his real name unknown—working in the dark for his own ends. The Bolshevists are behind the Labour unrest—but this man is behind the Bolshevists. He is always spoken of by the unassuming title of ‘Mr. Brown.’ He is the master criminal of this age. He controls a marvellous organization. Most of the Peace propaganda during the war was originated and financed by him. His spies are everywhere.”

“A naturalized German?” asked Tommy.

“On the contrary, I have every reason to believe he is an Englishman.”

“Where we find his tracks,” Carter continued, “somebody else has the chief rôle; afterwards we discover some nonentity in the background—and Mr. Brown has escaped us once more.”

“I wonder—” said Tuppence slowly. “In Mr. Whittington’s office the clerk—he called him Brown. He brought a telephone message.”

“Very likely,” said Carter. “That ‘message’ was Mr. Brown’s way of giving an order. He overheard the whole conversation. Was it after that that Whittington gave you money and an appointment for the following day?”

Tuppence nodded.

“Yes, undoubtedly the hand of Mr. Brown! Well, there it is—you see what you’re pitting yourselves against. I don’t quite like it. You’re such young things. I shouldn’t like anything to happen to you.”

“It won’t,” said Tuppence positively.

“I’ll look after her, sir,” said Tommy.

“And I’ll look after you,” retorted Tuppence.

“Then look after each other.” He smiled, then grew grave. “There’s something mysterious about this draft treaty. We’ve been threatened with it. The Revolutionary element as good as declare it’s in their hands—and intend to produce it. Yet they’re at fault about many provisions. It’s as though they’d got hold of an incriminating document, but couldn’t read it because it was in cipher—only we know it wasn’t in cipher. But there’s something. Of course Jane Finn may be dead—but I don’t think so. The curious thing is that they’re trying to get information about the girl from us.”

“What?”

“They know we’re looking. They’ll produce a Jane Finn of their own—say at a pensionnat in Paris.” Tuppence gasped; he smiled. “No one knows what she looks like. She’s primed with a trumped-up tale; her real business is to get as much information as possible out of us. See?”

“Then you think it was as Jane Finn they wanted me to go to Paris?”

“I believe in coincidences, you know,” said Mr. Carter wearily.

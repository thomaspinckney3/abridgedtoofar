THE YOUNG ADVENTURERS, LTD.

“TOMMY, old thing!”

“Tuppence, old bean!”

The two young people greeted each other affectionately, momentarily
blocking the Dover Street Tube exit. The adjective “old” was misleading.
Their united ages would certainly not have totalled forty-five.

“Not seen you for simply centuries,” continued the young man. “Where are
you off to? Come and chew a bun with me. We’re getting a bit unpopular
here--blocking the gangway as it were. Let’s get out of it.”

They walked down Dover Street towards Piccadilly.

“Now then,” said Tommy, “where shall we go?”

The very faint anxiety which underlay his tone did not escape the astute
ears of Miss Prudence Cowley, known to her intimate friends for some
mysterious reason as “Tuppence.” She pounced at once.

“Tommy, you’re stony!”

“Not a bit of it,” declared Tommy unconvincingly. “Rolling in cash.”

“You always were a shocking liar,” said Tuppence severely, “though you
did once persuade Sister Greenbank that the doctor had ordered you beer
as a tonic, but forgotten to write it on the chart. Do you remember?”

Tommy chuckled.

“I should think I did! Wasn’t the old cat in a rage when she found
out? Not that she was a bad sort really, old Mother Greenbank! Good old
hospital--demobbed like everything else, I suppose?”

“Yes. You too?”

“Two months ago.”

“Gratuity?”

“Spent.”

“Oh, Tommy!”

“No, old thing, not in riotous dissipation. No such luck! The cost of
living--ordinary plain, or garden living nowadays is, I assure you, if
you do not know----”

“My dear child, there is nothing I do _not_ know about the cost of living.
Here we are at Lyons’, and we will each of us pay for our own. That’s
it!” And Tuppence led the way upstairs.

The place was full, and as they wandered looking for a table, they
caught scraps of conversation.

“Funny scraps one does overhear,” murmured Tommy. “I passed two Johnnies
in the street to-day talking about some one called Jane Finn. Did you
ever hear such a name?”

Two elderly ladies rose; Tuppence slipped into a vacant seat. Tommy
ordered tea and buns. Tuppence ordered tea and buttered toast.

“And mind the tea comes in separate teapots,” she added severely.

They made a modern-looking pair. Tommy’s slicked-back red hair crowned a
pleasantly ugly gentleman-sportsman face; his brown suit was well cut
but perilously near the end of its tether. Tuppence, elfin-faced, with a
determined chin and wide grey eyes under straight black brows, wore a
small bright green toque over her black bob, and a very short shabby
skirt revealing uncommonly dainty ankles--a valiant attempt at smartness.

“Now then,” said Tommy, biting into a bun, “let’s get up-to-date. I
haven’t seen you since hospital, 1916.”

“Very well.” Tuppence helped herself to buttered toast. “Abridged
biography of Miss Prudence Cowley, fifth daughter of Archdeacon Cowley
of Little Missendell, Suffolk. Came up to an officers’ hospital. First
month: Washed up six hundred and forty-eight plates daily. Promotions
ensued: drying, potatoes, bread and butter, wardmaid with mop and pail,
then waiting at table, then on the Sisters! Eighth month: Slight check.
Sister Bond ate Sister Westhaven’s egg! Grand row! Wardmaid clearly to
blame! Mop and pail again! Ninth: Promoted to sweeping out wards, where
I found a friend of my childhood in Lieutenant Thomas Beresford (bow,
Tommy!), whom I had not seen for five long years. Tenth: Reproved by
matron for visiting the pictures in company with one of the patients,
namely the aforementioned Lieutenant Thomas Beresford. Eleventh and
twelfth: Parlourmaid triumph. Left in a blaze of glory. Then I drove a
trade delivery van, a motor-lorry, and a general--quite a young general!
Entered a Government office; many enjoyable tea parties. I meant to be a
land girl, postwoman, and bus conductress to round off my career--but
the Armistice intervened! Clung on limpet-wise till I was combed out.
Since then--job hunting.”

“There’s not so much promotion in mine,” said Tommy regretfully. “France
again; Mesopotamia; wounded a second time; hospital; stuck in Egypt till
the Armistice; demobbed; and for ten long, weary months--job hunting!
There aren’t any jobs! What good am I? What do I know about business?
Nothing.”

“What about the colonies?”

“I shouldn’t like the colonies--and I’m certain they wouldn’t like me!”

“Rich relations?”

“I’ve got an old uncle who’s more or less rolling, but he’s no good.”

“Why not?”

“Wanted to adopt me once. I refused.”

“You refused because of your mother----”

Tommy flushed. “Yes. It would have been rough on the mater. As you know,
I was all she had. Old boy hated her--wanted to get me away. Just a bit
of spite.”

“Your mother’s dead, isn’t she?” said Tuppence gently.

Tommy nodded.

“You’re a good sort, Tommy. I always knew it.”

“Rot!... Well, I’m desperate.”

“So am I! I’ve answered advertisements, screwed and saved and pinched!
No good. I shall have to go home!”

“Don’t you want to?”

“Of course I don’t! Father’s a dear--but short skirts and smoking are
immoral. Seven of us at home--housework and mothers’ meetings! I’ve
always been the changeling. I don’t want to go back, but--oh, Tommy,
what else is there to do?”

They sat in gloomy silence.

“Money, money, money!” burst out Tuppence. “I think about it morning,
noon and night! There are only three ways: to be left it, to marry it,
or to make it. First ruled out. No rich elderly relatives; the old ladies
I help over crossings never even ask my name!”

“Of course,” she resumed, “marriage is my best chance. I made up my mind
to marry money when I was quite young. Any thinking girl would! I’m not
sentimental, you know.”

“Certainly not,” agreed Tommy hastily.

“Well, there it is! I’m willing--but I never meet any rich men! All the
boys I know are as hard up as I am.”

“What about the general?”

“I fancy he keeps a bicycle shop in time of peace. No--there it is! Now
you could marry a rich girl.”

“I’m like you. I don’t know any.”

“That doesn’t matter. You tread on her foot, or pick up her
handkerchief--if she thinks you want to know her she’s flattered.”

“You overrate my manly charms,” murmured Tommy.

“On the other hand, my millionaire would probably run for his life! No--
marriage is fraught with difficulties. Remains--to make money!”

“We’ve tried that, and failed,” Tommy reminded her.

“The orthodox ways, yes. Suppose we try the unorthodox. Tommy, let’s be
adventurers!”

“Certainly,” replied Tommy cheerfully. “How do we begin?”

“That’s the difficulty. If we could make ourselves known, people might
hire us to commit crimes for them.”

“Delightful,” commented Tommy. “Especially from a clergyman’s daughter!”

“The moral guilt would be theirs--not mine. There’s a difference between
stealing a diamond necklace for yourself and being hired to steal it.”

“There wouldn’t be the least difference if you were caught!”

“Perhaps not. But I shouldn’t be caught. I’m so clever.”

“Modesty always was your besetting sin.”

“Don’t rag. Look here, Tommy, shall we really? Shall we form a business
partnership?”

“Form a company for the stealing of diamond necklaces?”

“That was only an illustration. Let’s have a--what do you call it in
book-keeping?... Oh, I know--a joint venture! It’s got an Elizabethan
flavour--galleons and doubloons. A joint venture!”

“Trading under the name of the Young Adventurers, Ltd.? Is that your
idea, Tuppence?”

“It’s all very well to laugh, but I feel there might be something in it.”

“How do you propose to get in touch with your would-be employers?”

“Advertisement. Have you a bit of paper?” Tommy produced a shabby green
notebook. Tuppence wrote busily. “Shall we begin: ‘Young officer, twice
wounded in the war----’”

“Certainly not.”

“Oh, very well. The papers are brimful of that. Now listen--how’s this?
‘Two young adventurers for hire. Willing to do anything, go anywhere.
Pay must be good.’ (We might as well make that clear.) ‘No unreasonable
offer refused’--like flats and furniture.”

“I should think any offer we get would be a pretty unreasonable one!”

“Tommy! You’re a genius! ‘No unreasonable offer refused--if pay is
good.’”

“I shouldn’t mention pay again. It looks rather eager.”

“It couldn’t look as eager as I feel! But perhaps you’re right. Now
I’ll read it straight. ‘Two young adventurers for hire. Willing to do
anything, go anywhere. Pay must be good. No unreasonable offer refused.’”

“It would strike me as either a hoax, or written by a lunatic.”

“It’s not half so insane as a thing I read this morning beginning
‘Petunia’ and signed ‘Best Boy.’” She tore out the leaf. “There you are.
Times, I think. Reply to Box so-and-so. I expect about five shillings.
Here’s half a crown for my share.”

“Shall we really try it?” said Tommy at last. “Shall we, Tuppence? Just
for the fun of the thing?”

“Tommy, you’re a sport! Let’s drink to success.” She poured the cold
dregs of tea into the cups. “Here’s to our joint venture, and may it
prosper!”

“The Young Adventurers, Ltd.!” responded Tommy.

They laughed rather uncertainly, then rose.

“I must return to my palatial suite at the hostel.”

“Perhaps it is time I strolled round to the Ritz,” grinned Tommy. “Where
and when?”

“Twelve o’clock to-morrow. Piccadilly Tube station.”

“My time is my own,” replied Mr. Beresford magnificently.

“So long, then.”

“Good-bye, old thing.”

They went off in opposite directions. Tuppence, bound for her hostel in
Southern Belgravia, for reasons of economy did not take a bus. Half-way
across St. James’s Park, a man’s voice behind her made her start.

“Excuse me,” it said. “But may I speak to you for a moment?”

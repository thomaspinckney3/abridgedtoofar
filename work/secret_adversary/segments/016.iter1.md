Julius sprang up. “What?”

“I thought you were aware of that.”

“When did she leave?”

“To-day is Monday? Then last Wednesday. Yes—after your—er—fall from my tree. A very urgent message came from Mrs. Vandemeyer. The young lady and the nurse in charge of her left by the night train.”

“Nurse Edith—left with a patient,” muttered Julius. “My God, to have been so near!”

Dr. Hall looked bewildered. “I don’t understand. Is the young lady not with her aunt, after all?”

Tuppence was about to speak; a glance from Sir James checked her. The lawyer rose.

“I’m much obliged to you, Hall. We’re grateful for all you’ve told us. We must track Miss Vandemeyer anew. The nurse—do you know where she is?”

The doctor shook his head. “We’ve not heard. I understood she was to remain with Miss Vandemeyer for a while. But what can have happened? Surely the girl has not been kidnapped.”

“That remains to be seen,” said Sir James gravely.

“You do not think I ought to go to the police?”

“No, no. In all probability the young lady is with other relations.”

Not wholly satisfied, the doctor wished them good-bye, and they left the hotel.

“How maddening,” cried Tuppence, “to think Julius was actually under the same roof with her for a few hours.”

“I was a darned idiot,” muttered Julius.

“You couldn’t know,” said Tuppence. “Could he?” she appealed.

“I should advise you not to worry,” said Sir James kindly. “No use crying over spilt milk.”

“The great thing is what to do next,” said Tuppence.

“You might advertise for the nurse,” said Sir James. “I do not hope for much. Otherwise there is nothing to be done.”

“Nothing?” said Tuppence blankly. “And—Tommy?”

“We must hope for the best. Oh yes, we must go on hoping.”

Over her bent head his eyes met Julius’s; he shook his head, almost imperceptibly. Julius understood: the lawyer considered the case hopeless. Sir James took Tuppence’s hand.

“You must let me know if anything further comes to light. Letters will always be forwarded.”

“You are going away?” said Tuppence dully.

“To Scotland. My dear young lady, I can do nothing more. Our clues have ended in thin air. If anything should arise, I shall be glad to advise you.”

Desolation swept her. “Thank you for trying. Good-bye.”

Sir James’s keen eyes softened. “Don’t be too disconsolate, Miss Tuppence,” he said low. “Remember, holiday-time isn’t always all playtime. One sometimes manages to put in some work as well.”

She looked up sharply. He shook his head with a smile. “No, I shan’t say any more. Great mistake to say too much. Remember that. Never tell all you know—not even to the person you know best. Understand? Good-bye.”

He strode away. Tuppence stared after him. Once before he had thrown a hint in this careless fashion. Was this a hint now? Had he not abandoned the case after all? Would he be working secretly while——

“Get right in,” said Julius. They started. “You’re looking thoughtful. Did the old guy say anything more?”

Tuppence opened her mouth, then shut it again. “Never tell all you know.” Another memory flashed: Julius at the safe; her own question; his pause before “Nothing.” Had he, too, held back? If he could make a reservation, so could she.

“Nothing particular,” she said.

“Shall we go for a spin in the Park?”

“If you like.”

As they drove, Julius spoke, discouraged: “Say, Miss Tuppence, do you think I’m ever going to find Jane?”

“Sir James hadn’t got any hope at all—that’s my take. He wouldn’t quit if there was a chance, would he?”

Tuppence was uncomfortable, but clung to her small reticence. “He suggested advertising for the nurse,” she said.

“Yes—with a forlorn-hope flavour. I’m about fed up. I’ve half a mind to go back to the States right away.”

“Oh no!” cried Tuppence. “We’ve got to find Tommy.”

“I sure forgot Beresford,” said Julius contritely. “That’s so. We must find him. But after—well, I’ve been day-dreaming since I started on this trip, and these dreams are poor business. Say, Miss Tuppence, there’s something I’d like to ask you.”

“Yes?”

“You and Beresford—what about it?”

“Tommy and I are friends—nothing more,” said Tuppence with warmth.

“I guess every pair of lovers has said that sometime or another,” observed Julius.

“Nonsense!” snapped Tuppence. “Do I look the sort of girl that’s always falling in love with every man she meets?”

“You do not. You look the sort of girl that’s mighty often getting fallen in love with!”

“What about marriage? Got any views?” he asked bluntly.

“I intend to marry—if I can find some one rich enough to make it worth my while. That’s frank, isn’t it?” She threw it like a challenge.

“I never despise business instinct,” said Julius. “What about me?”

“You?”

“Sure.”

“I couldn’t.”

“Why not?”

“It would seem so unfair.”

“I don’t see anything unfair. I admire you immensely. You’re so darned plucky. I’d love to give you a real good time. Say the word and we’ll run round to a jeweller and fix the ring business.”

“I can’t,” gasped Tuppence.

“Because of Beresford?”

“No—no—no!”

“Well then?”

She shook her head. “Thank you very much—and all that—but I think I’d better say no.”

“I’d be obliged if you’d think it over till to-morrow.”

“It’s no use.”

“Still, we’ll leave it like that.”

“Very well,” said Tuppence meekly.

They drove in silence to the Ritz. In her room, Tuppence sat before the glass and stared herself down.

“Fool,” she murmured. “Little fool. Everything you want—and you bleat out ‘no’ like a sheep. It’s your one chance. Why don’t you snatch it? What more do you want?”

Her eyes fell on the small snapshot of Tommy in its shabby frame. Control went; she caught it to her lips and sobbed.

“Oh, Tommy, Tommy, I do love you so—and I may never see you again....”

After five minutes she sat up, blew her nose, pushed back her hair.

“That’s that,” she said sternly. “Facts. I’ve fallen in love—with an idiot of a boy who probably doesn’t care two straws.” She paused. “Anyway, I don’t know that he does. He’d never have dared to say so. I’ve always jumped on sentiment—and here I am more sentimental than anybody. What idiots girls are! I’ve always thought so. I suppose I shall sleep with his photograph under my pillow, and dream about him all night. It’s dreadful to feel you’ve been false to your principles.”

Tuppence shook her head sadly, as she reviewed her backsliding.

“I don’t know what to say to Julius,” she went on. “He’ll insist on a reason. I wonder if he did find anything in that safe——”

Her thoughts veered; she reviewed the night, Sir James’s enigmatic words. Suddenly she started; the colour ebbed; her eyes stared, pupils dilating.

“Impossible,” she whispered. “Impossible! I must be going mad to think such a thing....”

Monstrous—yet it explained everything.

After a moment she sat down and wrote a note, weighing each word, then nodded, satisfied, and slipped it into an envelope addressed to Julius. She took it to his sitting-room and left it on the table.

A small page-boy stood outside her door on her return.

“Telegram for you, miss.”

Tuppence tore it open carelessly—then gave a cry. The telegram was from Tommy!

FURTHER ADVENTURES OF TOMMY

Out of a darkness stabbed with throbs of fire, Tommy dragged himself back to life. His head blazed, the room was unfamiliar, and the memory of the sinister house in Soho slid into place. He lay artistically inert as a voice at his ear— the bearded, efficient German’s— said, “He is coming to.” Then, with a muttered “Damn!” he sat up. The German held a glass to his lips. “Drink.” The fierce draught cleared his brain.

He was on a couch in the meeting-room. On one side stood the German, on the other the villainous doorkeeper who had let him in. The rest grouped themselves a little apart. One face was missing: Number One was gone.

“Feel better?” asked the German.

“Yes, thanks,” said Tommy cheerfully.

“Ah, my young friend, it is lucky for you your skull is so thick. The good Conrad struck hard.” The doorkeeper grinned. Tommy twisted his head.

“So you’re Conrad, are you? It strikes me my thick skull was lucky for you too. When I look at you, it’s almost a pity I’ve enabled you to cheat the hangman.”

Conrad snarled. The German’s cold accents took up the tale.

“Have you anything to say before you are put to death as a spy?”

“Simply lots of things,” returned Tommy, unruffled.

“Do you deny that you were listening at that door?”

“I do not. Your conversation was so interesting that it overcame my scruples.”

“How did you get in?”

“Dear old Conrad here. Don’t blame a faithful watchdog; he let me in— with your own password.”

There was a stir. The German stilled it with a hand. “Dead men tell no tales,” he said evenly.

“Ah,” said Tommy, “but I’m not dead yet!”

“You soon will be, my young friend.”

Tommy’s heart raced, but his tone stayed pleasant. “I think not. I should have a great objection to dying.” He saw their uncertainty and pressed it. “Why didn’t you kill me at once before I regained consciousness? Because you didn’t know how much I knew— and where I obtained that knowledge. If you kill me now, you never will know.”

Boris burst out, arms waving: “You hell-hound of a spy! Kill him! Kill him!” The working man cut across him gloomily: “Comrades, we have been betrayed!” An ugly murmur arose.

“You will tell us who has betrayed us,” said the German. “That shall not save you. Boris knows pretty ways of making people speak!”

“Bah!” said Tommy scornfully, mastering the unpleasant lurch of his stomach. “You will neither torture me nor kill me.”

“And why not?” growled Boris.

“Because you’d kill the goose that lays the golden eggs.”

They wavered. The working man eyed Tommy closely. “He’s bluffing you, Boris,” he said; Tommy hated him for the words. The German swung back.

“What do you mean?”

“What do you think I mean?” parried Tommy. Then, quietly: “I propose a bargain. My life and liberty against——”

“Against what?”

The room held its breath.

“The papers that Danvers brought over from America in the _Lusitania_.”

The effect was electrical. Men sprang to their feet. The German leaned over Tommy, face purpling. “_Himmel!_ You have got them, then?”

Tommy shook his head. “No.”

“You know where they are?”

“Not in the least.”

“Then—” baffled anger choked him.

“I don’t know where they are— but I believe I can find them. I have a theory— and facts known to no one but myself. What do you lose? If I produce the papers, you give me my life and liberty. Is it a bargain?”

“And if we refuse?” said the German quietly.

Tommy lay back. “The 29th,” he said thoughtfully, “is less than a fortnight ahead——”

There was a pause. Then the German signalled. “Take him into the other room.”

Five minutes in the dingy chamber next door, enlivened by flippant remarks that drove Conrad to the verge of homicidal mania— then: “The prisoner is at the bar, gentlemen,” said Tommy as he was marched back.

“We accept,” said the German harshly, “on terms. The papers must be delivered to us before you go free.”

“Idiot! How can I hunt for them tied by the leg here?”

“You will remain. One of our number will carry out your instructions. If the operations are complicated, he returns for further orders.”

“You’re tying my hands,” grumbled Tommy. “But— be curt if you must. So be it. One thing is essential: I must see the girl.”

“What girl?”

“Jane Finn, of course.”

The other looked at him curiously. “Do you not know that she can tell you nothing?”

“I shall not ask her to tell me anything. I want to watch her face when I ask her one question.”

“She will not be able to answer.”

“That does not matter. I shall have seen her face.”

The German laughed shortly, still studying him. “I wonder whether, after all, you know as much as we think?” he said softly.

“There may be things you know that I do not,” said Tommy. “Equally, I’ve something up my sleeve that you don’t know about. Danvers was a damned clever fellow——” He broke off. The German’s face lightened.

“Danvers. I see——” He waved to Conrad. “Take him away. Upstairs— you know.”

“Wait. What about the girl?”

“That may perhaps be arranged.”

“It must be.”

“We will see. Only one person can decide that.”

“Who?”

“Mr. Brown——”

“Shall I see him?”

“Perhaps.”

“Come,” said Conrad harshly.

Upstairs, Conrad thrust him into a small room, lit a hissing gas jet, and locked the door. There was no window. The air was peculiarly stale. Four crooked pictures from Faust— Marguerite with her jewels, the church scene, Siebel and his flowers, Faust and Mephistopheles— hung on the foul walls. In the sealed, close-fitting chamber, the sinister power of the arch-criminal seemed more real. Shout as he would, no one could hear. The place was a living tomb....

Tommy sat on the bed, head aching, stomach empty, silence pressing upon him. “Anyway,” he told himself, “I shall see the chief— the mysterious Mr. Brown— and, with a bit of luck in bluffing, the mysterious Jane Finn also. After that——”

After that, he was forced to admit, the prospect looked dreary.

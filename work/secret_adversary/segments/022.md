They stared at each other stupidly. Somehow, inexplicably, Mr. Brown had forestalled them.

“It accounts for the stitches being new,” said Tommy dully. “We might have guessed....”

“Never mind the darned stitches,” cried Julius. “How did he get ahead of us? We hustled all we knew. It’s downright impossible. And how did he know? Was there a dictaphone in Jane’s room? Then one of the nurses was a crook and listened at the door. How’s that?”

“No one could have known beforehand that she was going to be in that house—much less that room,” objected Tommy. “He may have found out months ago and removed the papers then—No, that won’t wash! They’d have been published at once.”

“Sure thing they would! No, some one’s got ahead of us to-day by an hour or so. But how they did it gets my goat.”

“I wish that chap Peel Edgerton had been with us,” said Tommy.

“Why? The mischief was done when we came.”

Tommy could not explain the illogical sense that Sir James’s presence would somehow have averted the catastrophe. He pulled himself together. “It’s no good arguing. The game’s up. There’s only one thing for me to do: get back to London and warn Mr. Carter. It’s only a matter of hours now before the blow falls.”

He took the midnight mail. Julius elected to stay the night at Holyhead. Half an hour after arrival, Tommy stood before his chief.

“I’ve come to report, sir. I’ve failed—failed badly.”

“You mean that the treaty——?”

“Is in the hands of Mr. Brown, sir.”

“Ah!” said Mr. Carter quietly. His face did not change, but Tommy caught a flicker of despair in his eyes. “Well, we mustn’t sag at the knees, I suppose. I’m glad to know definitely. We must do what we can. Don’t take it to heart, lad,” he added kindly.

“You did your best. You were up against one of the biggest brains of the century. And you came very near success. Remember that.”

Through Tommy’s mind flashed the assurance: “It’s hopeless, and he knows it’s hopeless!”

“I blame myself,” said Mr. Carter. “I have been blaming myself ever since I heard this other news.”

A new fear gripped Tommy. “Is there—something more, sir?”

“I’m afraid so.” He handed him a typed sheet.

“Tuppence——?” faltered Tommy.

“Read for yourself.”

The typed words danced: the description of a green toque, a coat with a handkerchief in the pocket marked P.L.C. Mr. Carter answered his look: “Washed up on the Yorkshire coast—near Ebury. I’m afraid—it looks very much like foul play.”

“My God!” gasped Tommy. “Tuppence! Those devils—I’ll never rest till I’ve got even with them! I’ll hunt them down! I’ll——”

“I know what you feel like, my poor boy,” said Mr. Carter gently. “But it’s no good. You’ll waste your strength uselessly. It may sound harsh, but my advice is: Cut your losses. Time’s merciful. You’ll forget.”

“Forget Tuppence? Never!”

“So you think now. Well, it won’t bear thinking of—that brave little girl! I’m confoundedly sorry.”

“I’m taking up your time, sir,” said Tommy, making an effort. “There’s no need for you to blame yourself. I dare say we were a couple of young fools. You warned us all right. But I wish to God I’d been the one to get it in the neck. Good-bye, sir.”

Back at the Ritz, he packed mechanically, his thoughts far away. He was still bewildered by the introduction of tragedy into his cheerful commonplace existence. What fun they had had, he and Tuppence! And now—oh, it couldn’t be true! Tuppence—dead! Little Tuppence, brimming over with life! It was a horrible dream.

They brought him a note: a few kind words of sympathy from Peel Edgerton, who had read the headline (EX-V.A.D. FEARED DROWNED). The letter ended with the offer of a post on a ranch in the Argentine.

“Kind old beggar,” muttered Tommy, flinging it aside.

The door burst open; Julius, with a newspaper.

“Say, what’s all this? They seem to have got some fool idea about Tuppence.”

“It’s true,” said Tommy quietly.

“You mean they’ve done her in?”

Tommy nodded. “I suppose when they got the treaty she wasn’t any good to them any longer, and they were afraid to let her go.”

“Well, I’m darned!” said Julius brokenly. “Little Tuppence. She sure was the pluckiest little girl——”

Something cracked in Tommy’s brain. He sprang up. “Oh, get out! You don’t really care, damn you! You asked her to marry you in your rotten cold-blooded way, but I loved her. I’d have given the soul out of my body to save her. I’d have stood by and let her marry you, because you could have given her the sort of time she ought to have had, and I was only a poor devil without a penny. But it wouldn’t have been because I didn’t care!”

“See here,” began Julius temperately.

“Oh, go to the devil! Don’t come here talking about ‘little Tuppence.’ Go and look after your cousin. Tuppence is my girl! I’ve always loved her—from the time we played together as kids. I shall never forget when I was in hospital, and she came in in that ridiculous cap and apron! It was like a miracle to see the girl I loved turn up in a nurse’s kit——”

“A nurse’s kit!” Julius swung round. “Gee whiz! I must be going to Colney Hatch! I could swear I’ve seen Jane in a nurse’s cap too. And that’s plumb impossible! No—by gum, I’ve got it! It was her I saw talking to Whittington at that nursing home in Bournemouth. She wasn’t a patient there! She was a nurse!”

“I dare say,” said Tommy angrily. “She’s probably been in with them from the start. I shouldn’t wonder if she stole those papers from Danvers to begin with.”

“I’m darned if she did!” shouted Julius. “She’s my cousin, and as patriotic a girl as ever stepped.”

“I don’t care a damn what she is, but get out of here!”

They were on the point of blows, when Julius’s anger dropped away.

“All right, son,” he said quietly, “I’m going. I don’t blame you. It’s mighty lucky you did say it. I’ve been the most almighty blithering darned idiot that it’s possible to imagine. Calm down—I’m going right away now—going to the London and North Western Railway depot, if you want to know.”

“I don’t care a damn where you’re going,” growled Tommy.

As the door closed, he turned back to his suit-case. “That’s the lot,” he muttered, and rang the bell.

“Take my luggage down.”

“Yes, sir. Going away, sir?”

“I’m going to the devil,” said Tommy, regardless of the menial’s feelings.

That functionary, however, merely replied respectfully: “Yes, sir. Shall I call a taxi?”

Tommy nodded. Where was he going? He hadn’t the faintest idea—only a fixed determination to get even with Mr. Brown. He re-read Sir James’s letter and shook his head. Tuppence must be avenged. Still, it was kind.

“Better answer it,” he decided. He went to the writing-table. There were envelopes but no paper. He rang. No one came. Fuming, he remembered there was paper in Julius’s sitting-room. The American had announced his immediate departure; there was no fear of meeting him.

The room was deserted. Tommy crossed to the writing-table and opened the middle drawer. A photograph, carelessly thrust in face upwards, caught his eye. He stood rooted; then took it out, shut the drawer, and sat down, staring.

What on earth was a photograph of the French girl Annette doing in Julius Hersheimmer’s writing-table?

## Chapter 1

The moment was not quite so triumphant as it ought to have been. Tommy's pockets were limited, and only after the lady recollected a plebeian twopence was the fare managed; the driver moved off with one last hoarse demand as to what the gentleman thought he was giving him.

"I think you've given him too much, Tommy," said Tuppence innocently. "I fancy he wants to give some of it back."

"What the—dickens, did you want to take a taxi for?"

"I was afraid I might be late. And really and truly, I haven't got anything smaller than a five-pound note."

"You did that part of it very well, old bean, but the fellow wasn't taken in—not for a moment!"

"That's the curious part about speaking the truth. No one believes it. Now let's go to lunch. How about the *Savoy*?"

"Is this a new brand of humour? Or is your brain really unhinged?"

"I have come into money, and the shock has been too much for me!" Tuppence wrenched open her bag. "Look here, and here, and here!"

"Great Jehosaphat! My dear girl, don't wave Fishers aloft like that!"

"They're not Fishers. They're five times better than Fishers, and this one's ten times better!"

Over lunch at the *Piccadilly*, surrounded by the many *hors d'œuvre* of Tuppence's dreams, Miss Cowley told him the whole story.

"And the curious part of it is," she ended, "I really did invent the name of Jane Finn!"

"You didn't invent it. *I* told it to you. I said yesterday I'd overheard two people talking about a female called Jane Finn. That's what brought the name into your mind so pat."

"How extraordinary—Tommy, what were they like, the two men you passed?"

"One was a big fat sort of chap. Clean shaven, and dark."

"That's him!" cried Tuppence, in an ungrammatical squeal. "That's Whittington!"

But Tommy had become serious. "Look here, old girl, what is this going to lead to? You can't bluff him forever. He'll want to know how much *you* know, and where you got your information from. What are you going to do?"

Tuppence frowned severely. "I've got a plan. Obviously what we've got to do is find out more about it all. We can only find out through Whittington—we must sleuth him. I can't, because he knows me. But he only saw you for a minute or two in Lyons'. After all, one young man is much like another."

"I repudiate that remark utterly."

"I'll go alone to-morrow and put him off again. You'll hang about outside. When he comes out of the building I'll drop a handkerchief, and off you go."

"Anyway, it will be rather a lark."

The day passed pleasantly; the evening even more so. Two of the five-pound notes were now irretrievably dead.

They met by arrangement the following morning. Tommy remained on the opposite side of the road while Tuppence plunged into the building. Just as he came abreast of it again, she darted across.

"The place is shut. I can't make anyone hear."

They tried again. On the third floor landing a young clerk emerged.

"Were you wanting the Esthonia Glassware? It's closed down. Since yesterday afternoon. Company being wound up. The office is to let."

"I suppose you don't know Mr. Whittington's address?"

"Afraid I don't. They left rather suddenly."

In the street they gazed at one another blankly.

"That's torn it," said Tommy at length.

"And I never suspected it!" wailed Tuppence.

"Cheer up. It can't be helped."

"Can't it, though!" Tuppence's little chin shot out defiantly. "If they are scared enough to run away like this, it shows there must be a lot in this Jane Finn business! We'll get to the bottom of it. We'll be sleuths in earnest!"

"But there's no one left to sleuth."

"That's why we'll have to start all over again. Lend me that bit of pencil."

She handed him back a slip of paper. Tommy read the words on it aloud:

"WANTED, any information respecting Jane Finn. Apply Y. A."

## Chapter 2

The day of disillusionment had been a Wednesday. On Friday, Tommy met Tuppence at the National Gallery with two answers to the advertisement.

"Tommy, you devil! Give them to me."

The first was from "A. Carter," 27 Carshalton Gardens, requesting a call at eleven the next morning; the second, from the *Ritz*, signed "Julius P. Hersheimmer," asked them round at lunch-time.

"A hundred pounds instead of fifty!" said Tommy. "Do I smell a Boche? Or only an American millionaire of unfortunate ancestry? At all events we'll call at lunch-time."

"Now for Carter."

Carshalton Terrace proved an unimpeachable row of ladylike-looking houses. A neat maid showed them into a small study, where presently a tall man with a lean hawklike face and a tired manner entered.

"Mr. Y. A.?" he said, and smiled. His smile was distinctly attractive. "Do sit down, both of you."

He took a chair opposite Tuppence. "What do *you* know about Jane Finn?"

"I don't see that that's got anything to do with it."

"No? But it has, you know." He leaned forward, his weary voice held a hint of persuasiveness. "Suppose you tell me…."

To Tuppence's surprise, Tommy spoke first, with an unusual note of deference. "I dare say the little we know won't be any good to you, sir. But you're welcome to it."

"Tommy!"

Mr. Carter's eyes asked a question. Tommy nodded. "I recognized you at once, sir. Saw you in France when I was with the Intelligence."

"No names, please. I'm known as Mr. Carter here. It's my cousin's house, by the way—she's willing to lend it when it's a case of working on strictly unofficial lines. Well now—who's going to tell me the story?"

"Fire ahead, Tuppence. It's your yarn."

Obediently Tuppence told the whole tale, from the forming of the Young Adventurers, Ltd. downwards. Mr. Carter listened in silence. When she had finished he nodded gravely.

"Not much. But suggestive. Quite suggestive. I don't know—you might succeed where others have failed. I believe in luck. How would you like to work for me? All quite unofficial. Expenses paid, and a moderate screw."

"What should we have to do?" Tuppence breathed.

"Just go on with what you're doing now. *Find Jane Finn.*"

"Yes, but—who *is* Jane Finn?"

He leaned back, brought the tips of his fingers together, and began in a low monotone:

"In the early days of 1915 a draft secret treaty was drawn up in America—then a neutral country—and dispatched to England by a special messenger, a young fellow called Danvers. He sailed on the *Lusitania*, carrying the papers in an oilskin packet next his skin. The *Lusitania* was torpedoed. Danvers's body was washed ashore, identified beyond doubt. But the oilskin packet was missing.

"During the launching of the boats, Danvers was seen speaking to a young American girl. No one actually saw him pass anything to her, but he might have done so. It seems likely he entrusted the papers to her, believing that she, as a woman, had a greater chance of bringing them safely to shore. Her name was Jane Finn, and it duly appeared among the list of survivors—but the girl herself seemed to have vanished completely. She was an orphan, a pupil teacher in a small school out West, going to join the staff of a Paris hospital. We tracked her across Ireland, but nothing could be heard of her after she set foot in England. No use was made of the draft treaty, and we concluded that Danvers had destroyed it. The whole affair was lost in oblivion."

"But why has it all cropped up again? The war's over."

"Because it seems the papers were *not* destroyed, and might be resurrected to-day with a new and deadly significance. Five years ago, that draft treaty was a weapon in our hands; to-day it is a weapon against us. If its terms were made public, it would mean disaster. It might possibly bring about another war—not with Germany this time. That document undoubtedly implicates a number of our statesmen whom we cannot afford to have discredited.

"You may have heard that there is Bolshevist influence at work behind the present Labour unrest? That is the truth. Bolshevist gold is pouring into this country for the specific purpose of procuring a Revolution. The Bolshevists are behind the Labour unrest—but a certain man is *behind the Bolshevists*. He is always spoken of by the unassuming title of 'Mr. Brown.' One thing is certain: he is the master criminal of this age."

"A naturalized German?" asked Tommy.

"On the contrary, I have every reason to believe he is an Englishman. We have no clue as to his real personality. Where we have come across his tracks, he has always played a secondary part—some nonentity, a servant or a clerk, in the background unnoticed. Afterwards we always find that the elusive Mr. Brown has escaped us once more."

"Oh!" Tuppence jumped. "I remember—in Mr. Whittington's office, the clerk—he called him Brown. You don't think—"

Carter nodded thoughtfully. "Very likely. A curious point is that the name is usually mentioned. An idiosyncrasy of genius. Did you notice a telephone in the outer office?"

"No, I don't think I did."

"Exactly. That 'message' was Mr. Brown's way of giving an order to his subordinate. He overheard the whole conversation. Was it after that that Whittington handed you the money, and told you to come the following day?"

Tuppence nodded.

"Undoubtedly the hand of Mr. Brown! Well, you see what you are pitting yourselves against? Possibly the finest criminal brain of the age. I don't quite like it. You're such young things, both of you."

"It won't," Tuppence assured him positively.

"I'll look after her, sir," said Tommy.

"And *I'll* look after *you*," retorted Tuppence.

"Well, then, look after each other. Now—there's something mysterious about this draft treaty we haven't fathomed. The Revolutionary element as good as declare it's in their hands, yet they are clearly at fault about many of its provisions. The position is much as though they had got hold of an incriminating document, but couldn't read it because it was in cipher—but we know the draft treaty wasn't in cipher. Of course, Jane Finn may be dead for all we know—but I don't think so. The curious thing is that *they're trying to get information about the girl from us*."

"What?"

"They know we're looking for Jane Finn. They'll produce a Jane Finn of their own—say, at a *pensionnat* in Paris. No one knows in the least what she looks like, so that's all right. She's primed with a trumped-up tale, and her real business is to get information out of us."

"Then you think—it *was* as Jane Finn that they wanted me to go to Paris?"

Mr. Carter smiled more wearily than ever. "I believe in coincidences, you know."

## Chapter 3

"Well," said Tuppence, recovering herself, "it really seems as though it were meant to be."

"Have you any tips before we clear out, sir?" Tommy asked.

"I think not. My experts, working in stereotyped ways, have failed. You will bring imagination and an open mind. I'm just warning you that you haven't much time. I have information that the big *coup* was planned for early in the new year. The less time they have to mature their plans the better."

Tuppence rose. "What exactly can we count upon you for, Mr. Carter?"

"Funds within reason, detailed information on any point, and *no official recognition*. If you get yourselves into trouble with the police, I can't officially help you out. You're on your own."

"I quite understand. Now—about money—"

"Three hundred a year. And an equal sum for Mr. Beresford, of course."

Tuppence beamed. "How lovely. You are kind. I do love money! I'll keep beautiful accounts of our expenses—debit and credit, and the balance on the right side."

He shook hands with them, and in another minute they were descending the steps with their heads in a whirl.

"Tommy! Tell me at once, who is 'Mr. Carter'?"

Tommy murmured a name in her ear.

"Oh!"

"And I can tell you, old bean, he's IT!"

"I like him. He looks so awfully tired and bored, and yet you feel that underneath he's just like steel, all keen and flashing. Pinch me, Tommy, do pinch me. I can't believe it's real!"

"Ow! That's enough!"

"Julius P. Hersheimmer!" they exclaimed together.

"We never told Mr. Carter about hearing from him."

"Come on, we'd better take a taxi."

On inquiring for Mr. Hersheimmer at the *Ritz*, they were taken up to his suite. He was younger than they had pictured—perhaps thirty-five, of middle height, squarely built to match his jaw, his face pugnacious but pleasant. No one could have mistaken him for anything but an American.

"Get my note? Sit down and tell me right away all you know about my cousin."

"Your cousin?"

"Sure thing. Jane Finn. My father and her mother were brother and sister."

"Then you know where she is?"

"No!" Hersheimmer brought down his fist on the table. "I'm darned if I do. Don't you?"

"We advertised to receive information, not to give it," said Tuppence severely.

But Mr. Hersheimmer seemed to grow suddenly suspicious. "See here. This isn't Sicily! No demanding ransom or threatening to crop her ears if I refuse. So quit the funny business, or I'll just sing out for that beautiful big British policeman I see out there in Piccadilly."

Tommy hastened to explain. "We haven't kidnapped your cousin. On the contrary, we're trying to find her. We're employed to do so." He gave a guarded version of the disappearance, alluding to Tuppence and himself as "private inquiry agents" commissioned in connection with "some political show."

"I guess that's all right. I was just a mite hasty. Just trot out your questions."

"When did you last see your cousin?"

"Never seen her. My father and her mother were brother and sister, but they didn't always get on together. When my aunt made up her mind to marry Amos Finn, a poor school teacher out West, my father was just mad! Said if he made his pile, she'd never see a cent of it. Aunt Jane went out West and we never heard from her again.

"The old man *did* pile it up. He went into oil, and steel, and railroads—made Wall Street sit up. Then he died last fall, and I got the dollars. My conscience got busy. I hired a man to hunt her down. Result, she was dead, and Amos Finn was dead, but they'd left a daughter—Jane—who'd been torpedoed in the *Lusitania* on her way to Paris. She was saved all right, but they didn't seem able to hear of her over this side. So I came along over to speed things up. I phoned Scotland Yard and the Admiralty first thing. Scotland Yard were very civil—even sent a man round this morning to get her photograph. I'm off to Paris to-morrow."

Oysters had just given place to Sole Colbert when a card was brought to Hersheimmer.

"Scotland Yard again. Another man. I hope they haven't lost that photograph. The Western photographer's place was burned down and his negatives destroyed—this is the only copy in existence."

An unformulated dread swept over Tuppence.

"You—you don't know the name of the man who came this morning?"

"Yes, I do. It was on his card. Inspector Brown. Quiet, unassuming sort of chap."

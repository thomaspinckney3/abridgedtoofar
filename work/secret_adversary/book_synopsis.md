On May 7, 1915, as the Lusitania sinks, an American girl named Jane Finn is entrusted with vitally important Allied papers by a desperate stranger who tells her to watch the Times personals for “Shipmate.” Years later, demobbed friends Thomas “Tommy” Beresford and Prudence “Tuppence” Cowley reunite in postwar London, broke but undaunted, and cheekily set up as the “Young Adventurers, Ltd.” Their whimsical newspaper ad—“Two young adventurers for hire”—draws shadowy interest: Tuppence is lured by the slippery Edward Whittington of the Esthonia Glassware Co., who offers her money to pose as a ward in a Paris pensionnat and bristles when she blurts the name “Jane Finn,” then vanishes overnight; a bona fide summons arrives from Mr. Carter of the secret service, who explains Jane’s role in a lost draft treaty carried on the Lusitania that could now topple a fragile Britain; and an eager American, Julius P. Hersheimmer, declares Jane his missing cousin and throws in his fortune to help the hunt. Looming over all is “Mr. Brown,” a faceless mastermind Carter calls the most dangerous criminal in England, orchestrating strikes and Bolshevist mischief while hiding in plain sight.

Pretending respectability, Tuppence insinuates herself into the service of the glamorous and menacing Mrs. Rita Vandemeyer, overhearing furtive conferences with a Russian named Boris and learning the household whispers about “Mr. Brown.” Tuppence parries an attempted drugging, flips the tables with a borrowed revolver, and extracts from Rita a promise to betray the chief, but the woman is found dead of chloral poisoning before she can speak—officially an accident, unofficially a warning. Meanwhile Tommy tails Whittington to a Soho warren, is knocked unconscious, and awakens a prisoner behind a door that will not budge, where he overhears a conclave chaired by a precise, bearded “Number One” and attended by Sinn Feiners, Bolshevists, and trade union men discussing a general strike and the vanished treaty; aided by a frightened French maid called “Annette,” he frees himself, bluffs his way out, and caches the address for later. Julius loses the only photo of Jane to a bogus “Inspector Brown,” and Sir James Peel Edgerton, a famed K.C. with an enigmatic smile, flits in and out dispensing help, hints, and lawyerly caution.

The trail bends toward a Bournemouth nursing home and then a shuttered country house, Astley Priors at Gatehouse, Kent, where Tuppence, summoned by a forged telegram apparently from Tommy, is abducted and meets “Annette,” a lovely amnesiac who cries out the single name “Marguerite.” Julius, abducting a Bolshevik power-broker named Kramenin at gunpoint, storms the house and flees with both girls under fire; Tommy, who has hung on to the rear of their car from London, orders the women to ditch pursuers by train and head for Sir James, as he and Julius double back to set an unseen trap. Carter, convinced that the strike’s success or failure hinges on exposing Mr. Brown, arrays police around the Soho house, and Tommy lays out the slender clue that cracked his own suspicions—the fake note to Tuppence signed “Twopence,” a misspelling only someone who had never seen her handwriting would make. Between the unnervingly helpful Sir James and the too-slick Julius, Tommy embraces uncertainty, tells Carter nothing about where he believes the papers are hidden, and gambles everything on catching the adversary red-handed.

With the house ringed by plainclothesmen, Tuppence, Jane, and Sir James return to the grim Soho room where Tommy had been caged; Jane plucks her “Marguerite” from the wall, slits the backing, and draws out the long-lost draft treaty—then Sir James smiles, straightens, and reveals himself as Mr. Brown. With a cool recital of his “two lives”—public advocate and supreme criminal—and his plan to shoot the women, blame a phantom intruder, and pocket the treaty, he raises a gun; but Julius and Tommy burst from behind the curtain, wrench the weapon away, and, as policemen pound up the stairs, Mr. Brown bites a poison capsule in his signet ring and dies. In the aftermath Carter commits the incendiary document to ash, Kramenin flees for Russia, and the Labour crisis—deprived of the one spark that would have set it off—dissolves into negotiation and face-saving compromise. The shadow-empire collapses without its architect, the Soho den yields incriminating records, and the faceless terror is unmasked as a man who had hidden in the one place no one thought to look: the law’s own inner circle.

In quieter rooms, long-buried identities are restored: “Annette” is indeed Jane Finn, who had survived the sinking, hidden the papers in the picture’s back, then feigned amnesia so convincingly she nearly lost herself in the part as her captors shuttled her through sanatoria and safe houses. Julius confesses his own red herring—losing, then secretly recovering, the photograph of Jane—and at last won by her courage, blurts out a proposal she unexpectedly accepts, her American loneliness soothed by a kindred audacity. Tuppence, whose unapologetic nerve and domestic disguises outmaneuvered professionals, learns that Sir James’s chilling brilliance and benevolent mask very nearly destroyed her, while Tommy’s plodding logic and stubborn decency never stopped worrying the facts until the single wrong “Twopence” gave him his man. Even the old guard bends: Sir William Beresford welcomes his errant nephew home, Mr. Carter raises a private toast to amateur audacity, and the Young Adventurers receive both official gratitude and cheques that will launch their next chapter.

At a celebratory supper at the Savoy, Carter reads from the dead K.C.’s private journal, a chilling self-portrait of genius curdling into megalomania, and the company drinks to the “Joint Venture” that saved a country. The romantic knots tie themselves briskly: Julius and Jane plan a hasty wedding and a swift detour through Regent’s Park, while in a different taxi Tommy and Tuppence move from exquisite awkwardness to a breathless mutual confession that marriage, like espionage, is a sport for equals. London’s postwar fog lifts just enough to reveal a thematic truth: in a world of masks, it is the frank partnership of two modern souls—witty, stubborn, and brave—that defeats the puppeteer who thought himself untouchable. And as the ash of a treaty flutters up a chimney in Soho, the two amateurs who began with a joke advertisement and empty pockets exchange the only pledge that ever really mattered to them: to hunt, to laugh, and to dare together.

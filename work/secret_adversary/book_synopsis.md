On the eve of catastrophe, as the Lusitania goes down in 1915, a young American named Jane Finn accepts a packet of vital papers from a desperate government courier and vanishes into the chaos. Years later, in a London starved of opportunity after the war, childhood friends Tommy Beresford and Prudence “Tuppence” Cowley form “The Young Adventurers, Ltd.” to make their luck by offering themselves for any enterprise. Their first nibble comes from a sleek, shifty businessman named Whittington, who tries to buy Tuppence’s silence about a mysterious name—Jane Finn—before abruptly disappearing and shuttering his office overnight. Recognizing a thread worth tugging, the pair advertise for information on Jane and are swept into a tangle of spies, aliases, and the whispered name of a master criminal known only as “Mr. Brown.”

Drawn into the hunt by the kindly, watchful government fixer Mr. Carter and the brash American millionaire Julius P. Hersheimmer—who claims Jane as his missing cousin—Tommy and Tuppence divide forces. Tuppence infiltrates the glittering but poisonous orbit of Mrs. Rita Vandemeyer, a society beauty with a velvet smile and iron will, and discovers that Jane survived but lost her memory. Tommy trails a foreign agent into a dilapidated Soho house and overhears a clandestine council plotting revolution around a phantom leader who may be anywhere and anyone. He’s imprisoned there, escapes by audacity and luck, and learns how far the enemy’s net reaches: even a false “Inspector Brown” has snatched away Jane’s only photograph.

As the conspirators move to break a nationwide strike with scandal, clues fork and mislead. A decoy “Jane Finn” is displayed in Manchester to draw out seekers, a Russian agitator named Kramenin lurks behind the unrest, and both Whittington and the alluring Mrs. Vandemeyer are unmasked as cogs in a ruthless machine. Tuppence, working as Vandemeyer’s maid, narrowly survives when the lady abruptly “dies” of an overdose after hinting she knows Mr. Brown’s true face. The team’s lone fixed star is a hidden draft treaty, the very document Jane once carried—if it surfaces in the wrong hands, it will discredit ministers, topple the government, and ignite a workers’ coup on the 29th.

Their quest keeps circling a shabby Soho room with crooked pictures, a shabby safe, and a peep-hole in the wall, the lair where Tommy had listened and nearly died. Meanwhile Julius mounts American-style “hustling” and almost catches up to Jane when a nursing home swaps patients and names. Sir James Peel Edgerton, a famed K.C. with polished courtesy and a steel core, hovers in the background offering discreet advice and warning the pair that cleverness must be matched to caution. Tuppence refuses to abandon her equal share of danger, and together the partners decide that only Jane’s memory, and the precise place she hid the papers, can save England.

The knot tightens: an urgent telegram lures Tuppence to an empty country house, and she is seized. Tommy smells a plant, doubles back, and sends Julius on a breathless chase that ends in a car rescue on a dark road; the “French maid” Annette in their care suddenly speaks perfect American and reveals herself as Jane Finn at last, long-amnesic and now clear-eyed. She remembers a gorse-fringed path and a rock like a begging dog on the Holyhead cliffs, and the pages she slid behind brown paper in a picture’s back. But when Tommy and Julius race to the spot and heat the pages to reveal hidden ink, the inscription reading “With the compliments of Mr. Brown” mocks their triumph: the adversary has arrived first.

With Labour Day looming, they stake everything on the Soho house. Carter’s men watch every exit, and Julius shepherds Jane and Tuppence inside to the curtained room where Tommy once listened and suffered. Jane tears open the picture and at last draws out the real, long-hidden treaty, only for the air to shiver with the presence they’ve half-feared and half-doubted all along. Sir James Peel Edgerton steps from patron to predator, announces himself as Mr. Brown, and outlines how his public virtue masks private despotism; in minutes, he will shoot two and wound one to script a perfect martyrdom and escape with the prize.

But the Young Adventurers have learned from hard knocks. Tommy and Julius, concealed, wrench the pistol from him and behold the unimaginable: the nation’s silver-tongued defender of law is the very spider at the web’s center. Cornered, Sir James bites his poison ring with a cold salute—“_Ave, Cæsar! te morituri salutant_”—and falls dead as the room fills with the bitter scent of almonds. From his pocket Carter recovers the treaty and consigns it to ashes, and from his little brown notebook spills the confession of a man who chose absolute power outside the law and wore borrowed personalities like gloves.

With the chief unmasked and his diary naming names, Kramenin flees, the Soho ring collapses, and the government averts catastrophe by calling the labour leaders to frank terms. The timid archdeacon hears his daughter toasted for nerve, and Carter raises a glass to the “Joint Venture” that beat a mastermind at his own game. Julius, who proved himself plucky as well as rich, drops the decoy photograph ruse and wins the very real Jane Finn, who regains not just her memory but the promise of a life unshadowed by plots. The American’s audacious road rescue and honest heart undo the long years of fear.

Tommy and Tuppence, who faked telegrams, outfoxed emissaries, and refused to be relegated to anyone’s role but their own, find that partnership carries a different kind of proposal. Flushed with success and a government cheque, they abandon talk of Argentine ranches and imagine, with as much fervour as any coup d’état, a bright flat with futurist curtains and a polished table. The themes of deception and identity—how a man can be both trusted counsel and secret tyrant, how a girl can forget her name and yet keep faith—unspool to the final reassurance that wit, courage, and loyalty can topple the tallest mask. In the end, the spark that might have lit England like a fuse is snuffed, the treaty is ash, and the Young Adventurers exchange coded ads and car chases for a sport Tuppence considers just as exciting: matrimony.

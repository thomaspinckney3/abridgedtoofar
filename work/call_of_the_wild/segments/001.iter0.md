Into the Primitive

“Old longings nomadic leap,
Chafing at custom’s chain;
Again from its brumal sleep
Wakens the ferine strain.”

Buck did not read the newspapers, or he would have known that trouble was brewing, not alone for himself, but for every tide-water dog, strong of muscle and with warm, long hair, from Puget Sound to San Diego. Men, groping in the Arctic darkness, had found a yellow metal; steamship and transportation companies were booming the find; thousands were rushing into the Northland. They wanted heavy dogs, with strong muscles by which to toil, and furry coats to protect them from the frost.

Buck lived at a big house in the sun-kissed Santa Clara Valley—Judge Miller’s place. Gravelled drives wound among lawns and poplars; behind were great stables and vine-clad cottages, orchards, berry patches, the artesian well and the big cement tank where the Judge’s boys plunged. Over this great demesne Buck ruled. Here he was born, and here he had lived four years. Other dogs there were—Toots, the Japanese pug, and Ysabel, the Mexican hairless, obscure house-dwellers; and a score of fox terriers who yelped fearful promises from behind housemaids’ brooms. But Buck was neither house-dog nor kennel-dog. The whole realm was his. He hunted with the Judge’s sons; escorted Mollie and Alice on twilight rambles; on wintry nights lay at the Judge’s feet before the roaring library fire; carried the grandsons on his back and guarded their adventures to the fountain and the berry patches. Among the terriers he stalked imperiously, and Toots and Ysabel he utterly ignored, for he was king,—king over all creeping, crawling, flying things of Judge Miller’s place, humans included.

His father, Elmo, a huge St. Bernard, had been the Judge’s inseparable companion; his mother, Shep, was a Scotch shepherd dog. Buck weighed one hundred and forty pounds, and dignity from good living and universal respect enabled him to carry himself in right royal fashion. He had lived the life of a sated aristocrat, a trifle egotistical; but he had saved himself by not becoming a mere pampered house-dog. Hunting and kindred outdoor delights had hardened his muscles; to him, as to the cold-tubbing races, the love of water was a tonic.

Such was Buck in the fall of 1897, when the Klondike strike dragged men from all the world into the frozen North. He did not know that Manuel, one of the gardener’s helpers, was an undesirable acquaintance. Manuel had one besetting sin. He loved to play Chinese lottery. He had one besetting weakness—faith in a system; and this made his damnation certain, for systems require money and a gardener’s helper’s wages do not lap over the needs of a wife and numerous progeny.

On the memorable night of Manuel’s treachery, the Judge was at a meeting of the Raisin Growers’ Association, and the boys were busy organizing an athletic club. No one saw Manuel and Buck go through the orchard to the little flag station known as College Park. A solitary man talked with Manuel, and money chinked between them.

“You might wrap up the goods before you deliver ’m,” the stranger said gruffly, and Manuel doubled a piece of stout rope around Buck’s neck under the collar.

“Twist it, an’ you’ll choke ’m plentee,” said Manuel.

Buck accepted the rope with quiet dignity. He had learned to trust men he knew. But when the ends of the rope were placed in the stranger’s hands, he growled menacingly—merely intimating his displeasure, in his pride believing that to intimate was to command. To his surprise the rope tightened, shutting off his breath. He sprang; the man met him, grappled him by the throat, deftly threw him, and the rope tightened mercilessly till his strength ebbed and his eyes glazed. He knew nothing when the train was flagged and he was flung into the baggage car.

He came to with his tongue hurting and the jolting of a conveyance; the hoarse shriek of a locomotive told him where he was. Into his eyes came the unbridled anger of a kidnapped king. He closed his jaws on a hand till his senses were choked out of him once more.

“Yep, has fits,” the man told the baggageman. “I’m takin’ ’m up for the boss to ’Frisco. A crack dog-doctor there thinks that he can cure ’m.”

On the San Francisco water front, in a shed back of a saloon, the man spoke most eloquently for himself. “All I get is fifty for it,” he grumbled, his hand wrapped in a bloody handkerchief, his trouser leg ripped. “How much did the other mug get?” demanded the saloon-keeper. “A hundred.” “That makes a hundred and fifty,” the saloon-keeper calculated; “and he’s worth it, or I’m a squarehead.” “If I don’t get the hydrophoby—” “It’ll be because you was born to hang,” laughed the saloon-keeper. They choked Buck repeatedly, filed the heavy brass collar from his neck, removed the rope, and flung him into a cagelike crate.

All that weary night he nursed wrath and wounded pride, oppressed by a vague sense of impending calamity. Each time the door rattled open he sprang up, expecting the Judge or the boys; each time it was the saloon-keeper’s bulging face by tallow-candle light, and the joyful bark in his throat twisted into a savage growl. In the morning four evil-looking men entered; they poked sticks at him and laughed; he lay sullenly and allowed the crate to be lifted. Thus he and his crate passed through many hands—express clerks, wagons, a ferry steamer, a railway depot—and were deposited in an express car. For two days and nights the car clattered behind shrieking locomotives. In anger he met the express messengers’ advances with growls, and they teased him, barking and crowing like detestable dogs. He neither ate nor drank. The lack of water fanned his wrath to fever-pitch; his parched and swollen throat and tongue inflamed him. Glad for one thing—the rope was off his neck—he resolved they would never get another around it. His eyes turned blood-shot; he was metamorphosed into a raging fiend; and the messengers breathed with relief when they bundled him off the train at Seattle.

Four men gingerly carried the crate into a high-walled back yard. A stout man in a red sweater came out and signed the book. Buck hurled himself against the bars. The man smiled grimly, brought a hatchet and a club.

“You ain’t going to take him out now?” the driver asked.

“Sure,” said the man, prying at the crate. From the top of the wall the others prepared to watch. Buck tore at the splintering wood, wherever the hatchet fell, he was there within, snarling to get out as the man in the red sweater was calmly intent on getting him out.

“Now, you red-eyed devil,” the man said, when the opening was sufficient. He dropped the hatchet and shifted the club.

Buck drew himself together for the spring—hair bristling, mouth foaming, mad glitter in his blood-shot eyes—and launched his one hundred and forty pounds. In mid air the club struck; he crashed to the ground. He had never been struck by a club and did not understand. He sprang again; again the shock came; a dozen times he charged, and as often the club smashed him down. Dazed, bleeding, his beautiful coat sprayed with bloody slaver, he staggered limply. The man advanced and deliberately dealt him a frightful blow on the nose; the exquisite agony roused him to a lionlike roar and another rush; the man, shifting the club, coolly caught him by the under jaw, wrenching downward and backward; Buck described a circle and half of another and crashed on head and chest. For the last time he rushed. The shrewd blow, long withheld, crumpled him utterly senseless.

“He’s no slouch at dog-breakin’, that’s wot I say,” one of the men cried. “Druther break cayuses any day,” came the driver’s reply as he climbed to his wagon.

Buck’s senses came back, but not his strength. The man in the red sweater read from a letter: “‘Answers to the name of Buck.’ Well, Buck, my boy,” he said genially, “we’ve had our little ruction, and the best thing we can do is to let it go at that. You’ve learned your place, and I know mine. Be a good dog and all ’ll go well and the goose hang high. Be a bad dog, and I’ll whale the stuffin’ outa you. Understand?” He patted the head he had so mercilessly pounded. Buck’s hair bristled, but he endured it. He drank eagerly when water was brought, and later bolted raw meat from the man’s hand.

He was beaten (he knew that); but he was not broken. He saw, once for all, that he stood no chance against a man with a club. That club was a revelation. It was his introduction to the reign of primitive law, and he met the introduction halfway. The facts of life took on a fiercer aspect; uncowed, he faced it with all the latent cunning of his nature aroused. As the days went by, other dogs came, in crates and at the ends of ropes, some docilely, some raging as he had come; one and all, they passed under the dominion of the man in the red sweater. Again and again the lesson was driven home: a man with a club was a lawgiver, a master to be obeyed, though not necessarily conciliated. He saw dogs that fawned and licked the hand; he saw one that would neither conciliate nor obey finally killed in the struggle for mastery.

Strangers came, talked excitedly and wheedlingly; money passed; dogs were led away and did not return. Buck wondered where they went, and was glad each time he was not selected. Yet his time came, in the form of a little weazened man who spat broken English and many strange exclamations.

“Sacredam!” he cried, eyes lighting upon Buck. “Dat one dam bully dog! Eh? How moch?”

“Three hundred, and a present at that,” promptly replied the man in the red sweater. “And seem’ it’s government money, you ain’t got no kick coming, eh, Perrault?”

Perrault grinned. Considering that the price of dogs had been boomed skyward, it was not unfair for so fine an animal. The Canadian Government would be no loser, nor would its despatches travel the slower. Perrault knew dogs, and when he looked at Buck he knew he was one in a thousand—“One in ten t’ousand,” he commented mentally.

Money passed; Curly, a good-natured Newfoundland, and Buck were led away. From the deck of the _Narwhal_ they watched Seattle recede—the last of the warm Southland. Below, Perrault turned them over to a black-faced giant called François, a French-Canadian half-breed, twice as swarthy as Perrault. Buck developed no affection for them, but honestly respected them—fair men, calm and impartial in justice, too wise in the way of dogs to be fooled by dogs.

In the ’tween-decks Buck and Curly joined two others: a big, snow-white fellow from Spitzbergen, friendly in a treacherous way, who smiled into one’s face while meditating an underhand trick—stealing from Buck’s food at the first meal. As Buck sprang to punish him, François’s lash sang through the air, reaching the culprit first; Buck recovered the bone. That was fair of François, he decided, and the half-breed rose in his estimation. The other dog made no advances nor received any. Gloomy and morose, he showed Curly he desired only to be left alone, and that there would be trouble if not. “Dave” he was called. He ate and slept, or yawned between times, and took interest in nothing, not even when the _Narwhal_ crossed Queen Charlotte Sound and rolled and pitched like a thing possessed. When Buck and Curly grew half wild with fear, he raised his head as though annoyed, favored them with an incurious glance, yawned, and slept again.

Day and night the ship throbbed to the tireless pulse of the propeller. The weather steadily grew colder. At last, one morning, the propeller was quiet and the _Narwhal_ pervaded with excitement. François leashed them and brought them on deck. At the first step upon the cold surface, Buck’s feet sank into a white mushy something like mud. He sprang back with a snort. More of this white stuff was falling through the air. He shook himself; more fell upon him. He sniffed it, then licked some up. It bit like fire, and the next instant was gone. He tried again, with the same result. The onlookers laughed uproariously, and he felt ashamed, he knew not why, for it was his first snow.

Buck’s first day on the Dyea beach was like a nightmare. Every hour was filled with shock and surprise. He had been suddenly jerked from the heart of civilization and flung into the heart of things primordial. Here was neither peace, nor rest, nor a moment’s safety. All was confusion and action, and every moment life and limb were in peril. These dogs and men were not town dogs and men. They were savages, all of them, who knew no law but the law of club and fang.

He had never seen dogs fight as these wolfish creatures fought, and his first experience taught him an unforgettable lesson. Curly was the victim. In her friendly way she made advances to a husky the size of a full-grown wolf. There was no warning, only a flash of a leap, a metallic clip of teeth, a leap out equally swift, and Curly’s face was ripped open from eye to jaw.

Thirty or forty huskies ran to the spot and formed an intent and silent circle. Curly rushed again; he struck, leaped aside, and, meeting her next rush with his chest, tumbled her. She never regained her feet. That was what the onlookers had waited for. They closed in, snarling and yelping, and she was buried, screaming, beneath the bristling mass.

So sudden was it that Buck was taken aback. He saw Spitz run out his scarlet tongue in the way he had of laughing; François, swinging an axe, sprang into the mess, and men with clubs beat the pack off. Two minutes from the time Curly went down, it was over. She lay limp and lifeless in the bloody, trampled snow, almost literally torn to pieces, the swart half-breed standing over her and cursing horribly. The scene often came back to Buck to trouble him in his sleep. So that was the way. No fair play. Once down, that was the end of you. Well, he would see to it that he never went down. Spitz ran out his tongue and laughed again, and from that moment Buck hated him with a bitter and deathless hatred.

Before he had recovered from the shock, François fastened upon him an arrangement of straps and buckles. It was a harness, and, as he had seen horses work, so he was set to work, hauling François on a sled and returning with firewood. His dignity was sorely hurt, but he was too wise to rebel. François was stern, and by virtue of his whip received instant obedience; Dave, an experienced wheeler, nipped Buck’s hind quarters whenever he was in error. Spitz was the leader, likewise experienced, and while he could not always get at Buck, he growled sharp reproof or cunningly threw his weight to jerk Buck into the way he should go. Buck learned easily. Ere they returned to camp he knew enough to stop at “ho,” to go ahead at “mush,” to swing wide on the bends, and to keep clear of the wheeler when the loaded sled shot downhill at their heels.

“T’ree vair’ good dogs,” François told Perrault. “Dat Buck, heem pool lak hell. I tich heem queek as anyt’ing.”

Perrault, in a hurry with his despatches, returned with two more dogs, brothers called “Billee” and “Joe.” Billee’s fault was excessive good nature; Joe was sour and introspective, with a perpetual snarl and malignant eye. Buck met them comradely; Dave ignored them; Spitz thrashed first one and then the other. Billee wagged appeasingly and cried when Spitz’s sharp teeth scored him; but Joe whirled to face Spitz, mane bristling, ears laid back, lips writhing and snarling, jaws clipping, eyes diabolically gleaming—the incarnation of belligerent fear. Spitz forewent disciplining him and, to cover his discomfiture, drove the inoffensive Billee to the camp’s edge.

By evening Perrault secured another, an old husky, long and lean and gaunt, battle-scarred, with a single flashing eye. He was called Sol-leks, the Angry One. Like Dave, he asked nothing, gave nothing, expected nothing, and even Spitz left him alone. He had one peculiarity—he did not like to be approached on his blind side. Buck, unwittingly guilty, learned it when Sol-leks whirled and slashed his shoulder to the bone. Forever after Buck avoided that side, and had no more trouble. His only apparent ambition, like Dave’s, was to be left alone; though, as Buck was afterward to learn, each possessed one other and even more vital ambition.

That night Buck faced the great problem of sleeping. As a matter of course, he entered the tent; Perrault and François bombarded him with curses and cooking utensils till he fled into the outer cold. A chill wind nipped him sharply and bit into his wounded shoulder. He tried the snow, but the frost drove him shivering to his feet, and savage dogs rushed him here and there. He bristled and snarled (for he was learning fast), and they let him go.

At last he returned to see how his mates fared—and they had disappeared. Circling the tent, the snow gave way beneath his fore legs. Something wriggled; he sprang back, then a friendly little yelp reassured him. A whiff of warm air rose, and there, curled in a snug ball under the snow, lay Billee, who even ventured, as a bribe for peace, to lick Buck’s face with his warm wet tongue. Another lesson. So that was the way. Buck selected a spot and, with much fuss and waste effort, dug his hole. In a trice his body’s heat filled the confined space and he slept, though he growled and wrestled with bad dreams.

He woke to the noises of the camp, completely buried by the night’s snowfall. The snow pressed him on every side, and a great surge of fear swept through him—the fear of the wild thing for the trap. The muscles of his body contracted; with a ferocious snarl he bounded straight up into the blinding day, the snow flying in a flashing cloud. Ere he landed, he saw the white camp and remembered all from Manuel to the hole he had dug.

A shout from François hailed him. “Wot I say?” he cried to Perrault. “Dat Buck for sure learn queek as anyt’ing.” Perrault nodded gravely; anxious to secure the best dogs, he was gladdened by the possession of Buck.

Three more huskies were added, making nine, and soon they were in harness and swinging up the trail toward the Dyea Cañon. Buck was glad to be gone, and though the work was hard he did not particularly despise it. Most surprising was the change in Dave and Sol-leks. All passiveness and unconcern dropped from them. They were alert, anxious that the work should go well, fiercely irritable with whatever delayed it. The toil of the traces seemed the supreme expression of their being.

Dave was wheeler; Buck pulled before him; then came Sol-leks; ahead, single file, to the leader—Spitz. Placed between Dave and Sol-leks, Buck received instruction from apt teachers who never let him linger in error and enforced their teaching with their sharp teeth. Once, tangled in the traces, he delayed the start; both flew at him and administered a sound trouncing. He kept the traces clear thereafter. François’s whip snapped less frequently, and Perrault even lifted Buck’s feet and carefully examined them.

It was a hard day’s run—up the Cañon, through Sheep Camp, past the Scales and the timber line, across glaciers and snowdrifts, and over the great Chilcoot Divide. They made good time down the chain of lakes and pulled late into the huge camp at the head of Lake Bennett, where thousands were building boats against the spring break-up. Buck slept the sleep of the exhausted just in his hole, but all too early was routed out into the cold darkness.

That day they made forty miles; afterward, for many days, they broke their own trail. Perrault, proud of his knowledge of ice, packed the snow ahead with webbed shoes; François at the gee-pole sometimes exchanged places with him. The fall ice was very thin, and where water ran swift there was no ice at all.

Day after day Buck toiled. They broke camp in the dark; the first gray found them on the trail; after dark they pitched, ate their bit of fish, and crawled into the snow. Buck was ravenous. His pound and a half of sun-dried salmon went nowhere. The others, born to it and weighing less, received a pound only and kept condition.

He swiftly lost the fastidiousness of his old life. A dainty eater, he found his mates, finishing first, robbed him. While he fought off two or three, the rest swallowed his ration. He ate fast; hunger compelled him, and he was not above taking what did not belong to him. He watched Pike, a clever malingerer and thief, slyly steal a slice of bacon when Perrault’s back was turned, and the next day duplicated the performance, getting away with the whole chunk. A great uproar was raised, but he was unsuspected; Dub, an awkward blunderer always getting caught, was punished for Buck’s misdeed.

This first theft marked Buck as fit to survive in the hostile Northland. It marked his adaptability, the lack of which would have meant swift and terrible death. It marked, further, the decay or going to pieces of his moral nature, a vain thing and a handicap in the ruthless struggle for existence. It was all well enough in the Southland, under the law of love and fellowship, to respect private property and personal feelings; but in the Northland, under the law of club and fang, whoso took such things into account was a fool, and in so far as he observed them he would fail to prosper. Not that Buck reasoned it out. He was fit, that was all. He did not steal for joy of it, but because of the clamor of his stomach. He did not rob openly, but stole secretly and cunningly, out of respect for club and fang. In short, the things he did were done because it was easier to do them than not to do them.

His development (or retrogression) was rapid. His muscles became hard as iron; he grew callous to ordinary pain; he achieved an internal economy. He could eat anything; his stomach extracted the last least particle; his blood carried it to the farthest reaches, building the toughest tissues. Sight and scent grew keen; in his sleep he heard the faintest sound and knew whether it heralded peace or peril. He learned to bite the ice from between his toes; when thirsty and a scum lay over the water hole, he broke it by rearing and striking with stiff fore legs. He could scent the wind and forecast it a night in advance; no matter how breathless the air when he dug his nest, the wind that later blew found him to leeward, sheltered and snug.

And instincts long dead became alive again. The domesticated generations fell from him. In vague ways he remembered back to the youth of the breed, when wild dogs ranged in packs and killed their meat as they ran it down. It was no task to learn to fight with cut and slash and the quick wolf snap. The old tricks stamped into the heredity of the breed were his tricks, coming without effort, as though they had been his always. And when, on the still cold nights, he pointed his nose at a star and howled long and wolflike, it was his ancestors, dead and dust, howling down through the centuries and through him—the cadences which voiced their woe and what to them was the meaning of the stiffness, and the cold, and dark.

Thus, as token of what a puppet thing life is, the ancient song surged through him and he came into his own again; and he came because men had found a yellow metal in the North, and because Manuel was a gardener’s helper whose wages did not lap over the needs of his wife and divers small copies of himself.

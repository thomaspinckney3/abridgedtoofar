Buck, a powerful St. Bernard–Scotch shepherd mix, rules like a country gentleman over Judge Miller’s sun-drenched Santa Clara Valley estate until the Klondike Gold Rush turns strong, furred dogs into valuable freight. Kidnapped by Manuel and sold down the line, he meets the man in the red sweater’s club and learns the first truth of the North: mastery is measured by violence. Shipped to Dyea and turned over to canny couriers Perrault and François, he tastes snow, harness, and hunger, and watches Curly die beneath a ring of huskies, absorbing the second truth: once down, there is no mercy. Under Spitz’s leadership and with iron instructors like Dave and Sol-leks, Buck adapts with frightening speed—stealing cleverly, toughening, and awakening to the “law of club and fang.” The treachery in Spitz’s grin becomes a goad, and the savage toil up the Thirty Mile, the Hootalinqua, and past the Five Fingers chisels Buck into something leaner, keener, and more ancient than the Santa Clara aristocrat. When a moonlit rabbit chase explodes the team into primal ecstasy, Buck and Spitz collide at last, and Buck’s imagination turns the tide: he breaks each foreleg, brings Spitz down within the silent circle, and emerges the dominant beast.

Placed at the head of the traces, Buck drives the team with a discipline fiercer and fairer than Spitz’s and carries Perrault to a record run into Skagway. Handed next to a Scotch half-breed with the mail, he labors through a season of monotonous miles that ends in Dave’s heartbreaking death in harness, pride and pain welded together. Buck dreams by campfires of a short-legged hairy man crouching against the dark and feels ancestral music rise in him, yet he holds the trail and his mates to order. Reduced by thousands of miles to sinew and will, the team staggers into Skagway, only to be dumped into the hands of greenhorns: Hal, Charles, and Mercedes. Their slovenly camps, overloaded sled, and misguided rations swing from glut to famine; Outside dogs die, and even the veterans become skeletons under the lash. On rotten spring ice at White River, Buck refuses to rise, sensing doom, until John Thornton knocks Hal aside, cuts the traces, and claims Buck’s life as his own; moments later, the trail drops out beneath the rest and swallows sled, fools, and folly.

Nursed by Skeet’s tender tongue and Nig’s amiable company, Buck heals under Thornton’s rough endearments and finds a new passion: love worthy of worship. That love turns lethal when “Black” Burton strikes Thornton and Buck tears the man down, and it turns heroic on Forty-Mile Creek when Buck, roped to the shore, hurls himself into rapids to drag his master to life. In Dawson’s Eldorado Saloon, Buck answers Thornton’s whisper—“As you love me”—by breaking out a thousand-pound sled and hauling it a hundred yards, winning Matthewson’s wager and staking the partners. With Hans and Pete, they push into the unmapped East on the legend of a Lost Cabin, living like Indians on straight meat, burning holes in frozen muck, and rafting nameless reaches as seasons wheel by. They do not find the cabin, but strike a valley where gold runs like butter in a pan; the men sack a fortune while Buck, idle and electric with vigor, hears a call threading deeper into the timber.

One night a timber wolf’s long howl draws him from camp, and after wary circling and playful truce, Buck runs beside his wild brother over divides and through green-shadowed miles until love tugging at his heart turns him back to Thornton’s fire. The pull of the pack thickens in him nonetheless; he hunts solo with fearsome cunning, kills a blinded black bear and scatters wolverenes from his spoil, and carries himself with a giant wolf’s pride, the shepherd’s intelligence, and the St. Bernard’s mass. In autumn he singles out a wounded bull moose and, with the patience of the wild, hounds him day and night until, spent and alone, the great animal drops and Buck feeds and sleeps by the kill. A new stir rides the air as he lopes homeward, messages whispered by birds and breeze that sharpen his pace and his caution. He hits a fresh trail that bristles his neck, finds Nig dead with an arrow through him and Hans feathered like a porcupine, and then sees the Yeehats crying over the wreck of the spruce-bough lodge. A hurricane of grief and fury possesses him, and Buck explodes among them, ripping the chief’s throat, scattering the band, and turning their arrows on one another before the survivors flee yammering of an evil spirit.

By the blood-muddied pool he learns all he needs: Skeet lies faithful at the brink, Pete is slain in his blankets, and Thornton’s trace ends in the water that holds him forever. Grief aches like hunger but is sated, for a time, by pride; he has killed the noblest game and cast off the last pretense that the old law binds him. When the wolves pour silver into the clearing at moonrise, Buck stands statue-still, slashes down the boldest, and fights the pack to a standoff against a bank, until his wild brother edges near and an old scarred wolf howls to the younger world. Buck’s own voice joins, unmistakable, and he steps from the angle to be sniffed, jostled, and accepted, then runs with them into the timber, side by side with kin he has always dimly remembered. In the years that follow, the Yeehats whisper of a Ghost Dog larger than any wolf, marked with brown on muzzle and white down the chest, who raids traps in fierce winters, slays dogs, and sometimes hunters, and leads the timber wolves through boreal light. Each summer, a great lone wolf comes to a quiet valley where gold trickles from rotted moose-hide sacks; he stands, remembers, and howls once, long and mournfully, before sliding back into the trees. And when winter falls and the pack’s song lifts under the moon, he runs at their head, leaping gigantic above his fellows, his deep throat giving voice to the old, triumphant music—the call to which he has finally and forever answered.

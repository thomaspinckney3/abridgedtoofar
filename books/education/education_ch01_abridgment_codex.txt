CHAPTER I

QUINCY (1838-1848)

Under the shadow of Boston State House, turning its back on the house of John Hancock, in the little passage called Hancock Avenue, February 16, 1838, a child was born and later christened Henry Brooks Adams. Had he been born in Jerusalem under the shadow of the Temple and circumcised in the Synagogue by his uncle the high priest, under the name of Israel Cohen, he would scarcely have been more distinctly branded. The branding was a handicap only if the century chose to make it one; for old names and old safeguards were irksome, but convenient. A hundred years earlier they would have secured success outright. Even in 1838 they gave a child unusual cards; and probably no child born in that year held better. The problem was not how to escape such luck, but what could become of a child of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries when he should wake and find himself required to play the game of the twentieth. Had he been consulted, he would have changed nothing. Only on the understanding that he accepted from birth a full partnership with the society of his age had his education an interest.

As it happened, he never quite got to playing the game; he lost himself in studying it. During his first years the old universe was thrown into the ash-heap and a new one made ready for him. The Boston and Albany Railroad, the Cunard steamers, and the telegraph made a breach between the troglodytic Boston into which he had been born and the world that was to educate him. Of this revolution he knew first only the color of yellow, for his earliest recollection was of sitting on a yellow kitchen floor in strong sunlight. The second lesson was one of taste: after scarlet fever had almost killed him, he remembered only an aunt entering with a baked apple. The third was one of discomfort: carried in blankets through the winter air from Hancock Avenue to Mount Vernon Street, he never forgot the want of breath and the noise of moving furniture.

In later life that fever took larger importance in his eyes. Physically it seemed to have left him smaller and less robust than his brothers. Mentally he fancied in it the beginning of certain habits: doubt; distrust of his own judgment and of the world's; a tendency to regard every question as open; hesitation to act except as a choice of evils; the shirking of responsibility; the love of line, form, and quality; the horror of ennui; the passion for companionship and the antipathy to society. His brothers were the type; he was the variation. Yet, as far as the boy knew, he remained healthy enough and normal enough. Whatever was peculiar in him came less from private character than from education, and from the eighteenth-century inheritance he bore in his name.

The atmosphere of that education was colonial, revolutionary, almost Cromwellian, as though the family had long lived in the odor of political crime. Resistance to something was the law of New England nature. Politics, as a practice, whatever its professions, had always been the systematic organization of hatreds; and Massachusetts politics had the harshness of its climate. Even nature taught by contrast. Winter and summer, town and country, force and freedom, became to the boy not conveniences but opposing principles. Town meant winter confinement, school, rule, discipline, straight streets banked with snow, the society of uncles and aunts and cousins who expected children to behave. Country, only seven miles away, meant liberty, diversity, outlawry, and the endless delight of mere sense impressions. Summer was the multiplicity of nature; winter was school.

So the education of Henry Adams began as a training in doubleness. The smells of hot pine-woods, sweet-fern, hay, ploughed earth, salt water, low tide, stables, barns, and marshes; the tastes of herbs, nuts, and even the spelling-book itself; the glare of a New England sun and the hard reds, greens, and blues of sea and flower and print, all belonged to Quincy, to summer, to freedom. Boston belonged to restraint, law, unity. The division ran too deep to be called fancy. The man who later pretended that life could reconcile these opposites seemed in the boy's eyes a schoolmaster, and a schoolmaster was a man employed to tell lies to little boys.

He liked the Adams side of his kin best because it stood for Quincy, country, and the absence of restraint; yet even a child knew that Boston looked down on Quincy and did so with reason. Boston had style, or what passed for style: bathrooms, a furnace, gas, the superior conveniences of the nineteenth century. Quincy had the older house, older manners, and the remnants of the eighteenth century: Queen Anne panels, Louis Seize furniture, diplomatic relics from Paris, simplicity so severe that the flint-and-steel with which his grandfather had once lighted his own fires remained a proper household instrument. For a boy, the old forms were not especially comfortable, but they were native. He never quite overcame the prejudice then formed. He could reconcile himself to much that was worse, but he could never care for nineteenth-century style, because he had somehow been born an eighteenth-century child.

Education, however, did not cease because Quincy was freer than Boston. There too the boy's will had to be broken into harness. His first grave contact with the President, John Quincy Adams, came in a rebellion against school. His mother, unable to compel him, was losing ground at the foot of the staircase when the old man opened the library door, came down, put on his hat, took the boy's hand without a word, and walked him up the road to the schoolhouse. The child believed he could dodge away before reaching the door; the President turned every strategical point; and the boy found himself seated inside, under public inspection, before his hand was released. The outrage ought to have made him hate his grandfather forever. It did not, because the President had done his disreputable work with intelligence. He showed no temper, no irritation, no cant about duty and obedience; above all, he held his tongue. The boy admitted force as a form of right more readily than sermonizing, and from intelligent silence he learned respect.

During those summers the relations remained friendly and almost intimate. He haunted the President's library, handled the books and papers, searched old purses for foreign coins, drew the sword-cane, snapped the travelling-pistols, invaded the dressing-closet where caterpillars lived vainly under tumblers, and watched the old man's hobbies in trees and seeds and peachstones. He carried up to the study the finest peaches in the garden and ate the poorer ones himself, which showed that he bore no great grudge. Somewhere there survived a little volume of Nursery Rhymes with the President's trembling inscription, while the family Bibles came in due course from the same hand and silver mugs from the other grandfather, Brooks.

The strongest domestic influence may have been less in lessons than in mere presence. In church the boy sat behind the back of the President's bald head and beneath the memorial of another President great-grandfather. It seemed natural that churches should continue, with bald-headed leading citizens in the chief pews and Presidents on the walls. When the Irish gardener once said, "You'll be thinkin' you'll be President too!" the remark startled him not because it flattered, but because it suggested for the first time that there could be a doubt. What had been would continue to be: that was the natural law of childhood.

The Madam, Louisa Catherine Adams, affected him differently. She was more remote, more decorative, more plainly not Bostonian. She seemed to belong to Washington or Europe, like her furniture and little old books. In truth her life had been formed elsewhere: born in London, driven in childhood to Nantes by the Revolution, returned to London, married there in 1797 to John Quincy Adams against Abigail Adams's severe New England doubts, carried afterwards to Berlin, America, St. Petersburg, Paris in the Hundred Days, the Court in England, Washington, the White House, and finally back to Quincy. To the boy she appeared as a vision of silver gray, presiding over old silver and Queen Anne mahogany, an exotic made gentle by fatigue. He could not know her inward history, but he could feel that she was Louis Seize, like the furniture, and that from her entered into the family some non-Boston element, some quarter taint of Maryland and Europe, from which doubts, hesitations, rebellions against discipline, and other forms of variation might one day descend.

A boy beginning life among such surroundings ought, in self-respect, to have felt at home in the eighteenth century and suspicious of the nineteenth. For ten years he did. Then the first chapter closed. One winter morning there was confusion in Mount Vernon Street, and he learned that the President had fallen and been paralyzed. The figure of his grandfather in the invalid chair fixed itself in memory; then, on February 21, 1848, the eighteenth century, as an actual companion, vanished. The funeral in Quincy church, with Dr. Lunt's voice and manner and the whole atmosphere of national respect and family pride, overwhelmed the ten-year-old mind. The later ceremony in Faneuil Hall, where Edward Everett pronounced the eulogy, impressed him too, but in another way. By then he already felt that the dead President could not quite belong to that audience. No rhetoric could reconcile State Street with the inherited Adams quarrel against State Street. The shadow of the War of 1812 still hung there; another shadow already darkened it.

Thus, at ten years old, the boy found himself face to face with a dilemma. What was he, and where was he going? Quincy had always seemed right because Quincy represented a moral principle: resistance to Boston, resistance in the name of truth, duty, and freedom. He was a ten-year-old priest and politician. He could not foresee what railways, telegraphs, coal, steel, and the rest would make of the next fifty years, and sixty years afterwards he still could not determine whether foreknowledge would have saved him. Had he seen in advance the New York stock-list of 1900, would he have renounced his ancestral prejudices, made pilgrimage to State Street, and asked for the fatted calf and a clerkship in the Suffolk Bank? Each course had its advantages, but the material advantages, looking back, seemed to lie wholly in State Street.
